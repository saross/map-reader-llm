## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Lakens supplies the normative half of the deviations literature: not how often researchers depart from a preregistration — that is the meta-scientific work he reviews — but how to decide whether a departure is defensible, and how to report it. The frame is error-statistical (Mayo, 2018) and methodologically falsificationist (Lakatos, 1978). A preregistration exists so readers can evaluate the severity of a test, high when a test is likely to support a correct hypothesis and to fail to support a wrong one; deviations are judged by their effect on severity, traded against validity, "the approximate truth of an inference" (Shadish et al., 2001, p. 34). Both are continuous, and cross into a four-cell table (page_index 3) in which following the registration rigidly can itself be the higher-severity, lower-validity cell. Five categories organise the paper — unforeseen events, mistakes in the preregistration or study, missing information, violated untested assumptions, falsified auxiliary hypotheses — each with a hypothetical worked example and the same two-part recipe: state the reason, then evaluate the consequence for severity. There is no new data; the categories come from a review of meta-scientific studies of deviation prevalence plus the author's own peer-review and preregistration experience, and he grants they are perhaps not exhaustive.

Three intersections with our errata log and recorded deviations. First, the headline stance backs D.9's counterweight that amendment count is a biased measure of registration quality: a principled approach does not treat all deviations as problematic, and some raise severity. Second, "missing information" names our failure mode better than "deviation" does — an error of omission, in which a vague registration forces analysis decisions to be taken with the data already in hand; his remedy, "the probability that information is missing can be reduced by preregistering the analysis code", is the analysis-grain move D.9 argues for, reached from the opposite direction. Third, he already occupies part of the automation cell: machine-readable hypothesis tests (Lakens & DeBruine, 2021) are offered as one of several remedies for under-specified registrations, alongside analysis code preregistered on simulated data, checklists, and conditional analyses, so the novelty claim must be narrower than machine-assisted registration as such.

Two scope caveats travel with all of it. His unit is a claim resting on a statistical test that differs from the registered one, with severity argued in Type 1 and Type 2 error terms; pipeline-configuration changes carrying no error rate sit outside the frame as written, and he exempts hypothesis-generating tests from adherence. And he separates deviations from sensitivity analyses — "(often unsystematic) variations of statistical tests" reported to show how far a claim depends on analytic decisions — which suggests most of our multi-variant reporting (greedy consensus alongside WBF, 20 m alongside 50 m, F1 alongside MCC) is sensitivity analysis rather than deviation to be logged. That same reporting is his severity defence: where only a few defensible options exist and all of them lead to the same claim, the deviation can be argued to have cost no severity.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: preregistration and open-science methodology — the normative companion to the adaptive-preregistration thread (Gould et al. 2026; Srivastava 2018), read for D.9's deviations-and-retrospective subsection. Lakens gives the evaluative grammar our errata log currently lacks: five categories of reason to deviate, and a two-part reporting recipe (specify when, where and why; then evaluate the consequence for the severity of the test), resting on the claim that a principled approach does not treat all deviations as problematic. He also marks two boundaries the paper must respect — his unit of analysis is a statistical hypothesis test whose severity can be argued in error-rate terms rather than a pipeline configuration, and machine-readable hypothesis tests are already proposed in his own prior work, so D.9's novelty has to sit in LLM-supported authoring and checking of a natural-language registration rather than in machine assistance as such.

### KP1
- **Quote (verbatim):** "A principled approach to the use of preregistration should not treat all deviations as problematic. Deviations from a preregistered analysis plan can both reduce and increase the severity of a test, as well as increase the validity of inferences."

- **Paraphrase:** Deviating is not in itself a failure: a departure from the registered analysis plan can lower the severity of a test, leave it untouched, or raise it, and it can improve the validity of the inference drawn. ### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "For every deviation clearly specify when, where, and why a deviation from a preregistration occurred, followed by an evaluation of the impact of the deviation on the severity of the test (and where relevant, the validity of the inference)."

- **Paraphrase:** The reporting unit is a per-deviation record with four slots: when it happened, where in the registration and the code it bit, why it was made, and what it did to the severity of the test — with the effect on validity added only where it applies. The same two-part shape (reason, then consequence) is restated at the end of each of the paper's five category sections.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 deviations table and errata log; preregistration appendix · Deviation reporting: the record format for an errata log and recorded deviations · **supports**

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "When a preregistration is missing information researchers need to make a decision about the statistical analyses they will perform at a time when they have access to the data"
- **Locator:** page_index 6 · p.7 · Missing information
- **Paraphrase:** Where the registration is silent or too vague to bind — which he calls an error of omission — the analysis choice necessarily gets made with the data already visible. He notes such choices may formally not be deviations at all, yet still have to be reported as departures from the registered plan, and how much they cost depends on how much latitude the missing information leaves.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 retrospective — the grain-not-volume diagnosis · Grain-not-volume: under-specification, not amendment count, is the registration cost that matters · **complicates**

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "all analyses lead to the same claim, a deviation from a preregistered analysis plan can be argued to have no effect on the severity of the test"
- **Locator:** page_index 7 · p.8 · Violating untested assumption
- **Paraphrase:** Robustness across the defensible alternatives is a severity defence: where only a few defensible options exist for handling a violated assumption and every one of them yields the same claim, the departure can be argued to have cost no severity. The sentence is scoped to the violated-assumption category, and the parallel argument in the missing-information section adds the condition that the most conservative test must be among the analyses that agree.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 deviations; also the multi-variant reporting convention (greedy vs WBF, 20 m vs 50 m, F1 vs MCC) · Robustness reporting across analysis variants as a severity defence · **supports**

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "preregister conditional analyses (if X, then Y, else Z)"
- **Locator:** page_index 8 · p.9 · Falsification of auxiliary hypothesis
- **Paraphrase:** A high-quality registration can commit in advance to branching rules of the form if X, then Y, else Z; the source's continuation is that these let researchers run data-dependent analyses without impacting the severity of the test. It is offered as a feature of good registration practice, illustrated by falling back to a robust test when a normality check rejects, not as an empirical finding.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 registered decision rules; §D.4 decision tree · Registered decision rules: the bridge to the adaptive and phased-design thread · **supports**

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Here I focus on scientific claims that are based on a statistical test that is not identical to the test in the preregistered statistical analysis plan. Researchers do not need to adhere to a preregistration when statistical tests are used to generate hypotheses."
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3 · Introduction (scope statement)
- **Paraphrase:** He scopes the whole framework to claims resting on a statistical test that differs from the registered one, and exempts tests used to generate hypotheses from any adherence requirement. His illustration on the same page treats a post-hoc decision to exclude a subset of observations as a paradigm deviation, and the introduction lists pre-processing among what a preregistration should specify, so data-handling changes sit inside the frame. What sits outside is a change whose consequence cannot be argued in Type 1 and Type 2 error terms — a corpus swap, a model version, a pipeline configuration — so applying the severity calculus to those is an extension of his frame rather than a use of it.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 deviations — what the severity calculus can and cannot price · Scope limit: an error-statistical frame applied to a machine-learning evaluation protocol · **complicates**
