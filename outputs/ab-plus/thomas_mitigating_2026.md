## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Thomas, Gligorić, and Shah attack a problem our own preregistration is exposed to: an LLM that already exists can be searched. Classical preregistration works because the data do not yet exist; but “Once released, an LLM is always accessible”, so a researcher can tune prompts, decoding parameters, or batching until a downstream test reaches significance; traditional preregistration therefore “cannot safeguard LLM-based statistical analyses against p-hacking.” Their remedy restores the asymmetry temporally: finalise the procedure on current models, preregister it with an eligible-model set and a selection rule, then confirm on the first such model released afterwards — one that cannot be hacked against because it does not yet exist.

Evidence: two tasks null by construction — labelling the first 100 ICLR 2020 reviews as AI or human (true prevalence 0%), and judging which of 100 paired MyFitnessPal food logs has fewer calories (true rate 50%). A “hack” is a threshold crossing on the target-label rate: at least 3% of valid outputs labelled “AI”, at least 53% of valid position-corrected outputs labelled “Day 2”. The mitigation rate is the fraction of hacked model-configuration cells whose configuration fails on the next released model. Across 20 models from four providers and 11 configurations: 17 of 23 hacked cells mitigated on reviews (73.9%, Wilson 95% CI [53.5%, 87.5%]) and 32 of 44 on diet (72.7%, [58.2%, 83.7%]); their own prospective run, confirmatory model Grok 4.20, gave 6 of 7 (85.7%). On the diet task alone — the only one whose calorie ground truth allows resampling to a known effect size — detecting injected effects of 55–95% costs no detectable power versus re-measuring the same model (overlapping bootstrap CIs). The stated cost is the wait: for a registration date drawn uniformly at random across their 7 August 2025–27 May 2026 release window, mean 14.5 and median 10.3 days to the next model.

Three things bear on us. Their Table 1 template is a checkable standard our registration can be audited against — conditions that include prompts and decoding parameters, output-validity and parsing rules, an eligible-model set with a selection rule, and a model-level abandonment rule the template requires the researcher to state rather than fixes (theirs, §3.8: discard above a 50% invalid-output rate). Their evidence, though, is text binary classification scored by thresholds; detection at F1 and MCC over match radii lies outside it, as they concede. And — the point that touches our novelty claim — nothing here implements or evaluates an LLM that authors or checks a preregistration (verified by search across the extracted text), though §6 proposes that autonomous AI-scientist systems “commit to the procedure and eligible-model set before the confirmatory model is available”: a proposal, not a system. Their nearest citation is Goldberg et al. (2024) on an LLM author-checklist assistant, folded into a qualified objection — checklists “help to make these disclosures more principled”, but have “limited effectiveness, since they rely solely on self-report” — the nearest prior art to our LLM-support element and the objection it must answer.

## Key points (salience-ranked to the citing paper)

### KP1
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Once released, an LLM is always accessible: a researcher can run, inspect, and revise the analysis immediately and repeatedly. Preregistration in the traditional way, therefore, cannot safeguard LLM-based statistical analyses against p-hacking."
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2 · §1 Introduction
- **Paraphrase:** Because a released model can be queried again at will, the commitment device that makes classical preregistration work — the data not existing yet — is absent for LLM-based analyses; on their argument, preregistering in the ordinary way does not protect such an analysis from p-hacking. The claim is scoped to LLM outputs feeding downstream hypothesis tests, and to an opportunistic researcher tuning configurations toward a desired conclusion; they do not claim ordinary preregistration is worthless, and they retain it as step 2 of their own protocol.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 preregistration in practice — deviations and the retrospective · D.9: the commitment value of a registration written against already-released models · **complicates**

### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "an LLM preregistration must specify the prompts and decoding parameters as conditions, the output-validity and parsing rules, the dependent variable as a formula over valid outputs, and most importantly the eligible-model set and the rule for selecting the confirmatory model"
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3 · §2 Proposed protocol: Preregistering for the next LLM
- **Paraphrase:** Beyond what a conventional study registers, they hold that an LLM preregistration must fix the prompts and decoding parameters as experimental conditions, the rules that decide whether an output is valid and how it is parsed, the dependent variable as a formula computed over valid outputs, and — the item they weight most — the set of eligible models plus the rule that picks the confirmatory one. Their Table 1 adds a model-level abandonment rule (an invalid-output rate above which the model is dropped) and a sample-size field naming dataset and version.
- **Relevance:** §D.9; methods — preregistration apparatus · D.9: a checkable standard for what an LLM-based registration must fix in advance · **extends**

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Across 20 models from four providers and 11 LLM-analysis configurations, the protocol would have blocked successful transfer of the p-hack in 73.9% and 72.7% of cases in the two tasks."
- **Locator:** page_index 0 · p.1 · Abstract
- **Paraphrase:** Over 20 models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and xAI and 11 prompt/decoding configurations, a configuration that produced a false positive on one model failed to do so on the next released model with a valid output under that configuration in 73.9% of evaluable cases on the review task (17 of 23 hacked cells) and 72.7% on the diet task (32 of 44). Both are rates over model-configuration cells, not over studies, and the denominator counts only cells where a hack occurred and a next model exists.
- **Relevance:** §D.9; discussion of transfer taxes and carried vs oracle operating points · Calibration transfer: configuration choices as model-local artefacts · **extends**

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "the scope of our empirical evaluation is focused on four model families and two tasks, both of which are binary classification problems, and each draws on 100 labeled items from two datasets, analyzed using thresholds"
- **Locator:** page_index 13 · p.14 · §6 Discussion — Limitations
- **Paraphrase:** The authors scope their own evidence: four model families, two text binary-classification tasks, 100 labelled items each, outcomes scored by fixed thresholds. They add that whether the protocol's effectiveness extends to multi-class labelling, free-form generation, regression-style outputs, other statistical analyses, other model families, or other corpora is future work — which leaves object detection scored by F1 and MCC at match radii outside what they demonstrated.
- **Relevance:** §D.9; §D.8 limitations · Scope limit: transferability of the protocol to detection-style VLM evaluation · **complicates**

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "every repair strategy is itself a researcher degree of freedom, and a researcher can tune these choices toward statistical significance of the final test"
- **Locator:** page_index 12 · p.13 · §5 Related work — Heuristic validation
- **Paraphrase:** Showing that an LLM instrument matches ground truth does not immunise a study, because the moves that produce the match — prompt engineering, persona design, response formatting, fine-tuning — are themselves analytic choices that can be steered toward a significant final test. Their target is tuning toward significance of a downstream hypothesis test in settings where human ground truth is unavailable; a study that tunes toward accuracy against adjudicated reference data and then reports the loss on transfer is differently placed, but the general point that configuration search is a researcher degree of freedom still applies.
- **Relevance:** §D.9; methods — configuration selection and carried operating points · Whether ground-truth validation licenses configuration search · **complicates**

### KP6

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "We envision that venues could encourage this form of preregistration for any work leveraging LLM-generated data, annotations, or using LLM-as-a-judge."
