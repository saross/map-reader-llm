## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Landauer, Maddison, Fontana and Posluschny train a ConvNeXt classifier on English hillforts and apply it across roughly 180,000 km² of England, Hesse and Molise — on their own account the largest study area yet subjected to automatic detection in archaeology (p.12). For our purposes it calibrates what numbers from the CNN prospection lineage actually mean.

Those numbers are low by our standards, for reasons the paper is candid about. The three English test areas (about 2,000 km² each) return precision 0.26, recall 0.71 and F1 38% overall, with the full-England search at 36% (pp.2–3). Three protocol features must travel with any comparison. Detection is cluster-level with a 500 m match radius, an order of magnitude or more looser than our 20 m and 50 m object-level tolerances. True negatives are declared not meaningful (the rest of the landscape is effectively all negative), and accuracy is set aside separately as misleadingly high on so imbalanced a problem, leaving precision, recall and F1. Matthews correlation coefficient needs true negatives and so is not computable here — our inference, not theirs: the paper never mentions it. And the target is deliberately hard: hillfort heterogeneity "likely explains" the low F1 and heavy false-positive load "compared to more uniform archaeological datasets, such as mounds or barrows" (p.3) — their words, placing our own target class at the easier end, albeit as three-dimensional earthworks in LiDAR rather than symbols on a scanned map.

The transfer experiment is the closest published analogue to our carried-versus-oracle operating points. Applied unchanged, the England-trained model scores F1 21% in Hesse and 12% in Molise; fine-tuning for 12 epochs on 3,000 randomly selected local patches plus 22 (Hesse) or 32 (Molise) known forts lifts these to 38% and 34%, with the known hillforts excluded from the final calculations (pp.3–4). A penalty of that size, bought back from an existing local inventory rather than by new annotation, is exactly the shape of result our 55-map deployment section argues about.

Human verification is measured, not assumed: 1,500 ranked vignettes triaged at about four seconds each, 95 escalated at roughly three minutes each, some eight and a half hours end to end including setup (p.4). The reference data is openly incomplete: the authors insist false positives be adjudicated, since some may be previously unknown true positives missing from the Atlas (p.3). In Hesse, 169 candidates above 90% confidence yielded 148 obvious false positives, 12 known hillfort sites mistakenly omitted from the training data, and four "possible hillforts" needing further investigation, with the remaining five split between two roads, two waste-disposal sites and a geological formation (pp.7, 9); Molise's top 100 above 0.94 gave 15 confirmed hillforts and 85 false positives, mostly terrain features (p.11). That is curator-versus-adjudicated ground truth, stated plainly.

Finally, cost. England's 266 GB and almost one million patches were processed in under five hours on a mid-range PC with a 16-core CPU and an RTX 2060 (p.3) — the baseline against which a per-call vision-language pipeline must justify itself.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Archaeological prospection prior art at what its authors believe ("to our knowledge", p.12) is the largest scale yet attempted in the field — 180,000 km² across three European countries — and therefore the source that sets realistic performance expectations for our related-work section: F1 in the mid-thirties, precision 0.26 in the three English test regions, and a workflow designed from the outset as AI triage feeding human adjudication rather than end-to-end detection. It is prior art in a different substrate (LiDAR terrain models, not scanned maps) and at a different granularity (cluster hits within 500 m; no true negatives, and hence no MCC — our inference, since the paper never raises MCC), so its figures anchor expectations rather than serve as a comparator for ours; the honest use is to say that this study reports F1 34–38% on heterogeneous hillforts, and that these same authors expect mounds and barrows to be easier. Its region-transfer result — 21% and 12% unfine-tuned, 38% and 34% after a 22–32-fort local sample — is also the nearest published relative of our carried-versus-oracle transfer taxes, which makes methods/metrics and calibration transfer its strongest secondary clusters.

- **Empirical 90% operating point** — Landauer et al. set their detection threshold by inspection rather than derivation: a vignette scoring above 90% confidence counts as a positive detection, a value the paper describes only as “determined empirically”. (page_index 2 · p.3 · §2.3 AI quality evaluation across the landscape)
- **Candidates below the threshold** — In the Northern England triage the ratio of candidates to samples fell as the classifier's confidence scores declined, but a candidate was still identified at probabilities as low as 66–70% — recall left on the table below the operating point. (page_index 4 · p.5 · §3.1.2 Results — Northern England)
- **Annotation-budget hypothesis** — The study's stated hypothesis is an annotation-budget claim: that a curated list of a few hundred known objects of a given type is sufficient to identify a large number of previously unknown objects of the same type within a region. (page_index 1 · p.2 · §1 Introduction and previous work)
- **579 training vignettes** — The positive training set is small and hand-pruned: manual inspection discarded vignettes whose sites had been destroyed, ploughed out or lost to urban development, leaving 579 hillfort vignettes to train on. (page_index 1 · p.2 · §2.1 Dataset creation)
- **Pseudo-labelling prune** — A pseudo-labelling pass pruned the training set by confidence — hillforts scoring below 85% and non-hillforts scoring above 5% were dropped as outliers — and a second network trained for a further 12 epochs on the reduced set of 505 hillforts and 15,156 non-hillforts. The reported payoff is a 4% increase in accuracy, which is the metric the same paper sets aside elsewhere as misleadingly high for a class this rare. (page_index 2 · p.3 · §2.2 Neural network training with semi-supervised quality improvement)
- **Random negatives and rarity** — Negatives were around 20,000 vignettes of random English land rather than curated hard negatives, a design the authors justify by rarity: less than 0.05% of British land is covered by hillforts, so the risk of accidentally sampling one as a negative is small. (page_index 1 · p.2 · §2.1 Dataset creation)
- **Hard negatives as remedy** — Terrain features were the commonest false positive in Molise — spurs and cliff-edged summits whose steepness demarcates a hilltop much as a rampart would — and the authors read this as confirming the pattern they had already found in Hesse. Their proposed remedy is hard-negative curation: train the classifier on non-site examples that resemble the distinctive but common natural features it mistakes for sites. (page_index 12 · p.13 · §3.3.2 Results — Molise)
- **Molise false-positive taxonomy** — Of the top 100 Molise detections scoring above 0.94, 15 were true positives and 85 false positives, which Table 5 breaks down by type as 65 terrain features, 10 villages and 10 field systems. The false-positive load is concentrated in a single class rather than spread across many. (page_index 11 · p.12 · §3.3.2 Results — Molise, Table 5)
- **Half-vignette grid step** — The 50% overlap in the landscape sweep — a 384 m step across a vignette twice that width — is an empirical choice, not a derived one: the authors say only that they found overlapping vignettes gave better results. (page_index 2 · p.3 · §2.3 AI quality evaluation across the landscape)
- **Maquis masks LiDAR returns** — Part of the Molise miss rate is a sensor limit rather than a model one: dense Mediterranean maquis makes standard LiDAR flights markedly less effective at producing accurate models than acquisitions over temperate Europe and the British Isles, so hillfort sites become masked by noise in the data. (page_index 12 · p.13 · §3.3.2 Results — Molise)
- **Unequal LiDAR coverage** — The three regions are not equally observed. Table 6 records all three DEMs at 1 m resolution but gives LiDAR coverage as 99% for England and 100% for Hesse against 66% for Molise, whose data were acquired between 2008 and 2015 — a third of Molise has no elevation data at all, a coverage gap that travels with any Molise figure. (page_index 14 · p.15 · Data accessibility statement — Table 6)
- **Code and data on request** — Openness is partial: the English training site locations are public at hillforts.arch.ox.ac.uk, whereas the source code and the Hesse and Molise training data only “may be available for research purposes upon request to the authors”. (page_index 13 · p.14 · Data accessibility statement)
