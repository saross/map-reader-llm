## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Wu and Xie (CVPR 2024; UC San Diego and NYU) argue that multimodal large language models (MLLMs) lack the visual-search mechanism humans use to find a small target in a cluttered scene, and that this absence — not the language model's reasoning — breaks detailed grounding on high-resolution images. The diagnosis is architectural: a frozen CLIP encoder trained at 224x224 or 336x336 means a two-thousand-pixel-wide image passes through a low-resolution bottleneck before the language model sees it. Their remedy, SEAL, pairs a VQA LLM that names the details it is missing with V*, a guided search that recursively splits the image into cue-ranked patches.

On the authors' V*Bench — 191 SA-1B images averaging 2246x1582, 115 attribute and 76 spatial questions — SEAL on a Vicuna-7B backbone scores 75.39 against GPT-4V's 54.97 and a human 98.95. GPT-4V and Bard were run through the web chatbot (accessed 31 October 2023) and Gemini Pro through the API (16 December 2023), while the open-source models were scored by highest option log-likelihood — so the cross-family ranking is a 2023 snapshot under two answer protocols.

Three things make this load-bearing for us. First, it is an unusually clear published statement of why we tile at all: the encoder's fixed input resolution, not the model's reasoning, is what loses a small mound glyph on a scanned sheet. Second, the paper names our regime — exhaustive uniform patchification, "often used in aerial image detection" — as the inefficient baseline guided search replaces, so a reviewer will ask why we do not search instead. Our answer rests on the absence of the comparison: the paper never pits exhaustive tiling against guided search empirically, the inefficiency verdict is asserted, and the nearest evidence (4.65 search steps against 6.62 for a sequential scan, Table 3, over solved samples only) measures steps, not accuracy. Narrower still, the ablation shows open-world detectors in SEAL's search slot (GroundingDINO 62.30, OWL-ViT 62.82) trailing V* (75.39) by 13.1 and 12.6 points, though both beat the no-search baseline (45.02). Third, V* is "primarily tailored to natural images and common objects": §5.4 records diagram questions ending in "failing to locate objects accurately because it was trained on common objects". Reading a topographic sheet as a diagram image is our inference — the source never mentions maps — but V*'s structured-scene prior has no cartographic analogue.

Cost framing converges with ours: 6.0 seconds per target on one A100, defended because harder tasks warrant more computation — analogous to consensus over passes plus a verifier, though on a 7B-scale local stack and fired only when global features are judged insufficient.

Metric hygiene: every headline is multiple-choice accuracy against a 35.99 random-guess floor (50.00 spatial, 26.73 attribute), not F1 or MCC at an operating point; search-length comparisons include only solved samples; and V*Bench is 191 items built by the method's authors, with questions crafted so the answer cannot be guessed without accurate grounding — designed to expose the bottleneck, not to estimate general VLM competence.
