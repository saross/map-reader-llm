## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Gamble and colleagues supply the clinical-trials half of the D.9 bridge: the first consensus statement on what a statistical analysis plan (SAP) should contain. The separate SAP long predates it - ICH E9 (1998) and SPIRIT 2013 both refer to one - but its content was unstandardised, which tempers any claim that the tradition is long-settled: 28 of 39 funders and regulators responded (72%), 21 of them reporting an absence of relevant guidance, and a literature search “did not identify any publications relevant to the content of SAPs” (page_index 2). The resulting 55 items are a consensus artefact, not an evaluation: a survey of all 45 UKCRC-registered trials units (46 respondents, 100% response); a two-round Delphi among 73 invited participants, 54 completing both rounds and reaching consensus that 46 of 110 items should remain in; an expert panel of 12 that took this to 63 in, 30 out and 17 referenceable elsewhere; critical review by 51 statisticians from 37 units; and piloting on 5 trials that required no changes.

What transfers to D.9 is the registration-hygiene apparatus. The SAP is defined as a document whose level of detail exceeds the protocol's: analysis grain beneath project grain. Items 4a-4c make the revision apparatus itself required content (revision history; justification for each revision; timing relative to interim analyses), item 2 requires a version number with dates, and item 31 requires the statistical packages to be named. The Discussion adds that a SAP should state consistency with the protocol's statistical methods, or carry a section detailing which analyses differ and why. That is an errata log and a deviations register, standardised in a high-stakes field, converting the paper's recipe from innovation to import.

Three limits bound the import. Timing is out of scope - “This guidance document does not cover when a SAP should be written” - and the early-authoring and blind-review-deadline rule that follows is Discussion recommendation, with the blind-review definition and the post-blind deviation-flagging rule both cited to ICH E9 rather than issued by the Delphi. Transfer is hedged inside the trials family (“other types of interventions, phases, and designs”), with observational studies, adaptive designs and Bayesian analyses listed as extensions still to be considered. And data-dependent branching is where their consensus frayed: the 2-stage analysis - test the assumptions, then let the result choose the method - was the “more controversial” of two issues raised at critical review, settled by flagging it in that item's elaboration rather than by ruling.

The open cell is the citing paper's. SAPs are increasingly public - the US National Institutes of Health Final Rule requires posting, JAMA one at submission - yet “there is no guidance on how the SAP should be used or evaluated”, and the nearest remedy offered is that a SPIRIT-style template “may be beneficial for SAPs”. Nothing in the extracted text mentions automation, machine learning, or language models (verified by search), and the word “preregistration” never appears in it - the vocabulary gap on which the C1-C3 citation-network bridge rests.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: preregistration and open-science methodology - the mature, high-stakes analogue for analysis-level registration, and the source that decides how much weight D.9's bridge to the clinical-trials tradition can bear. Gamble et al. supply a consensus content standard (55 items, six sections) in which a revision history and a justification for each revision are required items rather than optional apparatus - the authors' own gloss is “the minimum set of essential items required for a SAP” (page_index 5) - while an itemised statement of differences from the protocol is Discussion guidance rather than a numbered item, for a document explicitly finer-grained than the protocol governing it. What they do not supply is timing (out of scope by their own statement), coverage of adaptive designs (an extension still to be considered), or any account of how a registered plan should be used or evaluated - and the word “preregistration” never appears in the paper, which is the citation-network hole the bridge claim exploits.
