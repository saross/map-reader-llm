## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Canedo and colleagues take a LiDAR-based burial-mound detector into the field and rebuild it from what the field returns. The inherited pipeline runs a YOLO proposer over Local Relief Models built from 1 m LiDAR-derived terrain models in Alto Minho, northern Portugal, post-filters with a Location-Based Ranking rule and a Local Outlier Factor check, and reduces 3,417 raw inferences to 648, of which four archaeologists with remote-sensing experience digitally confirmed 470. The paper's contribution begins where most prospection papers stop: 237 of those 470 went to ground truthing.

What ground truthing returned is the most useful thing here. Only 23 inferences (9.7%) were confirmed True Positives and 63 (26.6%) False Positives; 64 (27%) were uncertain and 87 (36.7%) unreachable, so 63.7% of the sample resolved to neither class. Confidence was graded explicitly — 85% or above for a True or False Positive, with the 50–84% band subdivided into six 'very likely / probable / eventual' categories — and the grading came out asymmetric in a way worth carrying: no inference was assigned 100% confidence as a True Positive, whereas 37 of the 63 False Positives were.

Two refinements follow, and their architecture is ours in a different modality. A cheap geometric filter (mean slope over a bounding box dilated by 25 pixels) precedes a Vision Transformer fine-tuned to separate burial mounds from previously rejected inferences and described as 'the final filtering stage' — a proposer-then-verifier cascade whose verifier is trained on the proposer's own errors, 276 known mounds against 178 digitally invalidated inferences, augmented to a balanced split. It is instructive about the price. At Barbanza the cascade lifts F1 from 0.31 to 0.37, but the lift is a precision effect (0.30 → 0.61) partly paid for in recall (0.32 → 0.26): the verifier discarded 9 of 49 True Positives to remove 52 of 78 False Positives. For scale, 0.37 is the best F1 the paper reports, against 0.29 for the closest comparable published approach on the same region.

Three cautions transfer directly. First, both slope thresholds were chosen post hoc on the labelled evaluation data — the angle that excludes no True Positive while maximising False Positive removal, 17° at Alto Minho and 12° at Barbanza — making them oracle operating points reported as results. Second, the two differ because Barbanza's mounds sit on flatter ground, which the discussion concedes makes a cross-region threshold hard to set: a transfer tax stated but never costed. Third, the ViT's 0.91 validation accuracy, measured on a class-balanced augmented split of Alto Minho data, becomes 65% at Alto Minho and 73% at Barbanza in deployment. The Alto Minho figure is labelled 'precision' but is accuracy — 23 True Positives plus 26 correctly rejected False Positives over the 75 inferences classified — and Alto Minho recall is not computable at all, as the paper says, so its headline precision gain from 27% to 47% rests on a denominator of the 86 field-adjudicated inferences, not the 237 visited or the 648 proposed.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Archaeological prospection prior art of the directly comparable kind — the same target class, burial mounds, detected automatically — but from LiDAR-derived terrain models rather than map symbols, and with the detection stage inherited from the authors' earlier work rather than contributed here. Its value to us is structural rather than numerical: it is the closest published instance of a proposer-then-verifier cascade in mound detection and it prices the added filtering honestly (at Barbanza the slope filter and ViT together take precision 0.30 → 0.61, recall 0.32 → 0.26 and F1 0.31 → 0.37; the slope filter alone buys 0.30 → 0.39 of the precision at no recall cost, while the verifier stage takes 0.39 → 0.61 but contributes only 0.35 → 0.37 of the F1 and carries every point of the recall loss), while its two slope thresholds — 17° at Alto Minho, 12° at Barbanza, each selected on the labelled evaluation data — are oracle operating points of exactly the kind our carried-versus-oracle reporting exists to separate. It also supplies the field's most sobering ground-truth datum: of 237 AI inferences selected for ground truthing, 63.7% could not be resolved as either True Positive or False Positive.

### KP1
- **Quote (verbatim):** "we propose to take advantage of the Vision Transformer (ViT) model [31] for image classification as the final filtering stage"
- **Locator:** page_index 9 · p.10 · §4 Algorithm Refinement — the ViT filter
- **Paraphrase:** A separately trained image classifier is bolted on after the detector and the geometric filters, with the single job of rejecting the detector's False Positives. The same page gives its supervision: the burial-mound class is the 276 known, annotated Alto Minho mounds, and the False Positive class is the 178 inferences that four expert archaeologists had digitally invalidated in the authors' previous work — so the verifier is trained on the proposer's own rejected output. It also reads a different sensor from the proposer: the ViT classifies 25 cm RGB orthophotos (Direção-Geral do Território 2021 at Alto Minho, page 9; IGN-PNOA 2017 at Barbanza, page 13), whereas the YOLO proposer saw LiDAR-derived Local Relief Models — so the cascade's second opinion rests on independent evidence rather than a re-read of the same pixels. That is the structural antecedent to our adversarial verifier pass, with one difference that should travel: theirs needs a labelled error corpus to exist at all, ours is prompted.

### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Precision 0.30 0.39 0.61 Recall 0.32 0.32 0.26 F1 Score 0.31 0.35 0.37"
- **Locator:** page_index 13 · p.14 · §5.2 Barbanza — Table 9
- **Paraphrase:** Table 9's three columns are the 164 Barbanza inferences carried over from the authors' previously published pipeline — YOLO plus the Location-Based Ranking and Local Outlier Factor post-filters — then those same inferences after the slope filter, then after the ViT filter, scored against a ground truth of 152 known burial mounds. Precision 0.30 is therefore an already-filtered baseline, and the two new stages are the third and fourth filter in the stack. Filtering doubles precision from 0.30 to 0.61 and lifts F1 from 0.31 to 0.37, but recall falls from 0.32 to 0.26 because the ViT discards 9 of the 49 True Positives along with 52 of the 78 False Positives. The headline F1 gain conceals a recall regression, which is only visible because the paper reports all three metrics per stage. The authors' own conclusion undercuts these numbers in the safe direction: at Barbanza 'every detection that did not coincide with the known burial mounds contributed as a False Positive in the calculations, although some promising inferences could, in fact, be burial mounds' (page 14), so the precision and F1 quoted here are lower bounds on a closed-world reference set.

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Suppose an angle threshold that does not exclude any True Positives and maximizes the number of removed False Positives: 17o."
- **Locator:** page_index 12 · p.13 · §5.1 Alto Minho
- **Paraphrase:** The slope threshold is chosen with the evaluation labels in hand: pick the angle that throws away no confirmed True Positive while removing the most confirmed False Positives. That is an oracle operating point, and the numbers reported downstream (Table 8, precision 0.27 → 0.31 → 0.47) are oracle-point numbers. The paper does not report what a threshold carried from elsewhere would have cost, and the same procedure is repeated independently at Barbanza with a different answer. A principled alternative was available, and the paper sets it up on page 9 — a histogram of the mean inclination around the 276 known mounds, from which 'we can eliminate inferences that exceed a predefined inclination threshold' — but no result is ever reported from a threshold derived that way. The oracle selection displaced an available principled one, for which the authors had already assembled the apparatus.

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "These areas housing burial mounds in Barbanza are flatter than Alto Minho, making it more difficult to set a threshold for the slope filter that can be efficiently applied across regions."
- **Locator:** page_index 14 · p.15 · §6 Discussion
- **Paraphrase:** The authors concede that a filter threshold tuned in one region does not obviously carry to another, even when the two regions have similar topology and comparable mound morphology — the two selected thresholds were 17° and 12°. The concession is qualitative: no carried-threshold result is reported, so the size of the transfer penalty is unknown. The authors read their models as having generalised — trained on Alto Minho alone, YOLOv5 and the ViT 'demonstrated reliable generalization' at Barbanza — though that verdict rests on an F1 of 0.37 at recall 0.26, and they hedge it in the next sentence for regions of different topology and mound morphology. What they concede does not travel is the hand-set threshold.

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "This resulted in a high percentage (63.7%) of inferences that were not classified as True Positive or False Positive."
- **Locator:** page_index 5 · p.6 · §3.2 Results—Alto Minho
- **Paraphrase:** Of the 237 inferences selected for ground truthing, Table 1 records 23 True Positives (9.7%) and 63 False Positives (26.6%); the remaining 64 uncertain (27%) and 87 non-accessible (36.7%) — 63.7% together — could not be adjudicated.
