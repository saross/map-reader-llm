## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Van den Akker and thirteen colleagues offer a 25-question preregistration template for secondary data analysis, with per-question commentary and a worked example. Their premise is close to ours: preregistration guidance was built for studies that will collect new data, and leaves out the case where the analyses are new but the data are not. Our maps and gold standard pre-existed the study in that sense, so this template — rather than the standard OSF Preregistration template it was adapted from at a 2018 Society for the Improvement of Psychological Science (SIPS) session — is the closer instrument.

Its distinctive machinery is Part 4, ‘Knowledge of data’. Q17 asks for the publications, working papers, and conference presentations based on the dataset — per co-author, the commentary insists — with relevant variables named for each; Q18 asks what prior knowledge each author holds, stating the per-author requirement in the question itself. The Q18 commentary then draws a line: direct knowledge of a hypothesised association disqualifies a confirmatory test on that dataset — abandon the confirmatory framing, or move to a different dataset. Indirect knowledge does not disqualify, provided it is transparently reported. Which side pilot exposure to the same corpus falls on is ours to argue: the source defines direct knowledge by what is known about the association, not by who handled the data, so the permissive reading must be earned in the retrospective, not assumed.

What the source does not supply is finer-grained registration. The template is one static, up-front document: the questions answered before analysis, none of them mandatory, closing with a formal statement that no other preregistration exists ‘pertaining to the same hypotheses and dataset’. What has shrunk relative to a primary-data template is the object registered — an analysis of existing data — not the number of registrations. Citing it as analysis-level registration should say which sense is meant.

On volume they sit on the other side of our retrospective. They concede the count ‘may be overwhelming’, add that ‘not every question is relevant for every preregistration’ and that no question is mandatory, then recommend answering as many questions in as much detail as possible, because elaboration restricts more researcher degrees of freedom. Cost is not ignored — preregistering ‘is challenging and can take a lot of time’ but is ‘more than worth it’ — yet it is never weighed question by question, and the cost of revising or departing from an elaborate registration goes unmentioned.

Evidentially this is a tutorial, not a study: it ‘did not include any data or conducted analyses’, and the worked example’s second preregistrant is a link to Wikipedia’s Hogwarts staff page, so the example demonstrates the template rather than reporting an executed registration. The template came out of that expert session and a subsequent ‘digital back and forth involving the preregistration of an actual secondary data analysis’ — though the next sentence calls that same effort ‘the preregistration of an example analysis’, never separating the real exercise from the invented illustration.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Preregistration and open-science methodology, in the narrow sub-branch that applies to us: templates written for studies whose data already exist. Van den Akker et al. supply the machinery our own registration most needs — per-author disclosure of prior knowledge of the dataset, with a stated line between direct knowledge (which disqualifies a confirmatory test on that dataset) and indirect knowledge (which need only be reported) — while sitting against our retrospective on volume, recommending that authors answer as many questions in as much detail as possible (of those relevant to the study, since ‘not every question is relevant for every preregistration’) and judging the time cost more than worth paying. It is prior art for registering an analysis of pre-existing data, but not for registering at a finer grain than the study: their document is a single static up-front instrument, so any unit-shrinkage reading of this source must be scoped to the object registered, not to the number of registrations.

### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "In cases like this, where you have direct knowledge about a hypothesized association, you should disregard doing a confirmatory analysis altogether or do one based on a different dataset."
- **Locator:** page_index 11 · p.12 · §Q18 commentary (Part 4: Knowledge of data)
- **Paraphrase:** Where an author already knows the direction or magnitude of a hypothesised association in the dataset, the commentary says not to run a confirmatory analysis on that dataset at all — drop the confirmatory framing, or move to a different dataset. This is guidance attached to Q18’s commentary rather than one of the 25 template questions, and it is stated for direct knowledge of the association specifically; the preceding sentence grounds it in the argument that if you already know the test result, interpreting the test is invalid.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — what pilot exposure to the corpus costs a registration · Prior knowledge of the data and eligibility for a confirmatory test · **complicates**

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Any indirect knowledge about the hypothesized association does not preclude a confirmatory analysis but should be transparently reported in this section."
- **Locator:** page_index 11 · p.12 · §Q18 commentary (Part 4: Knowledge of data)
- **Paraphrase:** The complementary rule to the direct-knowledge bar: knowledge that bears only indirectly on the hypothesised association still permits a confirmatory analysis, so long as it is reported transparently in the preregistration’s prior-knowledge section. For indirect knowledge the remedy is disclosure, not abstention — and the same commentary concedes it is ‘unlikely that you are able to account for all the effects of prior knowledge’, unconscious knowledge included, so the disclosure is best-effort rather than exhaustive.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — the registration’s own honesty apparatus · Prior knowledge disclosure as the remedy short of abstention · **supports**

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Even though none of the questions are mandatory, we do believe that an elaborate preregistration is preferable over a concise preregistration simply because it restricts more researcher degrees of freedom."
- **Locator:** page_index 15 · p.16 · §Summary
- **Paraphrase:** No individual question in the 25-question template is compulsory, and the authors accept the count may feel overwhelming, but their recommendation is maximal: answer as many questions in as much detail as possible, because a more elaborate preregistration constrains more researcher degrees of freedom. The justification is quantity of constraint. Cost is acknowledged on the same page — preregistering ‘is challenging and can take a lot of time’ but is ‘more than worth it’ — yet never weighed question by question, and the concession about volume is immediately qualified by ‘not every question is relevant for every preregistration’.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — the over-bake retrospective (grain, not volume) · Volume versus grain in registration design · **complicates**

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "any test not included here must be labeled as an exploratory test in the final paper"
- **Locator:** page_index 12 · p.13 · §Q19 (Part 5: Analyses)
- **Paraphrase:** The registered set of statistical tests is exhaustive by default: a test not written into the analysis question has to be reported as exploratory in the final paper. Nothing is forbidden, but the confirmatory/exploratory boundary is fixed by what the registration says rather than by a later judgement about which tests were really planned; Q25 extends the same logic to exploration itself, asking that planned exploratory tests either be described in advance or be reported under a clearly differentiated heading.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 / analyses register · Registered-versus-exploratory boundary (the analyses register) · **supports**

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "However, note that decision trees come with their own problems and can quickly become very complex."
- **Locator:** page_index 14 · p.15 · §Q23 commentary (Part 5: Analyses)
- **Paraphrase:** Having just suggested that prior knowledge of a dataset can be used to build a decision tree for anticipated analytic problems, the commentary immediately qualifies it: decision trees carry problems of their own and grow complex quickly. The alternatives offered are selecting maximally conservative assumptions, preregistering robustness analyses that test sensitivity to different assumptions, and pre-specifying a single primary strategy while flagging an exploratory check of distributional assumptions.
- **Relevance:** §D.4 decision tree / §D.9 · Registered flexibility — the complexity objection to decision trees · **complicates**

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "if they hold for one or two of the regressions, we will conclude that our hypothesis is partially supported"
- **Locator:** page_index 13 · p.14 · §Q22 (Part 5: Analyses)
- **Paraphrase:** The worked example commits in advance to a graded verdict rule across the three regressions that operationalise its stated hypothesis: all three meeting both criteria means fully supported, one or two means partially supported, none means unsupported. The two criteria are a p-value below .01 — chosen as 0.05/3, rounded down, to account for one test per regression — and a coefficient exceeding the pre-stated minimum effect size of interest. The commentary’s rationale is that researchers otherwise move the goalposts when making inferences.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 / hypothesis adjudication · Pre-specified, graded inference criteria across multiple endpoints · **supports**
