## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Davis, Caspari, Lipo and Sanger train a Mask R–CNN, built in ArcGIS Pro on a ResNet50 backbone, on LiDAR-derived elevation, hillshade and slope composites, and run it over 6,712 km² of coastal South Carolina to find Archaic shell rings and mounds. The archaeological payoff is a range extension: five probable rings in Georgetown County, the northernmost 53 km beyond the Sewee Shell Ring. For us the interest is procedural: this is the closest published example of the pipeline our design is an alternative to, run end to end at landscape scale and candid about most of what it cost.

Three things transfer directly. First, the human triage. The trained model returned 2,035 rings and 3,254 mounds across Beaufort and Charleston; after manual evaluation to strip false positives from river channels, riverbanks and modern development, 120 rings and 287 mounds survived — roughly 94 per cent of ring detections and 91 per cent of mound detections discarded by hand. No precision or recall is reported anywhere in the body, so the discard rate is the only precision signal available, and the manual pass an annotation cost the paper never prices. (The ring breakdown, 52 plus 91, sums to 143 rather than the stated 120 — a slip in the published text, not an extraction artefact.)

Second, the operating point does not travel. In Georgetown County the authors lowered the confidence threshold to 0.2, because morphological differences between training and new rings suppressed detection at the higher thresholds used in prior tests. Even so, 63 objects were returned and 5 judged probably archaeological. That is our carried-versus-oracle problem in raw form: a threshold set on the calibration region, then hand-lowered for recall on the deployment region, which held no known rings against which the new value could be justified.

Third, the reported model metrics are thin. Table 1 gives validation accuracy only — 75 per cent for shell rings, 79.5 per cent for mounds, 59.5 per cent for the modern hard-negative class — on the 10 per cent of training data withheld, the caption conceding that ArcGIS Pro supplied accuracy rather than sensitivity or precision, which sit in supplementary files. Since the pool was already enlarged eightfold by 45-degree rotations before that split, rotated copies of the same 18 rings could fall on both sides; the paper does not say they were excluded. The 75 per cent is at least carried honestly into the discussion as a limitation.

Two further points are ours to use. The verification design — an independent random forest on Sentinel-1 SAR and Sentinel-2 multispectral data, re-identifying 9 of the 12 candidate rings north of Sewee — is a real antecedent for our adversarial verifier: a second, differently sensed opinion rather than a rerun of the proposer. And the barriers the paper names for deep learning in archaeology — training data, compute, expertise — are what a prompted vision-language model relocates rather than removes: the data and tool-building burdens fall away, while compute becomes a metered API bill.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Archaeological prospection prior art with a near-identical target class — mounded shell and earthen architecture, detected automatically — but in LiDAR-derived terrain rather than a cartographic symbol layer, and as region-proposal object detection across whole counties rather than symbol localisation on map tiles, which places it on a different rung of our difficulty ladder (real morphology, not a drawn convention) while sharing our object-level framing. Its two strongest uses for us are the attrition figure and the threshold retune: 2,035 raw ring detections reduced to 120 by hand, and a confidence threshold dropped to 0.2 on the transfer county because training-region morphology did not generalise — the human-in-the-loop cost and the transfer tax that our carried-versus-oracle operating points are built to measure, stated plainly by a study with no incentive to hide them. Secondarily it is a verification-design antecedent (an independent SAR and multispectral random forest as a second opinion on the detector's candidates) and a metric-hygiene caution, since no precision or recall appears in the body at all: the quality numbers are validation accuracies on a 10 per cent split of an eightfold-augmented training pool, plus loss curves and a 94.4 per cent re-identification rate on the training rings themselves.

- **Verifier separation is band-dependent** — The separation the independent verifier rests on is band-dependent, so its discriminating power comes from part of its evidence rather than all of it. In the single-polarisation Sentinel-1 bands (VV ascending and VV descending), confirmed and detected shell rings are statistically different from non-archaeological features (p < 0.04) and statistically similar to each other (p > 0.6); in the dual-polarisation bands, all three classes are statistically similar to one another (p > 0.1). (page_index 6 · p.7 · §4.1 Additional assessment of shell rings using SAR, multispectral, and random forest probability)
- **Verifier coverage and configuration** — The random-forest check does not cover the whole candidate set. Because the Sentinel-1 data cannot penetrate dense vegetative canopies, the analysis is limited to shell rings in marshes without heavy vegetation (n = 11), set against 33 locations around those rings with no recorded archaeological materials; the classifier is trained on 9 of the confirmed rings with 2 withheld for validation, using 128 trees and 3 iterations after Orengo and colleagues (2020). The second opinion is therefore itself a very small model applied to a favourable subset of the terrain. (page_index 5 · p.6 · §3.3 Additional support of deep learning results)
- **Third explanation not excluded** — In the Sentinel-2 multispectral data the suspected rings appear distinct not only from their surroundings but from the sample of confirmed rings as well. The authors offer three explanations — low spatial resolution at 10–20 m, diversity among shell ring signatures, or the possibility that some of the detected shell rings are not archaeological — and state that they believe the first two are the most probable. The third is judged less likely rather than excluded, which is the authors' own acknowledgement that verifier agreement does not settle whether every candidate is a site. (page_index 6 · p.7 · §4.1 Additional assessment of shell rings using SAR, multispectral, and random forest probability)
- **Recall is preservation-dependent** — The authors' own gloss on the out-of-region Sapelo Island test is that the model successfully identifies well-preserved shell rings but needs further improvement to locate damaged deposits. Since 1 of the 3 Sapelo rings was detected and the two missed are recorded as destroyed features (reported across pp.6–7), the study's evidence for recall on undamaged targets outside the training region rests on a single positive detection. (page_index 6 · p.7 · §4 Results — Sapelo Island out-of-region test)
- **New rings skew small** — The rings this study detected are smaller on average than those already on record — 4,331.62 m² against 5,980.77 m² for pre-documented rings within the study region — which the authors present as a continuation of a pattern in earlier LiDAR-based discoveries. The new detections therefore sit at the smaller end of the size range the model was trained on. (page_index 9 · p.10 · §5 Discussion)
- **Ground-validation base rates** — The ground-validation comparison is against the state Department of Natural Resources (DNR) site database: 47 of the study's identified ring and mound features fall within DNR lands, and 22 of those 47 are confirmed archaeological deposits, of which 'many' — the paper gives no count — were not used as training data for either the deep-learning or the random-forest model. Table 2, which lists these sites, is labelled a 'Selection' and carries only 9 site identifiers, so the 22 are never enumerated. (page_index 8 · p.9 · §4.2 Ground validation)
- **Training run stopped early** — Training stopped after 20 epochs at a learning rate of 6.30957e-06, when improvements ceased — against the 50 epochs the methods section says the run was configured for, with early stopping to save time and processing power (p.4). The 41 h 23 min of workstation GPU time the paper reports is therefore the cost of a run that converged at less than half its planned length. (page_index 5 · p.6 · §4 Results)
- **Scanned-area figures reconcile** — The headline coverage figure is internally consistent: the 6,712 km² scanned is exactly the sum of the three county areas given in the methods — Beaufort 2,390 km², Georgetown 805 km² and Charleston 3,517 km² (p.4). This is worth checking before citing the paper's ring-count slip, where 52 plus 91 is reported as 120: the arithmetic error there is a local one rather than a symptom of unreliable reporting throughout. (page_index 9 · p.10 · §5 Discussion)
