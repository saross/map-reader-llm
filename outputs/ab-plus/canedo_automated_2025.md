## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Canedo and colleagues detect hillforts by semantic segmentation, fusing LiDAR-derived local relief models (LRMs) with aerial orthoimages in an adapted CMX cross-modal transformer, post-filtering with margin and minimum-area rules, and closing an expert-in-the-loop hard positive and negative mining loop over training data biased towards negatives. It sits at the large-area-deployment end of the prospection prior art: after fine-tuning on four English tiles, the system was swept across all 232 LRM tiles covering England — over 600 GB of LiDAR-derived data, roughly 30 days of processing on a two-GPU machine, a duration the authors attribute to the slow WMS service and the 1/2 window step, and expect caching to cut to a week or less. (Page indices follow the 2024 early-view text, not the 2025 issue pagination.)

What is most useful to us is not the architecture but how the headline numbers were made. Both reported F1 scores rest on expert re-adjudication of the reference data. In the Northwest Iberia test region (PNPG) the refined model returned 49 inferences against nine annotated hillforts; validation found only 25 of the 40 nominal false positives to be false, promoting six previously unannotated hillforts to true positives and nine more to 'potential true positives', and Table 6's precision 0.49 / recall 1.00 / F1 0.66 groups those potentials into the numerator. Scored against the annotation the model was trained to match, precision is 9/49 (our arithmetic, not the paper's). This is the curator-versus-adjudicated distinction our ground-truth section draws, run as explicit protocol — and the model's own output triggered the revision.

England is scoped three further ways a casual citation loses. Raw matching against Atlas of Hillforts points gave 8113 inferences, 675 true positives, 7438 false positives, and 578 false negatives — precision near 0.08 on our arithmetic. The reported precision 0.45 / recall 0.84 / F1 0.59 comes instead from a 1461-inference expert subsample inside 34 of the 232 tiles (18% of the inferences), again grouping potential true positives with true positives, and with a recall denominator restricted to the 126 of 267 false negatives visible in the imagery. Both reconstructions reproduce the published figures exactly, so the reading is secure. Matching used a 300 m buffer on every inference, because Atlas points sit off the sites (the paper illustrates offsets of 103 and 212 m) — six to fifteen times looser than the 20 m and 50 m radii we match at.

Cost is the second lesson. False-positive control is bought by retraining: one mining iteration cut PNPG false positives from 5678 to 40, a 99.3% reduction, true positives retained. But the loop needs an expert archaeologist to classify inferences by hand (4462 images in the Iberian iteration), the model must be fine-tuned again for every new site typology and landscape, and the authors could not run the ablation that would numerically verify the orthoimage modality's contribution, so the fusion benefit is assumed, not measured. Our adversarial verifier pass is the prompt-time analogue of hard-negative mining, without its retraining or labelling.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Archaeological prospection prior art at the deployment end of the scale — a segmentation model swept across an entire country, where most of our prior-art neighbours are single-region studies — and the clearest worked example in the cluster of how much of a published F1 is produced by the evaluation protocol rather than by the model. Its two reported scores, 0.66 in Northwest Iberia and 0.59 in England, both fold expert-adjudicated 'potential true positives' into the numerator; the England score is scoped twice further, restricting the recall denominator to the misses visible in the imagery and matching at a 300 m buffer against Atlas points, neither of which applies in Iberia, where the test region records no false negatives at all and the reference data are annotated polygons; the unadjudicated England precision is nearer 0.08 on our arithmetic from its Table 9, and that is the number to carry when our object-level results at 20 m and 50 m are set beside it. Secondarily it is a cost datum: the 99.3% false-positive reduction was bought with a single expert-labelling iteration over 4462 images in Iberia, and carrying the result to England cost a further fine-tuning pass on four tiles plus roughly 30 days of sweep (WMS-bound; the authors expect caching to cut it to a week or less) — the budget against which our zero-training, prompt-plus-verifier design is measured.
