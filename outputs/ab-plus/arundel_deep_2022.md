## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Arundel, Morgan and Thiem fine-tune Mask TextSpotter V3 — an end-to-end scene-text spotter with a ResNet-50 backbone and a segmentation proposal network — to detect and read spot elevations on scans from the U.S. Geological Survey's historical topographic map collection (HTMC). It is the closest published analogue to our task in the historical-map lineage: one class of mark, on cluttered scanned topographic sheets, localised and interpreted in a single pass. The contrasts with a prompted vision-language model are the useful part.

Supervision comes first. Pretrained on SynthText and fine-tuned on four further benchmark scene-text corpora (ICDAR 2013, ICDAR 2015, SCUT and Total Text) with no map-specific examples, the workflow got no HTMC spot elevation right at all. Map-specific labels were made by hand: Geographic Names Information System (GNIS) summits sampled at random across the coterminous United States, bounding boxes heads-up digitised around each elevation string and every individual digit, giving 353 image/label pairs trimmed to 350. Thirty-five pairs lift the harmonic mean from 0 to 63.3%, 210 reach 78.6%, and the best model of that series — 245 training images at a reduced 200 k iterations — reaches 82.4%. The curve is not monotonic: every configuration scored worse at 140 training images than at 105, which the authors attribute tentatively to 'some error' in one 35-image batch that they could not identify. Cite the body's Table 4, not the abstract, whose '50' and '350' pairs match no row of it.

Compute comes second. One run is about 40 hours at 300 k iterations on a single Tesla P40; 200 k iterations halve that to 20 hours with little or no change in harmonic mean. The headline ~95% is bought with augmentation rather than more annotation: offset, rotation and scale copies build a mixed set of 1,333 examples (934 training, 399 test), and the mix-trained model averages 95.6% harmonic mean across the five test sets against 78.0–80.9% for the four non-mixed models — a margin the authors expected in direction but not in size, and one that confounds training-set size with training diversity.

Third, and decisive for how we cite the number: the substrate is curated. Every image is a 400 × 400 pixel crop holding exactly one spot elevation — the originals at 1:4,000 and centred on the mark, the augmented copies offset, rotated or rescaled. Scoring is a harmonic mean (their Table 3's F-score, which they note is commonly called accuracy) at intersection over union ≥ 0.5, on a single 70/30 split with no cross-validation and no interval estimates. The authors themselves expect the accuracy to plummet in an operational context, name map tiling, multiple features per image, disjointed text and duplicate instances as the unsolved problems, and close their comparison with earlier historical-map text work by calling a direct comparison impossible. Read it not as a rival number but as the supervision-and-compute counterfactual a zero-training detector must justify itself against — and its failure modes, other features crossing the characters and scan noise, are ours too.
