## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

A short ‘Scientific Life’ comment in Trends in Cognitive Sciences (October 2019, Vol. 23, No. 10, pp. 815–818), eight authors led by Nosek, two (Nosek, Mellor) disclosed as Center for Open Science employees. It is guidance, not evidence: no new data, and a single hedged appeal to any — “Some evidence suggests that such workflows are effective in improving the specificity of preregistrations” (their ref. [11]). Read it as the standard our retrospective is measured against, not a finding to cite.

Three aims open the piece: separating a priori from post hoc analyses (calibration for the unplanned, diagnosticity for the planned), detecting selective outcome reporting and HARKing, and blunting publication bias. Two arguments carry it. First, preregistration is a skill. Converting “a general sense” of design into explicit plans is hard, especially the first time, because “when the outcomes are unknown, the universe of contingencies is much larger”. The prescription is incrementalism, the structured registration workflows of OSF, SREE, and Declare Design, plus four skill-building strategies: simulating data; splitting real data and registering after seeing the exploratory subset; drafting methods and results in advance; and submitting the plan as a registered report. Second, deviations. A plan is not a prison and one should deviate where that improves the research, but all deviations “should be acknowledged”, and the apparatus is a five-item checklist: outcome-blind amendment where possible, every planned analysis mentioned, a table of deviations, supplements used to disclose rather than hide, and a decision log kept during analysis. The risk is named: “A seemingly trivial deviation might be highly consequential.”

For D.9 that checklist reads as an audit list. Our analyses register maps onto item (ii) and our recorded deviations onto item (iii); an outcome-blind amendment matches item (i) exactly, while item (v), decisions logged “as they were made”, is answered only so far as our logging was contemporaneous. The familywise-error argument — preregistration “showing how many statistical tests were conducted” — warrants family-level error control across a registered hypothesis set. Their exploratory/confirmatory split is the nearest thing to a sanctioned calibrate-then-confirm — one of four exercises for developing preregistration skill, not a design prescription — and it carries the condition their framing implies: the confirmatory subsample stays unviewed while the plan is fixed, where a calibration set folded back into an F1 and MCC evaluation would part company with them.

Two limits travel with it. The register is psychology throughout — stopping rules, skewed distributions, outliers, exclusion criteria, covariates — with no computational-evaluation instance anywhere, and silent on grain and on automation — though not on tooling, which gets a full paragraph, Box 1, and the piece’s one appeal to evidence. It says nothing about how many registrations a study should carry (the nearest, incrementalism and standard operating procedures, are across studies rather than within one), and computation, machine learning, automation, and language models appear nowhere in the extracted text (verified by search). It supports the retrospective’s tone and apparatus without contesting either the grain or the LLM-support claim.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

The canonical short statement of the standard a preregistration retrospective is judged against, and the source that most directly licenses D.9’s candour — primary cluster: preregistration and open-science methodology (secondary: methods/metrics, via the familywise-error argument). Its transparency checklist — outcome-blind amendment, mention every planned analysis, tabulate every deviation, log decisions with rationales as they are made — is close to an audit list our apparatus can be scored against, and its “plan, not a prison” framing pre-empts the reviewer who reads a deviations table as an admission of failure. What it does not supply is grain or automation — nothing on how many registrations a study should carry, and no mention anywhere in the extracted text of computation, machine learning, or automated authoring or checking, so it neither occupies nor contests the LLM-support cell — but tooling it does supply, and that is the nearest incumbent any automation would displace: structured registry workflows and decision tools for authoring a plan (OSF, SREE, Declare Design), and registered-report peer review for checking one; its worked examples never leave the psychology register, though the Box 1 registries are not psychology-specific.

## Key points (salience-ranked to the citing paper)

### KP1
- **Quote (verbatim):** "(i) when possible, update the preregistration with deviations before observing the data (e.g., fix errors in the analysis plan; add obvious exclusion rules that become apparent); (ii) mention all planned analyses, if only to explain why a planned analysis was not reported; (iii) include a table documenting and explaining all deviations; (iv) use supplements to share in full, not to hide inconvenient information; and (v) during analysis, actively log the decisions as they were made with rationales."
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3 · § Transparently Reporting Deviations Helps Calibrate Confidence in Claims
- **Paraphrase:** Five practices are offered as “Additional transparency strategies to maximize credibility of reported findings”: amend the registration before the data are seen where that is possible (fixing plan errors, adding exclusions that become obvious); name every planned analysis even if only to explain why it was not reported; tabulate and explain every deviation; use supplements to disclose in full rather than to bury awkward material; and log decisions with their rationales while the analysis is under way. They are presented as strategies rather than requirements, and the same section concedes the cost: reporting deviations “can be challenging, especially because of journal word limits, pressures for narrative coherence, and reviewer expectations”.

### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "When deviations from the plan will improve the quality of the research, deviate from the plan."
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3 · § Transparently Reporting Deviations Helps Calibrate Confidence in Claims
- **Paraphrase:** Fidelity to the plan is not the goal: where departing from it would improve the research, the authors direct researchers to depart. ### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Deviations inevitably make it harder to interpret with confidence what occurred in relation to what was planned. Transparency is key – all deviations from the plan should be acknowledged."
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3 · § Transparently Reporting Deviations Helps Calibrate Confidence in Claims
- **Paraphrase:** Deviating carries an interpretive cost that cannot be argued away — the relation between plan and outcome becomes harder to read with confidence — and the remedy offered is not fewer deviations but complete disclosure of them. The surrounding text scopes the risk concretely, with the worked example that “changing from a combined outcome variable to using just one component of the combination can inflate false positive risk”, and asks that, where possible, results under the original plan be reported alongside results with the deviations, with materials, data, and code shared so others can evaluate both.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — what the deviations cost and what disclosure buys · Deviation reporting: the completeness standard, and its cost · **supports**

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "preregistration of analysis plans makes clear which analyses were planned a priori and which were conducted post hoc"
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2 · § opening — the three aims of preregistration
- **Paraphrase:** The first of the three aims listed for preregistration is a bookkeeping one: the record shows which analyses were planned in advance and which were run after the fact. The source ties the payoff to two distinct things — “calibration of uncertainty for unplanned analyses, and diagnosticity of statistical inferences for planned analyses” — so the distinction is presented as informative in both directions, not as a verdict against post hoc work.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — what the registration apparatus was for · The analyses register: separating registered tests from post hoc analysis · **supports**

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Preregistration makes transparent the uncertainty of statistical tests by showing how many statistical tests were conducted and enabling an accurate familywise error rate."
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2 · § opening — the three aims of preregistration
- **Paraphrase:** Registering the analysis plan makes the denominator of the testing visible — how many tests were actually run — which is what allows a familywise error rate to be computed accurately. The argument in the surrounding text is that a nominal 5% Type I error rate holds only for a single test whose assumptions are met, and that “Undisclosed and unplanned analyses undermine the assumptions.”
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — the registration delivered its goods (family-level error control) · Family-level error control across a registered hypothesis set · **supports**

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "splitting the real data into exploratory and confirmatory subsamples and preregistering the analysis after viewing the exploratory subset"
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3 · § Preregistration Improves with Practice
- **Paraphrase:** One of four listed strategies for developing preregistration skill is to divide the real data, look at the exploratory part, and only then register the analysis to be run on the confirmatory part. The source offers this as practice for building the skill rather than as a general design prescription, and its logic requires the confirmatory subsample to remain unviewed while the plan is being fixed.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 (with the calibration-transfer argument) — registering after a pilot look · Calibration on a subset and leakage into evaluation · **supports**

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Having some plans is better than having no plans, and sharing those plans in advance is better than not sharing them."
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3 · § Preregistration Improves with Practice
- **Paraphrase:** An incomplete registration is worth more than none, and a shared plan worth more than an unshared one — the authors’ answer to researchers who “shy away from preregistration for worries about imperfection”. It sits under their instruction to “Embrace incrementalism.” and the claim that “Preregistration is a methodological skill that takes time to develop.” The improvement they describe is across successive studies; nowhere in the extracted text do they discuss splitting a single study’s registration into smaller units.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — the waterfall-to-agile arc and the incrementalism warrant · Grain-not-volume: what the incumbent literature does and does not say about registration granularity · **supports**
