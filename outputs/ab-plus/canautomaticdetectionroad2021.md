## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Can, Gerrits and Kabadayı segment seven road types from the Third Military Mapping Survey of Austria-Hungary (the Generalkarte, surveyed 1884–1918) with the dhSegment toolbox, over 256 × 256 map fragments. Of our historical-map extraction sources it has the closest substrate match: its 54 georeferenced sheets cover Bulgaria and parts of Greece, North Macedonia, Serbia and Albania — our country and terrain, one survey generation earlier, with the same dense legend printed over contour lines, toponyms and varying background colour. The scale differs: the Generalkarte is 1:200,000 against our 1:50,000.

It is best read as an annotation-budget argument and a difficulty ladder rather than a benchmark: ground truth is hand-drawn polylines, and two research fellows spent more than 1,250 hours digitising 300,000 km of road features in more than 64,000 segments. What the CNN buys is modest, uneven and less coherent than its headline. The conclusion's 'around 0.5 F_measure, 0.45 IoU and 0.93 pixel-wise accuracy', offered for the frequently seen Saumweg, Karrenweg and Erhaltener Fahrweg, is a best-per-metric composite across different road types, not one prediction set: under the better U-Net backbone Karrenweg reaches F_measure 0.5321 and Erhaltener Fahrweg 0.5336 but Saumweg only 0.2234 (Table 6), while the 0.93 accuracy and 0.45 IoU are Saumweg's alone (Table 4). Types whose legends do not separate from the background by thickness, colour or style fall to F_measure 0.10 (Hauptstrasse, 0.1022 under U-Net; ResNet-50's floor is 0.0359). Of those three headline metrics only F_measure discriminates: across the seven U-Net models IoU spans 0.4299–0.4644 and accuracy 0.8599–0.9288, against F_measure's 0.1022–0.5336. Background dominates thin linear features, flattening accuracy and, here, IoU with it — the aggregate-versus-class trap in another dress. Only two of the seven types reach that ceiling; the abstract claims only 'promising results'.

The architecture is the instructive contrast: fourteen models, seven road types by two pretrained backbones (ResNet-50 and U-Net), each a binary road-against-background classifier. Every symbol family costs its own labelled corpus and training run; seven of the legend's 15 road types were attempted. That is the closed-vocabulary, one-model-per-class regime our prompted vision-language model is an alternative to, and it sharpens the burial-mound case: mounds are a rare symbol family, hardest of all to fund per-class supervision for. The paper's own explanation for the spread — frequent roads have more images and score higher — is the rarity problem stated directly, but Table 2 refutes it: Saumweg has the second-largest pool (18,076 images) and F_measure 0.2234, while Erhaltener Fahrweg has the fifth-largest (6,317) and the best score of the seven. Class support constrains this route, but it is not what separates its good road types from its bad ones.

Finally, the paper qualifies the cross-study comparison we would be tempted to make, even as it performs one in Table 1: accuracies rise with map recency, and techniques tried on differently built datasets cannot be ranked. Ours is the more recent and cleaner substrate. Their headline is context for our numbers, not a bar we clear by arithmetic.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Historical-map extraction lineage, and the nearest-substrate member of it we have: the same country, the same class of scanned military topographic sheet — at 1:200,000 against our 1:50,000 — one survey generation earlier, tackled with per-class supervised segmentation. Its use to us is as the attested cost and ceiling of the trained-CNN route on this kind of map: a 1,250-hour digitisation campaign, itself undertaken for an earlier transport-network model and re-used here, standing behind an F_measure of about 0.53 on two of its seven road types, 0.22 on a third the conclusion groups with them, and 0.10 on the worst, from models trained on a flat 1,000-image-per-type subsample. It is also the most concrete rung on our difficulty ladder — detectability tracks how the legend renders a symbol at least as much as how common the symbol is — and it carries its own comparability warning: accuracies rise with map recency and cross-dataset technique rankings are not inferable, so this is context for our numbers, not a benchmark against them.
