## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Chen and colleagues ask what buying more language-model calls actually does to a compound system's accuracy, and answer it for the two simplest designs: Vote (draw K independent generations, take the majority) and Filter-Vote (an LM filter prunes the candidates first, the vote runs over the survivors, falling back to the unfiltered set if the filter rejects everything). The headline result is that accuracy is often non-monotone in K — rising then falling, or falling then rising — on MMLU PHYSICS (151 questions), TRUTHFULQA (817), GPQA diamond (198), and AVERITEC (500) with GPT-3.5-turbo-0125. The explanation is a mixture over query difficulty, where difficulty is defined by the aggregation's own asymptote: a query is easy if a system with infinitely many calls converges on the correct answer and hard otherwise, so more calls push every query towards its own limit — helping the easy ones, hurting the hard ones — and the aggregate curve can turn over where a task mixes the two. The mixture is necessary, not sufficient: Theorem 2, which maps the easy fraction α and the two correctness probabilities p1, p2 to each of four shapes, also gives monotone regimes for mixed datasets. Theorem 4 gives a closed form for the optimal K in the interior-optimum regime, printed as p1 + p2 > 1 and α < 1 − 1/t — but those side conditions must not be lifted into prose: the Appendix C.3 proof contradicts them, and all twelve Table 2 settings with an interior optimum have α ≥ 1 − 1/t (overflow notes §6). A fitted scaling model then locates the optimum from a handful of sample sizes.

This is the closest thing our consensus-over-passes design has to a scaling law, and it cuts both ways. It supports small K: across the fifteen synthetic bi-level settings of Table 2 the optimum ranges from 1 to 30 calls, and the analytical prediction matched the observed optimum in every one. It also names the failure mode our verifier exists to catch — repeated sampling sharpens the modal answer, so wherever the proposer's mode is a confusable non-mound, more passes entrench the error rather than average it away. And because the optimum is a function of the difficulty mixture, a K calibrated on a gold-standard subset transfers only insofar as the deployment corpus shares that mixture: a scaling-law statement of our transfer tax.

Three limits travel with the transfer. The scope is deliberately narrow — tasks with a small answer space that support a majority vote, with many-valid-output tasks flagged as under-explored — whereas our consensus is geometric, over point detections matched within a radius, not exact-match voting. The real-data curves are simulations rather than live K-call runs: one model at temperature 0.1, 400 generations per question, K of them resampled with replacement and averaged over 1,000 runs, so the diversity driving the analysis is low-temperature single-model diversity. And cost is explicitly out of scope, deferred to future work alongside latency — precisely the trade-space we measure.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: consensus / test-time-compute antecedent — this is the theoretical and empirical result behind treating K as a tunable with an interior optimum rather than a budget to maximise, and it supplies the mechanism (difficulty mixture, modal-answer entrenchment) that motivates a verifier pass downstream of greedy consensus — with the caveat that the paper reports its own filter stage turning over too, so a filter is evidence of the problem rather than a demonstrated cure. Its Filter-Vote design is a structural antecedent by analogy of our proposer-plus-verifier pipeline — its aggregation is exact-match voting over a small answer space, ours is geometric agreement over point detections, with the ordering reversed and the filter implemented as self-review (inferred: its prompt reads 'Review your previous answer', and only one model is named in the experiments) rather than an adversarial second pass. Secondarily it is a methods/metrics source: the difficulty-mixture argument is also an argument about how far an operating point chosen on one difficulty distribution can be carried to another, which is the shape of our carried-versus-oracle transfer question.

- **Filter stage is a self-review prompt** — The filter stage of Filter-Vote is driven by a self-review prompt: it asks the model to review its own previous answer, find problems with it, and conclude with a binary verdict of '[[correct]]' or '[[wrong]]'. The filter is thus a second look at an answer framed as the reviewer's own, with a two-way judgement, rather than an independently posed adversarial check — the prompt names no separate reviewer. Any claim that the paper validates 'verifier stages' should be scoped to self-review filters of this kind. (page_index 16 · p.17 · Appendix D.2 — Prompt for the filter)
- **Opposite-shaped scaling curves** — On the same dataset (MMLU PHYSICS) the two designs scale in opposite directions: the plain majority vote first improves and then degrades as calls are added, while the filter-then-vote variant first degrades and then improves. Adding a filter stage can therefore change the sign of the slope, not merely shift the curve up — so a call budget chosen for an unfiltered consensus is not automatically the right budget once a filter is placed in the pipeline. (page_index 1 · p.2 · Figure 1 caption)
- **How repeated sampling entrenches an error** — The authors' worked pair of AVERITEC fact-verification queries makes the mechanism concrete. On the easy query a single call returns the correct answer 67% of the time, which is more often than any competing answer, so more calls converge on it. On the difficult query the correct answer is returned only 34% of the time against an incorrect answer's 56%, so more calls converge on the wrong answer: repeated sampling sharpens whichever answer is modal, right or wrong. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §5 — Figure 4 caption, AVERITEC case study)
- **Scaling law fitted from five call counts** — The scaling law's accuracy check fits the law from system performance measured at just five call counts (K = 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5) and then asks it to predict performance for K from 1 to 100 — a cheap calibration extrapolated twenty-fold beyond the largest call count it was fitted on. The check is run on synthesised datasets with varying bi-level difficulties, not on the four real-world tasks. (page_index 17 · p.18 · Appendix D.5 — Figure 6 caption)
- **Fitting budget on real data** — In the paper's real-data case study (AVERITEC), the analytical scaling model is fitted from six call counts — 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100 LM calls — and the fitted model is then used to predict the performance of Vote and Filter-Vote at 100 randomly drawn call counts. That is the calibration budget the paper paid on real data. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §5 — case study on AVERITEC)
- **Real-data validation is qualitative** — The paper's evidence that the scaling model works on real data is a stated 'high correlation' between predicted and observed performance on AVERITEC, shown as a figure panel; no correlation coefficient, error, or confidence interval is given in the text. The exact-match claims about the predicted optimal number of calls belong to the synthetic experiments, not to this one. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §5 — case study on AVERITEC)
- **Diversity is a design choice** — The framework treats the source of generation diversity as a user's design choice rather than a fixed property of the system: with one model held fixed, diversity can come from a non-zero temperature and from varying the prompt wording or the few-shot examples between calls; and if the parameter set contains different models, the same definition also covers model ensembles. The framework therefore spans temperature-, prompt- and model-level diversity, whatever a particular experiment happens to instantiate. (page_index 3 · p.4 · §3 Inference System Designs — Building Blocks)
- **Stated limitations and the open problem** — The authors limit their claims to two instances of compound systems among many possible types, and to relatively objective language tasks, leaving subjective generation such as poetry and essays unstudied. They name predicting a query's difficulty without first querying the model as an open problem — that is, the difficulty mixture on which their optimal call count depends is not, on their own account, cheaply knowable in advance. (The Limitations text reads 'query difficult'; the slip is the source's.) (page_index 12 · p.13 · Appendix B Limitations)
- **Difficulty split is system-relative** — In the MMLU PHYSICS breakdown the easy/difficult split is close to even and differs between the two designs: 53% easy and 47% difficult under plain voting, 56% and 44% under filter-then-vote. Because difficulty is defined by the aggregation's own limiting behaviour, the same items are partitioned differently depending on which system is asked — difficulty is a property of the item-plus-system pair, not of the item alone. (page_index 2 · p.3 · Figure 2 panel labels — MMLU PHYSICS breakdown)
- **Theorem 2's printed cases are unreliable** — In the Appendix C.3 proof, the case p1 + p2 > 1 with α < 1 − 1/t — an overall-easy task carrying a large fraction of hard queries — is concluded to make performance decrease and then increase as calls are added. That is the opposite shape to the one Theorem 2's printed statement assigns to the same case, so neither the paper's mapping from difficulty parameters to curve shape nor the side conditions printed on its optimal-call-count formula can be cited as they stand. The citable results are the qualitative mechanism (non-monotonicity arising from a mixture of query difficulties) and the reported optima themselves. (page_index 14 · p.15 · Appendix C.3 — Proof of Theorem 2)
- **Reported error of the scaling law** — Across every query-difficulty setting in the synthetic sweep, the mean squared error between the scaling law's predicted performance and the observed performance is reported as between 1e-6 and 1e-4. That figure is measured on simulated bi-level-difficulty data, not on any of the four language tasks, where the model's agreement with observation is described only qualitatively. (page_index 17 · p.18 · Appendix D.5)
- **External precedent for large K** — As an existing use of consensus over many passes, the authors report that Google's Gemini Ultra reached state-of-the-art results on MMLU with a CoT@32 strategy: 32 calls per question, with the majority vote of the 32 responses returned as the answer. This is Chen and colleagues describing the Gemini technical report, not a result they themselves ran. (page_index 0 · p.1 · §1 Introduction)
