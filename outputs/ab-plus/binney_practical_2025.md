## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Binney and colleagues, writing as the British Neuropsychological Society Open Research Group, argue for preregistration less by exhortation than by mapping its routes and pricing them. Four are surveyed — repository preregistration, published protocols, standard Registered Reports, and an emerging family of flexible Registered Reports — with Table 2 scoring each on four scientific and four pragmatic criteria. The organising claim is an antagonism: higher bias control is bought with tighter restriction on the study's flexibility and time-course, so route choice is a design decision, and preregistration is "not an all-or-none proposition".

Two features make it the most operationally useful preregistration source we have read. The first is the six-level bias-control ladder of Table 4 — whose note credits the Peer Community In Registered Reports guide for authors — grading a study by how much data its authors had seen at Stage 1: Level 6, where no part of the data exists and none will be generated until after In Principle Acceptance (the "primary RR"), down to Level 1, where the key variables have been sufficiently observed but no preregistered analysis has yet been run. Exposure becomes declarable rather than binary, and they add the equivalence that matters to us: in exposure terms a secondary study with partial access and a primary study still collecting are not essentially different. Our map sheets pre-existed the study, so Level 6 was never on offer; the ladder gives a vocabulary for where each phase sat. The second is the countermeasure schedule on the lower rungs: Level 2 is admissible only with "additional steps to maximize bias control and rigour" — conservative statistical threshold, blinded analyst, robustness testing, multiverse or specification analysis — and Level 1 with more intensive versions of the same. That reads, by analogy, as a prescription for our carried-versus-oracle operating points, where a threshold chosen on the gold standard is carried to the 55-map deployment. The invariant they never trade away is results-blind evaluation, preserved even at Levels 2 and 1.

The paper is equally candid about what preregistration does not buy. A registration record "should not be viewed as a proxy for study quality" (page_index 2); repository preregistration — our own route — is the weakest for bias control, its quality "very variable" on the meta-scientific evidence they cite, with biases towards positive findings that may persist even so; few if any journals check preregistration documents; and for published protocols, deviations should be declared and explained in the final report "although no formal mechanisms exist to ensure this" (page_index 6). An analyses register, an errata log, and recorded deviations do work that few if any venues require.

One scope caution: their stated reach is any field that studies hard-to-access human samples "or" involves arduous or expensive means of data collection, so our transfer runs on the second disjunct alone: expensive ground-truth annotation. Nothing here addresses computational or model-based research, and they regard open-ended, exploratory work, more concerned with hypothesis-generation than hypothesis-testing, as possibly not suited to preregistration.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

The closest thing in the preregistration literature to an operating manual for our own registration retrospective, and the source that supplies the missing vocabulary: a six-level, declarable ladder of prior data exposure, with compensating controls specified for the two lowest rungs (Levels 2 and 1). It is prior art of the enabling kind rather than the competing kind — it makes our route (OSF repository preregistration, on their account the weakest of the four routes for bias control) and our below-Level-6 data situation describable and defensible, while being blunt that few if any journals require editors and reviewers to check preregistration documents, that there is no guarantee a published study with a linked preregistration has been reviewed for adherence, and that a registration record certifies nothing about quality. Primary cluster: preregistration and open-science methodology; it reaches methods/metrics only where the countermeasure list (conservative statistical thresholds, blinded analyst, robustness and multiverse testing) bears on carrying a calibration from a gold standard to a deployment.
