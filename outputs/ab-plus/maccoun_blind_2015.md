## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

A three-page Nature Comment (8 October 2015, vol. 526, pp. 187-189), not a study. MacCoun (a Stanford psychologist and law professor) and Perlmutter (a Berkeley physicist who shared the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics) argue that blind analysis — widespread in areas of particle and nuclear physics by the early 2000s, and used in some clinical-trial protocols and forensic laboratories — should spread to the biological, psychological, and social sciences, where the concept "is hardly known": their assertion, uncited. The origin is an observation by "physicists including Richard Feynman" that new estimates of basic physical constants "were often closer to published values than would be expected given standard errors of measurement", because non-conforming results were "more often systematically discarded or revised". The remedy is "temporarily and judiciously removing data labels and altering data values to fight bias and error" (the cache splits "judi ciously") — one investigator or, more typically, a program perturbs values, labels, or both, while the rest of the team analyses in the dark.

The load-bearing passage is their treatment of preregistration as a rival remedy. They grant that it "offers some of the same benefits as blind analysis", then name the residual: analytical decisions and programming bugs that cannot be anticipated force choices made with the results in view. That is the diagnosis our adaptive-preregistration thread starts from, answered the other way round: we keep the analyst sighted and pre-commit to rules; they leave the rules open and hide the outcome. §D.9 can therefore set two named remedies against one problem.

Their bar is one this study never met: all analytical decisions complete and all code debugged before results are revealed, gated by prior agreement to publish whatever emerges, without further debugging or rethinking. Our threshold sweeps, match-radius choices, and carried-versus-oracle operating points were settled with F1 and MCC visible. One convention is retrofittable where blinding is not: post-unblinding analysis is permitted provided it is labelled "post-blind" — which maps onto the analyses register and deviations log.

Two caveats. First, no evidence: the piece evaluates nothing, and says the efficacy of blinding, preregistration, and other measures against confirmation bias "should be treated as empirical questions to be answered by future research" — citing a 2015 study of preregistration's effects, not of blinding. Second, the technical challenge they name but do not solve — obscure the result while preserving enough structure to debug — bites hardest on a detection pipeline whose metric is also its debugging signal. The cheapest technique that fits our comparison is cell scrambling: permute the condition labels, so which configuration won stays hidden while the pipeline's health stays inspectable. That retrofit is our inference — the piece never mentions machine learning or automated detection, and "model" occurs only as a statistical model's parameters. Its computational register is the blinding apparatus, not the science being blinded: a "suitable computer program" does the perturbing, and the advance the authors want is "off-the-shelf algorithms in standard analysis software to maintain the blinding".

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: preregistration and open-science methodology — the rival remedy to the paper's own apparatus, sharing its diagnosis (analytical choices that cannot be anticipated get made with the results in view) but answering it by hiding outcomes rather than by registering rules. It is advocacy, not evidence: a Comment with a strategy box and no efficacy evaluation, which is why it belongs in §D.9 as a standard the study did not meet rather than as a finding it must reconcile. Its usable residue is one retrospective convention — labelling analyses done after the results were seen — and one cheap unused technique, cell scrambling across the factorial conditions.

- **Rival remedies set aside** — MacCoun and Perlmutter name the rival remedies they set aside — preregistered analysis plans, cross-lab replication, the p-curve, adversarial collaboration, Bayesian analytical methods, and sensitivity analysis — granting that these “all have their place” but asserting that they “do not fully address the specific problem”. The verdict is asserted rather than demonstrated: one reference covers the whole list, and none of the six is evaluated. (page_index 1 · p.2 · §Common objections — ‘Can’t we avoid bias another way?’)
- **Blinding masks the display** — Blinding is a property of the display rather than of the data the computer holds: the authors state that the machine need not — and probably will not — be blinded to data values, because it is the presentation of results that masks the information. (page_index 1 · p.2 · §Who knows what)
- **Choosing a blinding technique** — Which blinding technique suits a study depends on the properties of the data, and the authors name four: the type of statistical distribution, the lower and upper bounds, whether values are discrete or continuous, and whether cases were randomly assigned to experimental conditions or passively observed. (page_index 1 · p.2 · §Who knows what)
- **When blinding is worth it** — The authors concede that blind analysis costs extra effort, and offer a triage rule for spending it: the analyses that at first seem most worth the trouble are those involving expensive data, high-stakes decisions, or topics especially prone to bias. They pair the rule with a cheap floor — blinding “could be as simple as asking a colleague down the hall to scramble labels”. (page_index 1 · p.2 · §Common objections — ‘Isn’t blind analysis too much hassle?’)
- **Panels of possible results** — The blinded presentation the authors favour is a panel of possible results among which the real ones may or may not be interspersed with decoys; such a presentation, they claim, typically triggers useful questions. The hedge is theirs — “a fertile approach”, not a recommendation. (page_index 1 · p.2 · §Who knows what)
- **Post-hoc implications preserved** — Answering the objection that blinding forfeits analyses which depend on seeing the result, the authors say the set of actual results should ideally sit among the panels of blinded ones, so researchers can weigh its further implications alongside those of the other, hypothetical results. (page_index 1 · p.2 · §Common objections — outcomes lost to blinding)
- **Compliance as social control** — On whether investigators will simply peek at the raw data, the authors grant that blind analysis “is not immune to fraud” and place the safeguard socially rather than technically: teams of investigators help enforce compliance, and where blinding is part of the culture graduate students and postdocs become its most effective guardians — the example given is flagging the risk when an adviser asks for a plot that might unblind the result by accident. (page_index 1 · p.2 · §Common objections — ‘Won’t people just peek at the raw data?’)
- **Unblinded safety monitor** — Where safety is at stake — the authors’ example is some clinical trials — it often makes sense to set up an unblinded safety monitor while the rest of the analytical team works in the dark. It is their precedent for exempting one role from a blind that covers everyone else. (page_index 1 · p.2 · §Common objections — ‘Isn’t blind analysis too much hassle?’)
- **Motivational barrier to adoption** — The second of the two challenges the authors see for wider adoption is motivational: creating incentives for investigators to take up a method that might make it harder for them to arrive at desirable — although possibly false — results. (page_index 2 · p.3 · §Making it happen — two challenges)
- **Unexpected patterns as benefit** — Among the side benefits claimed for blinding, the authors say it exposes the investigator to unexpected patterns that fuel both creativity and scrutiny of the theory and methodology. The claim carries a citation (their reference 10) rather than evidence presented in the piece. (page_index 2 · p.3 · §Making it happen — claimed benefits)
