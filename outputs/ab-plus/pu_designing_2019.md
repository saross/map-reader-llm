## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Pu, Zhu, Kay and Conrad treat preregistration as a user-centred design problem and ask the question preregistration advocacy usually skips: what do the people filling in these forms think they are for? The evidence is fourteen semi-structured interviews (N = 14) — word-of-mouth and open-science-workshop recruits, mostly academic psychologists, from eight US universities, PhD students through full professors, 43 minutes on average (min = 23, max = 57, sd = 11.2). Two coders built codes from the ground up in NVivo 12, but only the first two interviews were double-coded; the rest were coded once "for efficiency". It is a small, self-selecting sample of advocates and early adopters, and the Limitations say so.

Two findings carry the paper. Norms are uncertain — whether a power analysis belongs in a preregistration is openly disputed, and, the point that matters most here, there is no agreed norm for how a completed study should be compared against its registration or how deviations should be reported. And users hold several distinct purposes: the Findings section names four (delimiting flexibility, increasing transparency, designing and implementing experiments, learning and teaching), while the Conclusion narrows to "two main purposes" — the pair the Discussion argues are in tension. Transparency implies length; length makes the paper-versus-registration comparison harder; so reviewers "routinely fail" to do it. The authors speculate that formats encode purposes: AsPredicted asks for sample size, OSF also for sample-size rationale.

Three things transfer to this study. First, the checking gap is empirical, not hypothetical: authors check linearly and thoroughly, reviewers and editors check by heuristic query "when something looks out of place", and one participant did not bother at all, on the grounds that registrations are public and post-publication review exists. Formats "may verge on being write-only media". That is the standing case for our own errata log and recorded deviations: no external reader is reliably going to run the comparison. Second, their remedy is close prior art for that apparatus — an author-generated "declaration of match", worked question by question, describing any protocol deviations and submitted with the paper, whose generation "could even be partially or fully automated". It is proposed here, not built and not evaluated; "automated" occurs once in the whole paper, and nothing about machine learning or language models appears anywhere in the extracted text. Third, the "not for me" barrier is voiced from interview data rather than from argument — a participant who cannot register a correlation study on OSF because the templates are "set up really for experiments" — which is the complaint Gould et al. (2026) put in their title, and the same template mismatch is recognisable in a computational evaluation study.

A scope limit travels with all of it. The deviations these participants discuss are sample sizes, exclusions and analysis plans in experimental psychology, not carried operating points or model versions; the paper licenses the norm we appeal to, not the instance we register.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

The empirical floor under the preregistration apparatus: an interview study of how registrations actually get written and how thinly they are checked by anyone other than their own authors, against which this study's self-checking machinery (analyses register, errata log, recorded deviations) reads as a response to a documented failure rather than as ceremony. Its primary cluster is preregistration and open-science methodology; it complements the adaptive-preregistration thread by supplying interview evidence for the "preregistration is not for me" complaint that Gould et al. ### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Existing preregistration formats are designed primarily for authors to quickly input the desired information, but may verge on being write-only media: they are ill-suited to the checking task that reviewers, paper readers, and in some cases even authors themselves might perform."
- **Locator:** page_index 23 · p.24 · Discussion — Designing for different user roles explicitly
- **Paraphrase:** The authors diagnose registration templates as optimised for fast authoring and poorly suited to being read back against a finished paper — "write-only media" is their phrase, hedged with "may verge on". Their two recommendations follow directly: make registration content easier to query, and encourage wider coverage of it during review. The claim is a design reading of their interview findings, not a measured property of the templates.

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "a long, detailed, maximally transparent preregistration may verge on the length of a full paper. It should then not be surprising that paper reviewers routinely fail to thoroughly review manuscripts with attached preregistrations"
- **Locator:** page_index 21 · p.22 · Discussion — Purposes may undermine each other and result in conflicting designs
- **Paraphrase:** Pursuing transparency to its limit produces a registration the size of a paper, and the authors argue that is why attached registrations go under-reviewed — the source's next clause is that it "amounts to asking for two reviews in one from already-overworked reviewers", and its evidence is the methodologists' blog critiques plus participant reports, not a measurement of review behaviour. Their prescription is conditional: a user or journal whose primary purpose is delimiting flexibility "may wish" to adopt a shorter format.
- **Relevance:** §4 Methods — preregistration subsection and its Supplement S3 placement · Transparency-versus-checkability tension in registration length · **complicates**

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "We speculate that preregistration formats (typically web forms) implicitly reflect what their designers consider the primary purpose of preregistration to be, through the inclusion and exclusion of questions"
- **Locator:** page_index 20 · p.21 · Discussion — How preregistration purposes translate into formats
- **Paraphrase:** The authors offer — explicitly as speculation, having found the link between format differences and purpose "not immediately clear" — that a template's question set encodes whichever purpose its designers held. Their worked case is sample size: AsPredicted asks for the number but not its rationale, which suits delimiting flexibility, whereas OSF prompts for the rationale as well, which suits transparency. A participant offers a competing explanation for OSF's length — that the template grew out of a series of focus groups — so the purpose reading is one hypothesis among two.
