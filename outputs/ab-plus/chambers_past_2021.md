## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Chambers and Tzavella review the Registered Report (RR) at its first decade — origins, early evidence, variants, limitations, and a programme for the 2020s — from inside the initiative: C.D.C. wrote the Cortex launch editorial (ref. 36) and declares RR editorships at seven journals plus co-founding Peer Community in Registered Reports. Read as prior art for our preregistration retrospective, it does three jobs.

It fixes the boundary. RRs are “mostly a subset of the wider family of preregistration methods” distinguished by pre-study review and in-principle acceptance (IPA) regardless of results (page_index 1). Ours is a preregistration with neither, so the impact evidence assembled here is evidence about that format, not about registration as such: from an analysis of 296 hypotheses across 127 RRs, “60% of RRs report null results” (a percentage stated per RR though the analysis base is hypotheses), “approximately five times greater than the rate in regular articles”, which is not itself given, though the next sentence gives psychology-specific figures from a different study — 4% of regular articles versus 56% of RRs failing to confirm the first hypothesis (page_index 3); and 57% computational reproducibility among the 35 psychology RRs with open data and code, against 31% in a separate earlier analysis of regular articles, a comparison the authors themselves say “clearly show[s] room for improvement and require[s] substantially more data to be confirmed” (page_index 4). The authors name the confounds: RR authors may test riskier hypotheses, and the format may select for authors already diligent about reporting bias.

It sets the priority baseline for grain. Nothing in the extracted text uses “adaptive”, “interim”, “living”, “just-in-time”, “micro” or “modular” registration; “incremental” occurs exactly once, in Table 1, for “incremental registrations after stage 2 acceptance” (page_index 3). The nearest structural antecedent is the Future section's “rolling IPA”, where approval is re-granted as the protocol updates and reviewers act as virtual observers (page_index 9). Its motivation is ours — workflows in which one experiment's results drive the next within days — but it is a speculative proposal about peer review, with no implementation reported. Srivastava appears only as the 2012 Pottery Barn rule behind accountable replications (page_index 4, ref. 62), never the 2018 preprint behind the adaptive-preregistration thread.

It also claims the automation aspiration while leaving the means open. “Computationally generated RRs” argues the required precision may be unrealistic without tools, and calls for design templates, ScienceVerse-style software, machine-readable hypotheses (for confirmatory-hypothesis RRs) and a web-based RR generation tool (page_index 10). No mention of language models, machine learning or artificial intelligence occurs in the 14 extracted pages (verified by search).

Two constraints travel with any citation. RRs are “particularly well suited to hypothesis-driven studies and are not designed to improve the robustness or transparency of purely exploratory science” (page_index 7) — the partition a phased design must keep visible. And for secondary analyses of existing datasets the safeguards named against “risk of bias and analytical overfitting” are unobserved data, an unseen holdout, or a blinded analyst (page_index 7).

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Where these contingencies would be too numerous (or even impossible) to specify in advance, the inclusion of pilot data at stage 1 can be used to verify assumptions and narrow the range of possibilities."

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "RRs can be changed en route in consultation with the journal editor and (where changes are substantial) the reviewers, as long as all changes are transparently reported at stage 2."
- **Locator:** page_index 3 · p.4 · Table 1 — misconception “RRs require authors to prespecify a fixed plan that cannot be changed”
- **Paraphrase:** Changing a registered plan mid-study is permitted, subject to consultation with the editor (and reviewers for substantial changes) and to transparent reporting of every change at stage 2: the offence is undisclosed change, not change itself. The consultation partner here is a journal, which a registry-only preregistration does not have, so in our setting disclosure carries the whole load.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — deviations, amendments and the errata log · Deviation reporting as a norm rather than a defect · **supports**

### KP6

- **Paraphrase:** For RRs proposing secondary analyses of existing datasets, the safeguards named against “risk of bias and analytical overfitting” are: never having observed the data or the key variables, running the key analyses in an unseen holdout sample, or recruiting a blinded analyst. ### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "RRs are mostly a subset of the wider family of preregistration methods but with the additional features of pre-study review and IPA regardless of results"
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2 · Fig. 1 caption, panel b — “Registered reports vs preregistration: how to spot the difference”
- **Paraphrase:** Registered Reports sit inside the broader family of preregistration methods, marked off by two extra features: peer review before the study is run, and in-principle acceptance granted whatever the results turn out to be. The hedge “mostly” covers the minority of RR formats that do not require public preregistration. A study registered publicly without pre-study review is therefore preregistration but not a Registered Report.
