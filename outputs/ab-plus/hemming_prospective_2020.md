## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Hemming and colleagues write in the journal's voice, not as investigators: a two-page Trials editorial announcing a submission policy, with no data and no evaluation. Its subject is where and when a statistical analysis plan (SAP) gets published — precisely the question a phased preregistration raises. The premise is that the protocol carries the principal features of the analysis while "a separate detailed SAP containing sufficient information to support replication by an independent statistician may be needed", and that "pre-specification is important to reduce the occurrence of, and facilitate the detection of, bias particularly in relation to selective analysis and reporting". The content standard they lean on is not set here: it is the 2017 JAMA guidance (Gamble et al., their reference [1]), an EQUATOR resource. This editorial supplies the publication route, not the checklist.

The route is a four-option menu: a section inside the protocol, an appendix to it, an addendum submitted after the protocol is already published, or a stand-alone article "with its own author list and DOI". The last two have purchase here — sanctioned prior art, from a field with registries and external regulation, for the move a phased registration makes: issuing an analyses register after the original registration rather than pretending the whole analysis was foreseeable at design time. The editorial gives the reason: for complex trials, "such as those with adaptive designs or those using new methodologies", SAP development "might take longer than that of the protocol". It scopes that in the same paragraph: for many RCTs the plan is uncomplicated and can be defined in full when the protocol is written.

Two constraints travel with the licence. The flexibility is about format, not timing: authors are told these approaches "do not distract from relevant legislation and guidelines on when SAPs should be competed" [sic], so deferring the writing does not defer a deadline set outside the journal. And the flexibility is rationed by reviewer capacity: peer review "might be treated somewhat like a scarce resource", separate publication is deliberately "not made mandatory", and requiring a published protocol and a separate published SAP for every trial "may well be beyond the timely reviewing capacity" of the community. A study that multiplies registration artefacts inherits that cost argument.

Two housekeeping rules transfer cleanly. SAPs are "living documents" with two named amendment routes — an update to the protocol or SAP paper, or changes documented in the final report per CONSORT — and amendments should also be recorded on the relevant clinical trials registration site. And where a SAP is published within a protocol, authors are asked to make clear whether it is the preliminary or the final version. Those map onto a recorded-deviations log, an errata log, and versioned registration artefacts. The scope caveat is large: everything is pitched at RCTs and one journal's workflow, nothing addresses computational studies, machine learning, or automated checking, and the transfer is by analogy to a field whose registration deadlines are set by regulators rather than the researcher.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: preregistration and open-science methodology — a policy source rather than an evidence source. Hemming et al. legitimate publishing the statistical analysis plan separately from, and later than, the protocol, with an addendum to an already-published protocol, reviewed afresh but by whatever route the handling editor deems appropriate, among the four sanctioned formats, and name adaptive designs and new methodologies as examples of the complex trials where the plan may take longer to develop than the protocol. For the citing paper it does one job well — it shows that registering the analysis grain after the design grain is a formally sanctioned route in a leading trials journal, not an improvisation — and it attaches two conditions worth inheriting: the format choice does not move the timing obligation, and the review burden of extra registration artefacts is explicitly rationed.
