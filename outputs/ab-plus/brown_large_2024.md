## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Brown and colleagues treat repeated sampling as an axis for scaling compute at inference: draw many independent samples from one model at positive temperature, then let a verifier choose the final answer. They split the problem into coverage — the fraction of problems solved by any generated sample — and precision, the ability to identify a correct sample within the collection. That split is our architecture stated abstractly: repeated proposer passes buy coverage, greedy consensus and the adversarial verifier are our precision mechanism. It is among the closest published antecedents for consensus-over-passes, and its central warning lands on our aggregation rather than on our sampling.

Coverage scales, and it scales far. With 10,000 samples per problem on MATH (128 test problems), GSM8K (128), MiniF2F-MATH (130) and CodeContests (140), and 250 attempts per issue on SWE-bench Lite, coverage improves smoothly with the budget on all five tasks, often log-linearly, fitted with an exponentiated power law the authors hedge as "not as exact as training scaling laws". SWE-bench Lite moves from 15.9% at one attempt to 56% at 250, above the 43% single-attempt state of the art. The wider model sweep (Llama-3, Gemma, Pythia, 70M–70B) covers only MATH and CodeContests, where Pythia scores zero even at 10,000 samples.

The caution is sharper than the headline. Coverage converts fully into performance only where correct samples can be identified — automatically, by unit tests or a Lean proof checker, or by some other verification algorithm that keeps up with the budget. Where no automatic tool exists, the available selection methods plateau: on MATH with Llama-3-8B-Instruct coverage runs from 82.9% at 100 samples to 98.44% at 10,000, while the best of majority voting and reward-model selection moves only from 40.50% to 41.41% over the same range. Majority voting cannot do otherwise: a rare correct answer does not change the plurality. Read against our design, raising the pass count buys union recall a vote-threshold aggregator cannot cash in — the true detection appearing in one pass of ten is exactly what consensus discards — and our verifier, the model-based self-assessment these authors name as future work, is what must recover it.

Cost is the other transferable result: holding the agent framework constant, five DeepSeek attempts at $0.0072 each solve 29.62% of SWE-bench Lite issues for $10.8, against single attempts from GPT-4o (24.00%, $39, 3.6x) and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (26.70%, $51, 4.7x). The FLOPs analysis keeps this task-dependent: the 8B model obtains more coverage per FLOP than the 70B on MiniF2F, GSM8K and MATH, but not on CodeContests.

Finally, an epistemic bonus worth stealing. The one GSM8K problem for which Llama-3-70B-Instruct produced no correct sample in 10,000 attempts had an incorrect ground-truth answer; 11.3% of SWE-bench Lite problems had flaky test suites; 35 of the 122 CodeContests test problems with Python3 solutions have supplied "correct" solutions that fail their own tests. Exhaustive sampling can double as a label audit, and verifier noise becomes a first-class measurement problem — our curator-versus-adjudicated reference question in another idiom.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Consensus and test-time-compute antecedent of the most direct kind: this is the paper that systematically characterises repeated sampling as a compute axis across tasks, models and sample budgets, supplies the coverage-versus-precision decomposition our proposer-then-verifier stack instantiates, and gives the empirical shape — log-linear over several orders of magnitude, fitted as an exponentiated power law, S-curved as coverage saturates — of the pass-budget curve we implicitly bet on when we fix K. It builds on prior repeated-sampling work it cites, AlphaCode among it, rather than originating the technique. It is simultaneously a direct published warning against our primary aggregation: on the two tasks lacking automatic verifiers, consensus-style selection saturated at an order of 100 samples (the paper states the point inconsistently, from "before reaching 100" to "beyond several hundred") while coverage climbed past 95%, so extra passes pay only through a verifier able to pick a rare correct sample out of a mostly-wrong collection — our adversarial pass's job, in a domain with no unit test to fall back on. Secondary clusters are methods/metrics and cost/accuracy: its "precision" means the ability to identify correct samples rather than TP/(TP+FP), and its "coverage" is pass@k, so every borrowed number needs relabelling before it can sit beside our F1 and MCC.

### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "While success rates initially increase with the number of samples for all three methods, they plateau around 100 samples. Meanwhile, coverage continues to increase with the number of samples and eventually exceeds 95%. In the case of majority voting, this success rate saturation is intuitive, since the occurrence of rare, correct solutions does not affect the most common answer that majority voting chooses."
- **Locator:** page_index 8 · p.9 · §4.1 Common Verification Methods Don't Always Scale with the Sample Budget
- **Paraphrase:** All three selection methods tested — majority vote, reward-model best-of-N, and reward-model-weighted majority vote — stop improving at roughly 100 samples while coverage keeps climbing past 95%. For majority voting the authors give the mechanism, and it is structural rather than incidental: an answer that occurs rarely cannot change which answer is most common, so the rare-but-correct sample is invisible to the rule by construction. One hedge to carry: the paper states the plateau point loosely and inconsistently — "around 100 samples" here, "approximately 100 samples" in the introduction, "several hundred samples" in the abstract, and "saturate before reaching 100 samples" in the Figure 7 caption, the last of these putting the plateau below 100 where the abstract and introduction put it above — so treat it as an order of magnitude, not a threshold. Scope: GSM8K and MATH, 128 problems each, Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Llama-3-70B-Instruct.
- **Relevance:** §4 architecture — the limit of greedy consensus over passes · Consensus and test-time compute — why vote-based aggregation saturates · **complicates**

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "The effectiveness of repeated sampling is determined by two key properties: 1. Coverage: As the number of samples increases, what fraction of problems can we solve using any sample that was generated? 2. Precision: How often can we identify correct samples from our collection of generations?"
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2 · §1 Introduction — the two properties
- **Paraphrase:** Repeated sampling has two separable failure modes: the model may never generate a correct answer at all (coverage), or it may generate one the selection step fails to pick (precision). Both must hold for real-world performance. The authors note that with unlimited samples any model assigning non-zero probability to every sequence attains perfect coverage, so the practical questions are whether adequate coverage is reachable on a feasible budget and whether the correct samples can be identified once generated. Terminological warning for us: "precision" here is the identification problem, not the detection metric TP/(TP+FP).
- **Relevance:** §4 architecture — why the design is proposer-then-verifier · Consensus and test-time compute — the architecture frame · **supports**

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Model Cost per attempt (USD) Number of attempts Issues solved (%) Total cost (USD) Relative total cost DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct 0.0072 5 29.62 10.8 1x GPT-4o 0.13 1 24.00 39 3.6x Claude 3.5 Sonnet 0.17 1 26.70 51 4.7x"

- **Paraphrase:** Holding the Moatless Tools agent framework constant on SWE-bench Lite, five attempts from DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct at $0.0072 each solve 29.62% of issues for $10.8 in total, against a single Claude 3.5 Sonnet attempt (26.70%, $51, 4.7x the cost) and a single GPT-4o attempt (24.00%, $39, 3.6x). Sampling a weaker, cheaper model several times was both more accurate and several times cheaper here — on one benchmark, one agent framework, and at API prices the paper does not date. Two cautions: the table's own caption understates the result, calling it "the same issue solve-rate" when the sampled model in fact scores higher than both; and the FLOPs analysis on the same page shows the cheaper-model-more-samples verdict is task-dependent, the 70B model being "almost always" more cost-effective on CodeContests. A third caution matters for transfer: 29.62% is the fraction of issues solved by any of five attempts, a coverage figure that presupposes SWE-bench's unit-test harness to pick the successful attempt, set against single-attempt success rates — the cost advantage therefore travels only to settings that have something able to select among the attempts.

### KP5

- **Paraphrase:** Coverage against sample count is often log-linear and can be fitted with an exponentiated power law, offered as early evidence for inference-time scaling laws.
