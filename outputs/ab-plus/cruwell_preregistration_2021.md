## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Crüwell and Evans answer a complaint the citing paper knows well — that preregistration, built for straightforward experiments and standard inferential statistics, cannot constrain an intricate computational workflow — by building the missing template rather than arguing in the abstract. Their domain is cognitive modelling, specifically model application: using an existing model as a measurement instrument to test hypotheses about its parameters, one of four categories (development, evaluation, comparison, application) from Crüwell et al. 2019. The recipe is offered beyond their own field: the abstract promises "a guide for how preregistration templates may be developed in other diverse or intricate research contexts". Three moves. Enumerate the field's own researcher degrees of freedom, because restricting the generic ones "is not sufficient for a successful preregistration in more diverse contexts": they list nine (M1–M3, E1–E3, I1–I2, RC1), self-described as non-exhaustive. Extend an existing general template rather than invent one: OSF Prereg plus parts of Weston et al.'s secondary-data template, with four sections added (cognitive model, parameter estimation, robustness checks, contingency plans). Then demonstrate it: a preregistered diffusion-model replication on a 70-participant subset of an existing 133-participant dataset, returning only weak evidence for the groups not differing in distance from reward-rate optimality (BF = 1.182, and that in the direction of the fixed-trial group being closer to optimality) and strong evidence that both were too cautious (BF Time = 149, BF Trial = 31.664).

Four threads meet the citing paper. Analysis grain: their Future Directions propose "iterative preregistrations for different categories of cognitive modelling within a single study", used "either simultaneously or sequentially", each analysis constrained by its own registration — data-dependent registration stated in 2021, left unnamed, and conditional on category templates that did not then exist. Robustness: performing and reporting checks is itself a degree of freedom, and template section C exists so that checks and recovery simulations "are not performed and/or reported selectively" — binding on a paper reporting threshold sweeps, two match radii, and aggregation variants. Contingency plans: an issue → condition → plan triple fixed in advance, one worked example being a compute-budget overrun; their caveat: the section arrived through peer review and was never used in their own example application. Deviations: they disclose an omission from their own preregistration, hold that "a preregistration is not a prison", and cite Claesen et al. on how rarely deviations are reported.

Two limits travel. The template covers one of the four categories, and the interplay between them is unresolved — the remedy offered is combining templates that mostly do not exist. And nothing here touches automation: language models, machine learning, and automated authoring or checking appear nowhere in the extracted text (verified by search). Their constraint machinery is stylistic: a plan "specific ... precise ... and exhaustive", achieved by repeating the word "only" — exactly the labour an LLM-supported registration claim addresses. The extract stops at the reference list: appendices A and B (the full example preregistration and the template) are absent, leaving only the sections reproduced in §4.1.

## Key points (salience-ranked to the citing paper)

### KP1
- **Quote (verbatim):** "this system of iterative preregistrations for different categories of cognitive modelling within a single study provides the ideal balance between constraint and diversity, as researchers are free to investigate the data in as much detail as they wish while each analysis performed is constrained"
- **Locator:** page_index 18 · p.19 · §6.2 Future directions
- **Paraphrase:** They propose registering each analysis within a single multi-part study separately, so that exploration stays free while every analysis actually performed is constrained by its own registration — the analysis-grain idea, stated in 2021 and given no name. Two scope limits travel with it. The units are their four modelling categories rather than arbitrary analyses; and the proposal is conditional, applying "with specific templates for each category" and offered "either simultaneously or sequentially", while §6.1 concedes that the combination scenario "is not currently possible, as there is no preregistration template for model evaluation ... or guidelines for how to combine these templates". The sequential branch is the data-dependent one: their worked hypothetical registers a model comparison, conducts it, and only then registers a model evaluation of each of the compared models.

### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "In order to appropriately apply preregistration to research contexts that are not purely experimental, it is important to identify the unique researcher degrees of freedom within the area of research that the preregistration should ideally constrain."
- **Locator:** page_index 6 · p.7 · §3 Researcher degrees of freedom in model application
- **Paraphrase:** The first step in extending preregistration to a non-experimental context is to enumerate that context's own researcher degrees of freedom, because the generic ones are not the binding constraint — the same page states that restricting them "is not sufficient for a successful preregistration in more diverse contexts". Their own enumeration runs to nine items (M1–M3, E1–E3, I1–I2, RC1); they describe it as non-exhaustive, devised qualitatively by reviewing the literature and drawing on their research experience, and note that the generic degrees of freedom still apply on top.

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "if researchers were to only perform or report the robustness checks that were successful in showing their results to be robust, then readers would likely become overconfident in the results of the study"
- **Locator:** page_index 9 · p.10 · §3.1.6 Robustness checks
- **Paraphrase:** Selectively performing or reporting only the robustness checks that came out favourably would leave readers over-confident, since the findings would appear robust to everything reported. They are answering the argument that robustness checks are a superior alternative to preregistration, and their reply is to keep the checks but register them: template section C "ensures that robustness checks and parameter recovery simulations are not performed and/or reported selectively" (page_index 12), and asks in advance how their results will be interpreted, including when they show a lack of robustness.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — preregistration in practice: deviations and the retrospective · Robustness checks and sensitivity sweeps as a registrable degree of freedom · **supports**

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Issue: The proposed methods end up taking more time than planned. Condition: Executing the originally preregistered plans would take a month of computing time. Contingency plan: A preregistered version that is simpler, such as a reduced version of the model, or a simpler method of performing inference."
- **Locator:** page_index 12 · p.13 · §4.1 Preregistration template, section D — Contingency Plans (template item 36)
- **Paraphrase:** One of their three worked contingency examples is a compute-budget overrun: the issue, the condition that triggers the fallback, and the simpler pre-registered alternative are all fixed in advance. That triple is the shape they prescribe for the section generally — the broader issue, "the more specific conditions that will be assessed to determine whether the contingency plan is used or not", and the plan itself — which is registered flexibility under another name, at template-item granularity. Their own caveat is load-bearing: section D was added during peer review at a reviewer's suggestion, and "As this section was only added during the review process, we were not able to include it in the example application preregistration" (page_index 14). They also warn that a preregistration "can not (and should not) cover every eventuality".

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Even after carefully going over the example application preregistration several times, we still accidentally omitted a minor detail about the process of parameter estimation: that we used a migration algorithm to assist convergence"
- **Locator:** page_index 17 · p.18 · §6 General discussion
- **Paraphrase:** They disclose a specific omission from their own preregistration — an undocumented migration algorithm used to assist convergence — found despite several careful passes over the document, and present the imperfection as evidence for three claims: that "a preregistration is not a prison", that well-motivated and transparently reported changes are always possible, and that writing preregistrations is a learning process that improves with time. They add that the volume of available degrees of freedom, and so the detail the preregistration demanded, "was at times surprising", and they cite Claesen et al. both for examples of transparent deviation reporting and for a critical view of how rarely deviations are in fact reported.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — preregistration in practice: deviations and the retrospective · Deviation reporting and the imperfect first attempt · **supports**

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "the additional transparency of openly stating all preexisting knowledge of the specific secondary datasets that one plans to analyse will hopefully provide similar added credibility"
- **Locator:** page_index 8 · p.9 · §3.1.4 Secondary data analysis
- **Paraphrase:** Where a study reuses existing data, they require the researcher to declare up front everything already known about the specific datasets to be analysed, as a substitute for the credibility that ordinarily comes from registering before collection — hedged as what will "hopefully" provide it. They are candid that the substitute is weaker: the same subsection notes that preregistering secondary data analyses "makes it particularly difficult to verify whether the preregistration was completed before the researchers had started exploring the data", and that a "postregistration" could be disguised as one. Their template handles it structurally, replacing the "Sampling Plan", "Design Plan" and "Variables" sections with a "Data Description for Preexisting Data" section adapted from Weston et al.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — preregistration in practice: deviations and the retrospective · Preregistration over pre-existing data; declaring prior knowledge of the corpus · **complicates**

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "the classical power analysis used within purely experimental contexts could potentially be replaced with a parameter recovery study that matches the preregistered experimental design, model, and model parameterization"
- **Locator:** page_index 9 · p.10 · §3.1.5 Outliers and power analysis
- **Paraphrase:** When the analysis is not a significance test, the power-analysis slot in a generic template should be filled by a domain-appropriate adequacy check — for them a parameter recovery study run against the registered design, model, and parameterisation, on the ground that models with poor recovery can produce spurious conclusions. The recommendation is doubly hedged: "could potentially", and they decline to specify recovery standards as "beyond the scope of the current article", asking only that researchers "strongly consider" performing some recovery and including it in the preregistration. Within the main text they report none for their own example application (appendix A, the full preregistration, is not reproduced in the extract), where they instead aimed to recruit more than 30 participants per group and used Bayes factors, on the stated ground that power analyses "are not currently possible" for intricate cognitive models.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — preregistration in practice: deviations and the retrospective · What fills the power-analysis slot when the analysis is not a significance test · **extends**

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "we believe that the goal of preregistration is not to prevent dishonesty or outright fraud, but to help researchers avoid fooling themselves into believing that their post hoc explanations and explorations were a priori predictions and decisions"
- **Locator:** page_index 8 · p.9
- **Why:** An epigraph for D.9's retrospective, and a yardstick: it fixes what the registration was supposed to buy — protection against self-deception, not against fraud — which is the standard a candid deviations audit should be measured against rather than amendment count. It gains force from its position in the source: they say it while conceding that a preregistration over pre-existing data cannot be verified as genuinely prior, and they later admit their own preregistration was incomplete.
