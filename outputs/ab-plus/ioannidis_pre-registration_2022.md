## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Ioannidis's Mathematical Biosciences piece is a Perspective — "the current commentary", in its own words — not a study: it asks whether preregistration, a protocol time-stamped and deposited before the work is performed, can be extended to mathematical modelling, and answers with conditions rather than a template. It presents no new data; two tables and two boxes carry the argument. Table 1 pairs seven conditions that favour preregistration against their opposites, the last of which is "Existing data are used". Our study straddles that table, which is what makes it useful in both directions.

Three moves land on our apparatus. First, and most consequential for priority: Ioannidis already contemplates registration at the grain of the individual step — a modeller depositing "the plans for the next step and then report on what was found", which he calls "small bites of pre-registration" — four years before the adaptive-preregistration thread the paper cites (Gould et al. 2026), though hedged as something that "may sometimes be feasible and even helpful", with no worked instance. The word "adaptive" appears nowhere in the extracted text (verified by search; the only cognate is "Re-adaptation", of evaluation metrics, page index 5), so this is priority over the practice, not over the name. Second, he licenses the split our design makes between exploratory method search and fixed evaluation: models "can go wild in playing with different options" provided the performance criteria are pre-specified, explicit, and unambiguous — though he offers this as an addition, not an alternative: one paragraph earlier he suggests registering "all the wild variety of approaches that may be tested", which for such work "may be even more useful". Third, §8 makes metric pre-specification a defence against "shifting the goalposts", and pairs rules for judging success with rules for deciding when recalibration is needed — our carried-versus-oracle operating points in miniature.

He also supplies the sharpest objection to our own registration. Registering when existing information is already accessible "probably adds less trust", because no reader can rule out that iterations and explorations happened first and that the registered plan encodes "the seemingly best-performing models". That is our situation: the maps and the gold standard existed, and pilot work preceded registration. §4 leaves the objection standing; the answer comes elsewhere, in §2's deviation record — with a registered protocol on file, rationales attach to each departure and readers judge whether they were justified.

Two limits travel with the source. Its worked terrain is biomedical and health-sciences modelling, reaching only as far as simulation research and computational cognitive psychology; and where computational tools "e.g. artificial intelligence or even plain predictive models" are concerned he routes trust through randomised trials of the technology rather than a model-protocol template, so nothing here is a ready-made instrument for a VLM evaluation. He also concedes there is "no large-scale empirical evidence" that preregistration improves research quality or efficiency. No mention of language models, or of automated authoring or checking, appears anywhere in the extracted text (verified by search).

### KP1
- **Quote (verbatim):** "a researcher doing iterative modeling with multiple steps may still deposit (for immediate or later public release) the plans for the next step and then report on what was found. This process of small bites of pre-registration is one form on open live streaming of research"
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2 · §3 Rationale for pre-registration in different settings and study designs
- **Paraphrase:** A modeller working through a sequence of steps, each contingent on the last, can still register the plan for the next step alone and then report what that step found; Ioannidis names this "small bites of pre-registration" and likens it to open live streaming of research. It is offered as a possibility within a commentary — the preceding sentence hedges that registering each step "may sometimes be feasible and even helpful" — with no worked instance, template, or infrastructure attached, and he contrasts it with the other extreme, registration of a whole study or a sequence of studies. A passage on page indices 4–5, immediately after the Box 2 forecasting example, is the nearest the source comes to a registration that updates as the work proceeds, and it strengthens the priority reading: preregistration "does not necessarily mean that the team should be forced to keep exactly the same evaluation procedure also in the future", "Re-adaptation of the evaluation metrics to include more meaningful ones can be done", and the revised metrics may then be registered afresh as a new protocol.

### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "models can go wild in playing with different options (whichever they deem best), but then they need to make a forecast and the accuracy of that forecast will be assessed with a pre-specified specific set of criteria"
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2 · §3 Rationale for pre-registration in different settings and study designs
- **Paraphrase:** Where the research tools themselves are exploratory, Ioannidis adds fixed evaluation as a further option rather than a substitute for registering the method — pre-registering outcome evaluation is "also an excellent choice even when the research tools are highly exploratory": modellers may try whatever options they judge best, provided the outcome is committed to in advance and scored against criteria settled beforehand. His worked case is forecasting accuracy, and the condition he attaches is that the criteria be pre-specified and agreed in advance, with the following sentences insisting they be explicit and unambiguous. The preceding paragraph is a caveat this point should carry: for wildly exploratory work he allows that "one may try to pre-register all the wild variety of approaches that may be tested", and that doing so "may be even more useful in these circumstances, since selective reporting can be more potent as a source of bias otherwise" — the fallback being that such research "should then be clearly stated to have been exploratory".
- **Relevance:** §2 study design / §D.9 — why metrics were fixed while prompts were not · Pre-specified evaluation criteria under exploratory method search · **supports**

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Pre-registration probably adds less trust to the research work, when existing information is accessible in ways that one cannot guarantee that multiple iterations and explorations have not already happened before one decides to register a protocol—which may reflect the seemingly best-performing models."
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2 · §4 Registration with retrospective and prospective elements
- **Paraphrase:** Registering a protocol for work that draws on data already available buys less trust than registering before collection, because a reader cannot rule out that the registered plan is the survivor of iterations and explorations already run, and so may encode whichever models had already looked best. The claim is hedged ("probably adds less trust"), and Ioannidis still endorses registration in this situation — the same section says registration "can be done even for studies that depend on data and materials that are already available". He is grading trust, not withholding it.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — limits of our own registration; §3 corpus and gold standard · Ground-truth epistemics: registration against pre-existing data and prior exploration · **complicates**

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "It would be beneficial to pre-specify what tools will be used. Agreement within a field about metrics of success, and uniformity of using such metrics not just for single projects but for the whole field can help ensure that distinct steps of progress are being made, rather than just spurious progress is documented because of shifting the goalposts."
- **Locator:** page_index 3 · p.4 · §8 Complements to pre-registration — "Pre-specified agreed rules for judging success and/or need for recalibration"
- **Paraphrase:** Because model performance can be scored by many different metrics, Ioannidis argues for naming the metrics in advance, and for a field agreeing on them across projects rather than per study, so that apparent progress is not manufactured by moving the criterion once the result is in. The passage's own examples are model-fit tools (Bayesian information criteria, other parsimony-corrected indicators), and its heading pairs pre-specified rules for judging success with pre-specified rules for deciding when recalibration is needed.
- **Relevance:** §4 metrics — F1 and MCC, tile-level and object-level at 20 m / 50 m · Metric hygiene: pre-specification against post hoc metric choice · **supports**

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "when a pre-registration record exists, investigators may explain the rationale behind each of the deviations. Therefore, other researchers and readers can judge whether these deviations were justified or not"
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2 · §2 Pre-registration: definitions
- **Paraphrase:** The working value of preregistration on this account is not that plans go unchanged but that changes become inspectable: with a registered record on file, investigators can attach a rationale to each departure, and readers can decide for themselves whether the departures were warranted and whether trust in the work should survive them. The surrounding passage grades departures explicitly — many trivial, others major but well-justified, and some cases where "overt moving of the goalposts may be entirely inappropriate".
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — recorded deviations and errata log · Deviation reporting as the working purpose of preregistration · **supports**

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "In the absence of clear, unambiguous rules, definitions, and outcomes, almost any forecast may self-promote itself post hoc to have been accurate"
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2
- **Why:** An epigraph for why the evaluation protocol was fixed before any detection run: without explicit rules, definitions, and outcomes settled in advance, a system's output can be talked into looking accurate after the fact. Ioannidis's case is epidemic forecasting — Box 1 works it through on COVID-19 mortality — but the mechanism, choosing the scoring criterion once the result is in, is exactly what pre-specified F1, MCC, and match radii are meant to foreclose.
