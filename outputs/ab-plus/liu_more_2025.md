## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Liu and colleagues ask whether test-time reasoning buys perception, and answer no. Comparing eight reasoning-tuned multimodal models against their non-reasoning Qwen2.5-VL backbones, on five hallucination benchmarks, they find hallucination uniformly higher for the reasoning variants; the deficit holds at both 3B and 7B, which the authors read as a property of the reasoning paradigm rather than of scale. An attention analysis supplies the mechanism: reasoning models put less attention on visual tokens and more on instruction tokens, and the shift intensifies as the chain lengthens. This is the most consequential external result for our thinking-mode settings, because mound spotting shares the property that defines the paper's perception tasks — the answer is in the image, not in the reasoning — even though the paper measured that property on natural-image VQA benchmarks rather than on symbol detection in a scanned map.

Two scoping conditions must travel with it. First, 'reasoning model' in Sections 2 and 3 means a separately fine-tuned checkpoint (RL-only or SFT+RL), not a decode-time thinking budget on fixed weights; the within-model evidence is Section 4 (and, for attention, Figure 6's normal-thinking versus overthinking comparison), where chain length is controlled by token budget forcing, test-time scaling, and a latent-steering parameter α ∈ [-0.15, 0.15], applied to five reasoning models over six benchmarks. Second, the authors state that every model evaluated is built on the Qwen2.5-VL backbone, so transfer to the Gemini family is our assumption, not one they license.

Section 4 is the part we can act on. The effect of length is non-monotonic — performance rises then falls — and the optimum is task-dependent: MathVista rewards longer chains, the hallucination-oriented MMHalu does best at shorter or moderate lengths, and Figure 7's control windows were set asymmetrically to match ([0–600] tokens for reasoning, [0–300] for hallucination). The floor is real too: the Zerothink condition, which keeps the reasoning structure but empties it of substantive content, drops performance on both reasoning and perception benchmarks below normal reasoning lengths, so emptying a reasoning model's chain of content is worse than reasoning moderately. That is not a verdict on a thinking-off setting: Zerothink is a degenerate condition inside a reasoning-tuned checkpoint, whereas Section 2.2 reports the non-reasoning Qwen2.5-VL baseline scoring highest of all across four perception benchmarks, ahead of both RL-only and SFT+RL.

The metric lesson imports directly. Because reasoning and perception peak at different lengths, the authors argue that single-point metrics and simple averages mislead, and propose RH-AUC — the trapezoidal area under the reasoning/hallucination balance curve across lengths, with both axes min-max normalised — alongside RH-Bench, 1,000 samples split 500 reasoning and 500 perception, whose open-ended perception items are scored 0 to 6 by GPT-4o with anything below 3 counted as a hallucination. Reported RH-AUC runs from 0.46 to 0.63. The structural claim survives even if the metric does not: one F1 or MCC per thinking mode is a single-point sample of a curve, and which mode wins can depend on where the sample was taken.

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "under the overthinking mode, attention to visual tokens significantly decreases, while attention to instruction tokens intensifies. This pattern indicates that longer reasoning chains cause the model to increasingly rely on linguistic cues rather than grounded visual evidence."
- **Locator:** page_index 4 · p.5 · §3.2 Visual Focus Declines with Longer Reasoning Chains
- **Paraphrase:** The mechanism on offer: as chains lengthen — in the authors' 'overthinking' condition, induced by latent-state steering — attention mass moves off visual tokens and onto instruction tokens, which the authors read as increasing reliance on linguistic priors over grounded visual evidence.
