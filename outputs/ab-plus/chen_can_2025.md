## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Chen and colleagues ask whether multimodal large language models (MLLMs) genuinely learn in context from multimodal demonstrations, or whether their text is doing all the work. The study manipulates two factors — demonstration content and demonstration selection — across seven OpenFlamingo and IDEFICS models from 3B to 9B, on VQAv2 and OK-VQA (visual question answering), GQA (visual reasoning), and MSCOCO (captioning), at 4, 8, 16, and 32 shots, with the selection-experiment tables averaged over five evaluation seeds and reported with standard deviations, not confidence intervals.

The content result is blunt. Removing the images from the demonstrations, or replacing them with blank images (all pixel values set to 255), leaves in-context learning (ICL) performance largely intact; corrupting the demonstration text does not. Replacing demo questions at random, or the answers with random English words, degrades performance sharply — the latter to nearly zero — whereas substituting a different but valid answer to the same question costs little, which the authors read as the correct label space and semantics mattering more than images. The query image is different: remove it and visual question answering falls by up to 50 per cent, image captioning by nearly 100 per cent. That asymmetry — the image under analysis carries the signal, the exemplar images may not — is the axis our text-versus-image modality comparison runs along.

The proposed mechanism is architectural, and that caveat must travel with the finding. Flamingo-style masked cross-attention lets each text token attend only to the last preceding image, so query text never sees the demonstration images directly; masking the demonstration visual embeddings leaves hidden states around 90 per cent similar, whereas masking the query image does not. The models we use are not from this family. The authors report that Qwen-VL, which does not use this structure, shows "similar patterns" in a supplementary table absent from our text cache, but call further investigation future work — so the finding reaches a mound-detection prompt as a hypothesis worth testing cheaply, not a result to cite forward.

The selection half is more usable for an annotation budget. Retrieval by image similarity (RICES) beats random selection even though the retrieved images are near-inert, because visually similar demos carry query-relevant text; retrieval on text alone underperforms, falling below random selection on VQAv2. Their MMICES method filters K candidates by image similarity, then ranks N by text. The budget claim is worth borrowing: on GQA with OF-9B, four-shot MMICES beats thirty-two-shot random selection, and on IDEFICS-9B every sixteen-shot MMICES cell beats both thirty-two-shot baselines — better-chosen exemplars buy more than more exemplars. It does not win everywhere: on VQAv2 with OF-9B, MMICES never beats RICES. And more shots is not reliably better — on OF-4B/VQAv2 all three strategies decline steadily from four shots to thirty-two. Number hygiene is imperfect: three figures in the §5.2 prose disagree with the tables they summarise, and an improvement quoted as "around 10%" is relative, not percentage points (3.68 points, 40.54% to 44.22% on GQA with IDEFICS-9B).

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: few-shot / in-context learning and annotation budget. It is a directly relevant warning that multimodal few-shot exemplars may do their work through their text rather than their pictures — directly relevant if we spend annotation budget curating example map crops — but the evidence base is predominantly Flamingo-family masked cross-attention models on natural-image benchmarks (the non-Flamingo checks are a supplementary Qwen-VL table the authors report as consistent and MMICL in the selection results), not the proprietary models we use and not scanned cartography, so it enters our argument as a scoped hypothesis and a design heuristic rather than a transferable result. Its most usable half is the selection finding: image similarity is a good retrieval signal even where image content is inert, and better-selected exemplars beat more exemplars, which is exactly the trade our cost/accuracy framing needs.
