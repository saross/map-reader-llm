## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Huang and colleagues (Google DeepMind and the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign) ask whether a large language model can repair its own reasoning by reviewing it, and answer no. The pivotal definition is intrinsic self-correction: revision “based solely on its inherent capabilities, without the crutch of external feedback” — the setting that matters operationally, because high-quality external feedback is often unavailable. Under it, accuracy falls for every model on every benchmark. GPT-3.5 on GSM8K goes 75.9 → 75.1 → 74.7 as calls rise from 1 to 3 to 5; on CommonSenseQA 75.8 → 38.1 → 41.8; GPT-4 on GSM8K goes 95.5 → 91.5 → 89.0; Llama-2-70b-chat on GSM8K falls 62.0 → 43.5 → 36.5. Three feedback-prompt variants (Tables 5–6, on GPT-4-Turbo and Llama-2) never reverse the direction. The mechanism they propose is offered as an “Intuitive Explanation” rather than a measured one: for a well-aligned model with a thoughtfully designed initial prompt, the initial response should already be optimal relative to that prompt and decoding algorithm, so a review prompt may bias it away. The empirical half is firm: the models cannot properly judge the correctness of their own reasoning.

Three evaluation critiques do most of the work for us. First, oracle labels: the gains reported by Kim et al. (2023) and Shinn et al. (2023) come from ground-truth answer correctness used to decide when to stop revising, and vanish once the labels are withdrawn. Second, budget matching: replicating multi-agent debate on the full GSM8K test set with gpt-3.5-turbo-0301, 3 agents and 2 rounds, debate reaches 83.2 at 6 responses and 83.0 at 9, where plain self-consistency reaches 85.3 and 88.2 — so debate is better read as an expensive route to consistency than as critique. Third, prompt design: on Madaan et al.’s Constrained Generation task the self-correction gain disappears once the requirement hidden in the feedback prompt is moved into the initial one.

For our architecture the reading is direct. Consensus over repeated passes is what their evidence favours at matched budget — though they use self-consistency as the baseline a correction method must beat rather than an architecture they recommend, and their closing note hopes for models needing no multiple generations at all. A verifier stage therefore has to be scored against a budget-matched consensus baseline: the honest counterfactual for its calls is more proposer passes. Their internal/external taxonomy puts other models, tools, and knowledge sources outside intrinsic self-correction, which licenses an independent verifier — but a differently prompted pass of the same model family over the same image sits uncomfortably close to the intrinsic side, and the difference (a bounded accept/reject judgement re-grounded in the image, not a re-derivation of a chain) is ours to defend rather than assume. Their oracle-label critique is our carried-versus-oracle operating point in another vocabulary. One caveat travels with all of it: the finding is scoped to reasoning benchmarks in text, the authors allow that self-correction can work on other properties, and our task is largely perceptual.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Consensus / test-time-compute antecedent, and the strongest available warrant for our architectural split between a sampled proposer and an independent verifier: Huang et al. show that a model asked to review its own reasoning without external feedback makes matters worse, and that the gains attributed to multi-agent critique are really the gains of self-consistency — plain majority voting scores higher at the same number of responses. It sits with us as licence and constraint at once — licence, because their own remedy is external feedback, which their taxonomy extends to other models and tools; constraint, because a second pass of the same model family over the same image is not obviously external in their sense, and because their three evaluation critiques (budget-matched baselines, oracle labels, prompt-design confounds) name exactly the traps our verifier ablation and our carried-versus-oracle operating points have to avoid. The result is scoped to text reasoning benchmarks, so its transfer to a perceptual symbol-detection task is an argument we have to make rather than one we can borrow.

### KP2

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "for self-consistency with an equivalent number of responses, multi-agent debate significantly underperforms simple self-consistency using majority voting"

The authors read debate as a way of achieving consistency across generations rather than as critique.

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "the self-correction improvement claimed by some existing work stems from the sub-optimal prompt for generating initial responses, where self-correction corrects these responses with more informative instructions about the initial task in the feedback prompt"

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "If an LLM possesses the ability to self-correct, why doesn't it simply offer the correct answer in its initial attempt?"
- **Locator:** page_index 0 · p.1
- **Why:** The paradox in one line, and the cleanest opening for our justification of an independent verifier rather than a self-review pass. Use it with its scope attached: the authors put the question to reasoning tasks, and their own answer, offered as an intuitive explanation — that for a well-aligned model the initial response should already be optimal relative to its prompt, so review may bias it away — is an argument about revising a single response, not about aggregating over repeated samples, which their own comparison treats as the stronger option. Our design answers the question differently: the consensus stage exploits variation across passes rather than asking one pass to think again.

- **Damage scales with model strength** — The paper's headline generalisation flattens a wide spread across model tiers. Table 4 gives GPT-4-Turbo on GSM8K at 91.5 for a single call, 88.0 after one round of self-correction and 90.0 after two, and on CommonSenseQA 84.0, 81.5 and 83.0; Llama-2-70b-chat over the same three points goes 62.0, 43.5 and 36.5 on GSM8K and 64.0, 37.5 and 36.5 on CommonSenseQA (both models are evaluated on 200 randomly sampled questions per dataset, per the setup section). After two rounds the strongest model has lost 1.5 and 1.0 points; the weakest has lost 25.5 and 27.5, about two-fifths of its accuracy in relative terms. Intrinsic self-correction is near-neutral for a strong model and catastrophic for a weak one, which makes the finding an argument about which tier a self-review design could ever be safe on rather than a blanket prohibition. (page_index 4 · p.5 · Table 4 — intrinsic self-correction, GPT-4-Turbo and Llama-2)
- **Isolate selection from correction** — The authors set an explicit design condition on any claim that a multi-call architecture corrects reasoning: the benefit of merely selecting among multiple generations must be excluded before the remainder can be attributed to critique. An ablation that adds a review stage without holding the number of generations, and the selection over them, fixed cannot tell the two apart. (page_index 6 · p.7 · §4 Multi-Agent Debate Does Not Outperform Self-Consistency)
- **Prompt bias, not reasoning failure** — The authors' account of the CommonSenseQA collapse is a prompt-bias mechanism rather than a reasoning failure: the false options are plausible enough to be tempting, and a prompt inviting the model to find fault pushes it towards one of them, producing a high correct-to-incorrect rate. They give this as the primary reason for the collapse, while hedging the mechanism itself — the self-correction prompt “might bias” the model. (page_index 3 · p.4 · §3.3 Why does the performance not increase, but instead decrease? — empirical analysis)
- **Where the accuracy is lost** — Figure 1's answer-change breakdown for GPT-3.5 separates two very different failures. On GSM8K the model keeps its first answer 74.7 per cent of the time and the harm-benefit margin among the changes is narrow: 8.8 per cent of all items go from correct to incorrect against 7.6 per cent repaired, with 8.9 per cent changed but still wrong. On CommonSenseQA it keeps its answer only 42.8 per cent of the time and 39.8 per cent of all items go correct to incorrect against 5.8 per cent repaired. (Each panel lists, in the figure's legend order, No Change, Incorrect ⇒ Incorrect, Incorrect ⇒ Correct, Correct ⇒ Incorrect.) The aggregate loss is dominated by the multi-choice benchmark, not spread evenly across the two. (page_index 5 · p.6 · Figure 1 — changes in answers after two rounds of self-correction)
- **What counts as workable external feedback** — Every remedy the authors endorse supplies a signal from outside the model's own reasoning: a code executor, which when unit tests specify the intended behaviour they describe as a perfect verifier, together with the error messages it returns; external tools such as search engines and calculators; or a verifier or critique model trained on a high-quality dataset. Their positive cases are grounded or separately trained judges, never a further prompt to the same model. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §6 Conclusion and Discussion — Leveraging external feedback for correction)
- **Size of the withdrawn oracle gains** — The oracle-label gains the paper withdraws are largest on exactly the benchmark where intrinsic self-correction later does most damage. With ground-truth labels deciding when to stop revising, GPT-3.5 goes 75.9 to 84.3 on GSM8K, 75.8 to 89.7 on CommonSenseQA and 26.0 to 29.0 on HotpotQA; GPT-4 goes 95.5 to 97.5, 82.0 to 85.5 and 49.0 to 59.0. GPT-3.5's 13.9-point oracle gain on CommonSenseQA is on the same benchmark whose accuracy falls to 38.1 in Table 3 once the labels are removed, so the label is doing all the work and then some. (page_index 3 · p.4 · Table 2 — reasoning benchmarks with oracle labels)

- **Two GSM8K baselines, two kernels** — The multi-agent-debate replication runs on a different model kernel from the self-correction experiments: gpt-3.5-turbo-0301, chosen to match Du et al.'s implementation exactly (same prompt, three agents, two rounds), on the complete GSM8K test set rather than the 100 examples Du et al. used. That kernel change is why standard prompting on GSM8K reads 76.7 in Table 7 on this page but 75.9 in Table 3, where the setup names gpt-3.5-turbo-0613: the 0.8-point gap is a kernel difference, not a finding. Any table placing the self-correction and debate results side by side has to carry the kernel with each baseline. (page_index 6 · p.7 · §4 Multi-Agent Debate Does Not Outperform Self-Consistency — replication setup)
- **A well-chosen review prompt is nearly free** — Three feedback prompts were tried on GPT-4-Turbo, and the best of them makes self-review close to free rather than beneficial: “Verify whether your answer is correct, and provide an explanation” returns 91.0 on GSM8K at both rounds against a 91.5 single-call baseline, and 81.5 then 83.5 on CommonSenseQA against 84.0. No variant exceeds the baseline in any cell, so the paper's direction holds; but the cost on a strong model spans 0.5 to 9.5 points depending on the wording, so the defensible reading here is “no gain” rather than “large loss”. (page_index 4 · p.5 · Table 5 — GPT-4-Turbo with different feedback prompts)
- **Round cap and two decoding regimes** — The protocol caps self-correction at a maximum of two rounds throughout, and deliberately spans two decoding regimes: temperature 1 for GPT-3.5-Turbo and GPT-4, temperature 0 for GPT-4-Turbo and Llama-2. The models that degrade are therefore split across sampled and greedy decoding, and the reported accuracies for the temperature-1 models are single draws from a stochastic decoder rather than deterministic values. (page_index 2 · p.3 · §3.1 Experimental Setup — Test Models and Setup)

(page_index 4 · p.5 · §3.3 footnote 1)
- **The one cell that does not fall** — The “all models, all benchmarks” generalisation holds after two rounds but not cell by cell after one. In Table 3 GPT-4 on HotpotQA is unchanged at 49.0 after the first round of self-correction and falls to 43.0 only at the second, while GPT-3.5's HotpotQA column reads 26.0, 25.0 and 25.0. Every other cell is already below its baseline at round 1. A reviewer checking Table 3 cell by cell will find that one flat cell, so the generalisation is safest stated of the two-round result. (page_index 3 · p.4 · Table 3 — reasoning benchmarks with intrinsic self-correction)
