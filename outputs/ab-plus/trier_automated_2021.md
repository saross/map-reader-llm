## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Trier, Reksten and Løseth apply Faster R-CNN to airborne laser scanning (ALS) data in southern Norway, detecting Iron Age grave mounds, pitfall traps and charcoal kilns in local relief model (LRM) visualisations of a 0.25 m digital terrain model. The detector is off the shelf: ImageNet-pre-trained VGG16 weights, fine-tuned on 18,376 augmented images from 1,931 training objects, with predictions scoring below 0.7 discarded. Its value to us is that it reports three operating regimes rather than only the flattering one.

On 737 curated test images (150 m × 150 m, each containing at least one object), 87 per cent of true objects were found and 24 per cent of predictions were false. Carried onto the full 67 km² Larvik area the all-class false-positive rate rose to 89 per cent while grave-mound recall fell from 84 to 38 per cent. Over 937 km² of Øvre Eiker the figures read 38 per cent false at 90 per cent recall — but Øvre Eiker had no ground truth: both are scored against a reference built by visually inspecting the method's own 1,130 predictions, counting the 119 'possible' kilns as non-false and taking recall over 644 confirmed kilns, 578 of them the detector's own hits plus 66 found during that inspection — a self-referential upper bound, not a second landscape measurement. The authors attribute the gap in part to class scope (three object types against kilns alone); the larger difference is that only Larvik had an independently rebuilt reference.

Two mechanisms behind the Larvik drop matter most. First, the reference set moved: Larvik's grave-mound ground truth grew from 57 to 269 objects when the area was properly annotated, so much of the recall collapse is a denominator effect rather than a change in the detector. Second, the labelled reference was built from what the substrate shows — all the vector data, training, validation and test alike, were checked against the ALS visualisations, outlines of objects not clearly visible were deleted, and at Lamøya only 25 of 93 mapped mound contours survived. The network was tuned on legible positives and scored on the curated crops against that same filtered reference, then measured against the harder rebuilt Larvik truth. Our curator-versus-adjudicated distinction is the same problem under another name.

Metric hygiene needs care: the paper reports counts and percentages only — no F1, no Matthews correlation coefficient, no confidence intervals, no box-to-object match criterion — so nothing transfers cleanly onto our 20 m and 50 m match radii. Its object denominator counts duplicates (2,144 instances from 597 unique objects), and its 'consumer's accuracy' means recall while its 'producer's accuracy' means precision, the reverse of the usual convention.

The false-positive problem also resisted the obvious training-side fix: inserting the detector's own errors as 'natural mound', 'natural pit' and 'natural platform' confusion classes did not improve performance, which argues for spending compute on a separate verification stage — our adversarial verifier pass, or the at-least-eight volunteer passes per image in the Dutch project the authors survey.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Archaeological prospection prior art of the most operationally relevant kind: the same target class as ours — grave mounds — detected automatically, but in airborne lidar relief models rather than as symbols on a map, and by a fine-tuned Faster R-CNN rather than a prompted model. Its primary use to us is as an attested measurement of a transfer tax: a detector scoring 84 per cent grave-mound recall with 30 per cent of its grave-mound predictions false on curated crops (Table 7; the 24 per cent headline is the all-class false-positive rate) falls to 38 per cent grave-mound recall with 86 per cent of its grave-mound predictions false when carried onto a whole 67 km² landscape (Table 8; 89 per cent counting the kiln and pitfall-trap predictions, in an area whose reference contains no true objects of either class), with the reference set growing from 57 to 269 mounds doing much of that work — which is precisely the gold-standard-to-deployment gap our carried-versus-oracle operating points set out to quantify. Secondarily it anchors our ground-truth epistemics (a reference built from what the substrate makes legible) and our difficulty ladder (grave mounds were the class the authors found most readily confused with natural terrain), while reporting no F1, no MCC and no match criterion, so it cannot be placed on our metric axis without restatement.

### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "The main reason was that the number of grave mounds in the ground truth data for the Larvik test area had increased from 57 to 269, thus including a large number of grave mounds that were difficult to see in the LRM visualizations of the lidar data."

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "The detection results (Table 7) were obtained by running the method on 737 test images not seen during training. Each test image contained one or more cultural heritage objects, and 597 unique cultural heritage objects in total. - **Paraphrase:** The headline 87 per cent is computed over 2,144 object instances, not the 597 unique objects, because one 150 m × 150 m crop was extracted per catalogued object and every object falling inside it was annotated, so mounds in a dense grave field are scored once per neighbouring crop (the LRM source files also overlap, which is the paper's stated reason for 737 images from 597 objects); the same physical mound can therefore be scored several times, and — our inference, not the paper's — counted as a hit in one crop and a miss in another. - **Quote (verbatim):** "False positives of all three classes were inserted into the ground truth data as 'natural mound', 'natural pit' and 'natural platform'.
