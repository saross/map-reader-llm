## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Willroth, Graham, and Mroczek write a short tutorial in Psychology and Aging for a problem that is structurally ours: preregistering an analysis of data that already exist. Their setting is coordinated data analysis — the same, or nearly the same, statistical model run independently within several existing longitudinal datasets, the per-study effect sizes then synthesised meta-analytically — adopted in ageing research because preregistration “is often not feasible in its traditional form”. Two claims motivate the template. Existing templates assume the registrant has complete and accurate information about the data, which secondary analysis can violate — once per dataset in a coordinated one. And coordination forces most analytic decisions to be fixed in advance anyway, so registering them is “merely formalizing and publicizing planning steps that already take place”.

The transferable design is two-layer. General decision rules pre-commit what will be done if the data are not as expected (their worked example allows any response options that result in a continuous composite measure); dataset-specific information records the variables actually available in each dataset and how each will be scored. They recommend that the first not be used as a replacement for the second, because including both reduces the likelihood that a necessary contingency goes unaccounted for. For a lean-upfront-plus-just-in-time registration that is a constraint rather than an endorsement — and they concede in the same paragraph that “It is impossible to account for all possibilities”.

The template's four substantive parts follow the analysis pipeline rather than the paper — dataset inclusion and exclusion criteria, variable harmonisation, model harmonisation, results synthesis — and are offered as an add-on to an existing secondary-data template (Weston et al., 2019), not a replacement. Three worked answers are directly reusable metric hygiene for a multi-map evaluation: recomputing each model's meta-analytic estimate over only the subset of studies eligible for all models, so that a difference between models is not a difference in which datasets contributed; a leave-one-out re-estimate, recalculated twelve times, once per dataset, testing whether a conclusion rides on one particularly large sample or effect; and between-study heterogeneity statistics, Cochrane's Q and I², registered in advance. Amendments are equally concrete: new datasets may join only if identified before the meta-analysis stage, and joining triggers a timestamped amendment.

Evidence is thin by design: a tutorial, not an evaluation, illustrated by one project (personality and healthcare utilisation) that is retrofitted — it was registered with the older secondary-data template before the add-on existed, and the paper shows how that registration “could have been improved”. No claim is tested: the paper asserts that including dataset-specific information alongside general decision rules reduces the likelihood of deviation from the pre-registration, but offers no evidence. And the unit-shrinkage is forced by circumstance, not argued as a design: with the data already collected and analysts distributed across sites, the analysis is the only unit left to register. A deliberate micro-registration argument occupies that space; this source supplies a prior instance of the mechanism, not a competing claim to the principle.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Prior art for the unit-of-registration shrinkage that D.9 turns on, but shrunk by circumstance rather than argued as a design: with the longitudinal data already collected, Willroth et al. drop the registrable unit to the analysis because nothing earlier is left to register, and build a domain add-on template for the four steps a general template omits. Its primary cluster is preregistration and open-science methodology; it also touches methods/metrics, since its worked answers pre-commit three hygiene guards a multi-map evaluation needs — a constant-subset recomputation so that a difference between models is not a difference in which datasets contributed, a leave-one-out re-estimate testing whether a pooled conclusion rides on one dataset, and between-study heterogeneity statistics registered in advance alongside the pooled estimate. For D.9 it is a supporting citation with a caution attached: it shows the two-layer recipe (general decision rules plus dataset-specific detail) already exists for pre-existing data, while warning that the general layer must not stand in for the specific one.
