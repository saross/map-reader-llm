## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Bertini Baldassini and colleagues (CVPR Workshops 2024) ask what makes multimodal in-context learning (M-ICL) work; their answer, largely, is the text, not the images. They build an ablation framework over IDEFICS 9B (OpenFlamingo 9B in the appendix behaves differently — minimal impact from image perturbation, which is why they focus on IDEFICS) across twelve vision-language datasets — captioning (COCO, Flickr30k, scored with CIDEr), classification (CIFAR-100, ImageNet-1k and two binary sets) and six visual-question-answering sets — perturbing the context at 0, 4, 8, 16 and 32 shots by removing or randomising each modality. Two selection regimes are compared: uniform random sampling, and RICES, a CLIP-similarity retrieval arranging demonstrations by increasing similarity to the query.

The headline is scoped — though the abstract and conclusion state it unscoped — and the scoping is what matters here. For image-to-text tasks — captioning and classification, the shape an image-in/label-out map task takes — demonstration images matter a great deal under random sampling: removing or randomising them leaves CIFAR and ImageNet near the zero-shot level and MS-COCO below it; adding more random-image demonstrations pushes performance lower still. The condition is load-bearing: under RICES retrieval the authors report no significant degradation from the same perturbations, and say the text-driven behaviour is exacerbated there. For VQA, where the prompt already carries text, altering or omitting demonstration images typically costs 1.2 to 1.5 points, a random question 9.5. In captioning, text-only demonstrations are worth 31 points over zero-shot and images add 20 more (CIDEr points on an unbounded scale, not percentages): there, demonstrations teach output format and word distribution first, visual grounding second. In classification, where the label is a word or two, text-only demonstrations buy 0.47% accuracy over zero-shot — next to nothing without images.

The second half — the shortcut analysis — has teeth for a consensus architecture. Under RICES, input similarity tracks response similarity and mean response similarity tracks the score, so the authors conclude RICES works because it retrieves near-answers. In classification, a bare RICES k-nearest-neighbour vote over the retrieved labels matches or beats the model's in-context performance; in the appendix's binary-classification setting (Hateful Memes, rendered SST-2), forcing a balanced label distribution cuts the reported gain over random sampling from 18.90% to 8.40%. Ordering compounds it: on RICES contexts the model exactly reproduces the final demonstration's response in 12% of VQA items and 24% of captioning items at four shots; reversing the similarity order does not remove it — recency, not similarity, is doing the work.

Two boundary conditions travel with all this. The models are 9B open-source interleaved-trained LMMs, and the authors explicitly name GPT-4V and Gemini as untested. And no explicit task instruction is used: the demonstrations are the whole task specification — not an instructed detection prompt's regime. Read as a hazard catalogue, it names three things a few-shot arm of our design would have to control: exemplar class balance, exemplar order, and the possibility that a measured gain is a vote over the exemplars rather than better perception.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: few-shot / in-context learning and annotation budget — this is a controlled account of what image demonstrations actually contribute, and its answer is that the contribution is task- and regime-dependent — little in VQA, a great deal in image-to-text tasks under random sampling and much less under similarity retrieval — and that in classification the gain similarity retrieval buys is not learning at all. It is usable in two places: behind any decision to stay zero-shot or to balance and order-randomise few-shot exemplars, and as a secondary antecedent for the consensus thread, since its central negative result is that in classification tasks similarity-retrieved M-ICL degenerates into a majority vote over the demonstrations' answers (for open-ended generation the authors report the opposite — majority voting is insufficient and the KNN baseline falls short of random sampling) — a degenerate consensus over exemplars, where ours is a consensus over repeated passes of the same query. Its transfer to our setting is bounded and the bound must be stated whenever it is cited: IDEFICS and OpenFlamingo at 9B — two implementations of the same architecture that disagree with each other on the image-to-text exception — no explicit task instruction, and the authors' own closing note that GPT-4V and Gemini remain untested.

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "For VQA, the final context response is used 12% of the cases, regardless of the number of shots. For captioning, this ranges from 24% with four shots to 4% with 32 shots."
- **Locator:** page_index 8 · p.9 · §5.2 M-ICL tends to copy recent similar responses
- **Paraphrase:** With RICES-retrieved contexts the model reproduces the last demonstration's response exactly in 12% of VQA items — flat across shot counts — and in 24% of captioning items at four shots, falling to 4% at 32 shots. ### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "we found the following order of performance from worst to best: random sampling (comparaison point), balanced random sampling (+1.74% improvement), balanced RICES sampling (+8.40% improvement), and RICES sampling alone (+18.90% improvement)."
- **Locator:** page_index 13 · p.14 · §8.2 Balanced sampling (Appendix)
- **Paraphrase:** In a binary-classification setting (Hateful Memes and rendered SST-2), forcing an equal number of demonstrations per class costs roughly half the benefit: measured against random sampling, balanced random gains 1.74%, balanced RICES 8.40%, and unbalanced RICES 18.90%. The paper's own gloss is that balanced RICES yields only half the boost of RICES alone, so the label distribution of the exemplar set — not only its similarity to the query — is doing much of that work. The paper writes these as '% improvement' without saying whether they are relative or absolute, so the ratio is the safe claim, not the raw figures.
- **Relevance:** §3 methods — annotation budget and exemplar selection · Annotation budgets and exemplar composition · **extends**
