## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Taubenfeld, Sheffer and colleagues (Google Research, the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, Columbia; ACL 2025 Findings) introduce Confidence-Informed Self-Consistency (CISC), a drop-in replacement for self-consistency in which the plain majority vote over sampled reasoning paths becomes a confidence-weighted one. Each sampled path is scored by the model itself, the scores are normalised by a softmax with a tunable temperature, and the answer carrying the greatest weighted mass wins. This is the closest published antecedent to the question our consensus-over-passes stage faces: whether the proposer's own stated confidence should modulate the vote, or whether agreement frequency alone should decide.

The headline is a cost claim, not an accuracy claim. Across nine instruction-tuned open-weights language models (Gemma2 2B/9B/27B, Qwen2.5 3B/14B/72B, Mistral 8B/22B/123B) and four reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-Pro, Big-Bench-Hard), the best-performing confidence method, P(True), matches self-consistency's accuracy using 10 responses where self-consistency needs 18.6 on average — a 46 per cent cost reduction, macro-averaged over all models and datasets. That average is right-censored: where self-consistency never caught up within 30 responses the count is recorded as 31 (footnote 3), which caps the metric near 68 per cent at budget 10 and makes 46 per cent a floor. The accuracy improvement at matched budgets is small: 1.6 and 1.1 per cent relative at budgets 5 and 10 macro-averaged, with the micro-averaged bootstrap intervals all excluding zero. Statistically decisive; practically about one point. As prior art for a cost/accuracy trade-space, CISC buys sample efficiency rather than a higher ceiling.

Three results transfer directly to our design. First, the calibration trap: the best-calibrated method (Verbal Binary, ECE-t 0.005) is the worst for weighted voting (10 per cent cost reduction at budget 10), while P(True) at ECE-t 0.030 is the best (46 per cent). Standard calibration compares confidence across questions; weighted voting needs it to separate correct from incorrect answers to the same question. The authors' Within-Question Discrimination (WQD) metric measures precisely that, and its ordering reproduces the CISC ordering exactly. If we elicit verbalised confidence, we should evaluate it within-item, not by aggregate calibration. Second, confidence must supplement frequency rather than replace it: the 'Max' ablation, which takes the single highest-confidence path, is worse than plain self-consistency (−11 and −84 per cent cost reduction; −1.9 and −4.5 accuracy improvement). Third, normalisation helps but must be tuned — softmax without temperature scaling is 'strongly discouraged', and one configuration goes outright negative. That temperature is a single hyper-parameter fitted on a 10 per cent held-out set drawn from the same four datasets it is then applied to — within-corpus fitting, and so a weaker analogue of our carried-versus-oracle operating points than it first looks.

The scope caveats are substantial for us. Every model is a text-only open-weights language model, not a VLM; every task has one discrete answer per question, not a set of localised detections to be matched at a radius; every prompt is zero-shot; and P(True) needs token-level probabilities, which the authors report open-weights and closed-weights frameworks both expose, though possibly not universally.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: consensus / test-time-compute antecedent. CISC is the direct methodological ancestor of the question our aggregation stage poses — whether repeated-sampling consensus should be weighted by the proposer's own stated confidence — and it answers yes, but under two conditions we must import: the weighting works best when normalised with a tuned temperature (unnormalised P(True) still returns a 32 per cent cost reduction at budget 10 against 46, but a softmax at T = 1 is explicitly discouraged and can turn negative), and it has to supplement rather than replace answer frequency, since selecting the single highest-confidence path is worse than plain self-consistency. Secondarily it sits in the calibration / verbalised-confidence cluster, and its sharpest transferable lesson lives there: calibration measured across questions poorly predicts whether confidence helps a vote within a question, so verbalised confidence in our pipeline should be judged by within-item discrimination rather than aggregate calibration. The result also cuts against a straightforward transfer, because CISC's best confidence signal, P(True), needs the model's per-token probabilities: the authors report these are available in open-weights frameworks (Hugging Face) and in closed-weights ones, naming the OpenAI API, while flagging that the feature 'may not be universally available across all frameworks', so whether our own serving stack exposes them is a question for us rather than one this paper answers, and where it does not the fallback is the weaker verbal methods (30 and 10 per cent cost reduction at budget 10, against 46) — and because the whole demonstration is text-only, single-answer, and zero-shot, none of which describes our task.

- **Quote (verbatim):** "the additional cost of the confidence extraction step consists only of encoding len(e) ≈20 tokens and generating a single token."
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3 · §4.1 Confidence Extraction Methods — Efficient and Consistent Confidence Prompting

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "as T →∞, the distribution of normalized confidence scores approaches the uniform distribution, and CISC collapses to vanilla self-consistency."
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3
- **Why:** The cleanest available framing for our aggregation stage: unweighted consensus is not an alternative to confidence weighting but its limiting case, the corner of a one-parameter family where the temperature is infinite and every pass counts equally. It lets us describe greedy consensus as a deliberate choice of T rather than an absence of design, and it names the axis along which a confidence-weighted variant would move. The other end of the same sentence must travel with it, though, because it is the warning: as T → 0 the softmax approaches a hard maximum, 'prioritizing the single response with the highest confidence and disregarding the overall frequency of answers' — and their Table 9 shows that end of the family performing worse than plain self-consistency.
