## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Li, Zhang, Yu, Fu, and Ye report that large-language-model accuracy rises with the number of independent samples drawn from one model and aggregated by majority vote — a two-phase "sampling-and-voting" procedure they name Agent Forest, a tribute to Random Forest. The claim is deliberately unglamorous: no chain-of-thought design, no debate protocol, no supervision or extra training data, just N raw calls to one model and a vote. Across five benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, chess state tracking, MMLU, HumanEval) and three backbones carrying the method (Llama2-13B, Llama2-70B, GPT-3.5-Turbo; GPT-4 appears in the headline table only as a single-query reference), accuracy climbs with ensemble size; the "Ours" column is fixed at ensemble size 40, its results averaged over 10 independent runs (the §5.4 ablation averages 20; Debate-integrated runs stop at 10).

This is the nearest published antecedent to our consensus-over-passes stage, most useful where it is most specific. First, voting is a general similarity rule rather than string matching fixed in advance: each sample is scored by its cumulative similarity to the others and the highest scorer returned, the function chosen to suit the answer type — occurrence frequency for close-ended tasks, where the rule reduces to ordinary exact-match majority voting; mathematical equivalence for arithmetic; BLEU for open-ended code. Our greedy consensus over spatial detections runs the same skeleton with proximity as the similarity function — a structural precedent, not a loose analogy, though their argmax returns one sampled answer unchanged whereas ours pools and clusters detections into a set no single pass produced. Second, relative gain grows with the task's difficulty relative to the model, but in their controlled arithmetic task gains rise and then taper: more samples stop paying once the task exceeds what the model can reason about. Third, absolute performance tracks the prior probability of the correct answer, from which they derive a hierarchical variant: decompose a low-probability task into higher-probability subtasks and route them to different models, cheap ones on easy stages.

Three limits should travel with any citation. Every task returns a single answer per query — a number, an option, a board state, or one program — never a set of located objects, so the mapping onto detection is ours, not theirs. The only reported performance metric is accuracy — no precision, recall, F1 or MCC — so it says nothing about the class-imbalanced regime our tile-level metrics inhabit, and it uses "%" in three incompatible senses: percentage-point gains in §5; the relative gain ratio η = (Pm − Ps)/Ps defined at the head of §6; and, in the §6.1–§6.3 isolation experiments, a "relative gain" that is again a difference, best-of-40 minus single-query accuracy. And the cost-substitution headline — Llama2-13B at ensemble size 40 reaching 59% on GSM8K against the 54% Llama2-70B scores from a single query — is not compute-matched: Llama2-70B under the same method reaches 74% on the same task. Token usage, the authors concede, rises proportionally with the number of agents, and the conclusion names escalating cost as the open problem.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: consensus / test-time-compute antecedent — this is the paper that studies "sample N times, then vote" as a scaling axis in its own right, and our consensus-over-passes stage is a spatial-detection instance of exactly that manoeuvre, down to defining agreement through a similarity function rather than fixing it to string identity. It sits at a distance from us in three respects worth naming rather than papering over: its outputs are single answers rather than sets of located symbols; its only performance metric is accuracy, so it has nothing to say about the imbalanced precision–recall regime our tile-level F1 and MCC inhabit; and its cost-substitution headline compares forty samples from a small model against a single query to a larger one, not against a compute-matched ensemble. Its most transferable content for us is not the scaling curve but the difficulty analysis — gains rise with difficulty measured relative to the model, and taper at the top of the ladder in their controlled arithmetic task, while absolute performance tracks the prior probability of a correct answer — which is the sharpest prior we have found on where a hard task should expect consensus to pay and where it should not, though reading their prior probability across to our base rate of positives is our extension: theirs is 1/K over K equiprobable intervals of their result space, not the prevalence of a class.

### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Table 6: The relative performance gain (%) becomes more significant when the relative difficulty between the LLM and the task increases. It is calculated based on Table 2."
- **Locator:** page_index 8 · p.9 · §6 Understanding the Performance Gains — Table 6 caption
- **Paraphrase:** The gain from ensembling is larger when the task is hard for the particular model, not when the task is hard in some absolute sense — the stated variable is difficulty relative to the LLM. Table 6's own figures, computed from Table 2 with the ensemble at size 40 and relative gain defined as η = (Pm − Ps)/Ps: on GSM8K (labelled easy) 69, 37 and 16 per cent for Llama2-13B, Llama2-70B and GPT-3.5-Turbo; on MATH (labelled hard) 200, 120 and 34 per cent for the same three models.

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Yet, at I = 400, gains taper off, implying that excessive complexity may exceed the model's reasoning capabilities, leading to diminishing returns for our method under extreme task difficulty."
- **Locator:** page_index 9 · p.10 · §6.2 Inherent Difficulty — Property 1
- **Paraphrase:** In the controlled arithmetic task, where inherent difficulty is varied through the integer range I with the step count S and interval count K held fixed and GPT-3.5-Turbo as the backbone, gains are largest at I = 100 and I = 200 and fall away at I = 400. The authors read the fall as the task exceeding the model's reasoning capacity, so the benefit of sampling-and-voting is not monotonic in difficulty: past a point, more samples buy less. Note the definitional wrinkle — the gains plotted in this section are the difference between the best ensemble accuracy (40 samples) and a single query, averaged over 10 runs, whereas the same chapter opens by defining relative gain as a ratio.
- **Relevance:** §5 discussion — choosing K, and why more passes may not rescue the hardest tiles · Consensus / test-time-compute antecedent — limits of repeated sampling; difficulty ladder · **complicates**

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Surprisingly, our method enables a smaller LLM to outperform a larger counterpart by simply scaling up the ensemble size. For instance, the enhanced Llama2-13B model achieves 59% accuracy on the GSM8K dataset, outperforming the Llama2-70B model, which scores 54%."
- **Locator:** page_index 5 · p.6 · §5.1 Generalizability
- **Paraphrase:** With the ensemble at size 40, Llama2-13B reaches 59 per cent on GSM8K, above the 54 per cent Llama2-70B scores from a single query — test-time sampling substituting for parameter count. The comparison is not compute-matched, and the paper's own Table 2 supplies the missing arm: Llama2-70B under the same method reaches 74 per cent on the same task. The result is therefore that a small model with a large sampling budget beats a large model called once, not that it beats the large model outright.
- **Relevance:** §5 discussion — cheap-model-with-more-passes versus expensive-model-with-fewer · Cost/accuracy trade-space — test-time compute as a substitute for model tier · **extends**

- **Quote (verbatim):** "Given that our method scales up by increasing the ensemble size, the token usage increases proportionally with the number of agents or when combined with other methods. When addressing specific tasks, one can trade a higher token budget for improved performance."

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "For open-ended generation tasks such as code generation, the BLEU score proposed by Papineni et al. (2002) is utilized to quantify similarity. Conversely, for close-ended tasks like multiple-choice questions, similarity is measured by occurrence frequency."
- **Locator:** page_index 3 · p.4 · §3 Method — Voting
- **Paraphrase:** Agreement is defined through a similarity function rather than fixed to string identity: each candidate is scored by its summed similarity to every other candidate and the highest-scoring candidate is returned, with the similarity function chosen to suit the answer type — occurrence frequency for closed-ended answers, where the rule collapses back into exact-match majority voting, mathematical equivalence for arithmetic, and BLEU for open-ended code. The generalisation to answer spaces where exact match is meaningless is the paper's; the further step to sets of located point symbols voted by proximity is ours.

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "decomposing low-probability tasks into multiple high-probability subtasks and addressing them hierarchically can boost performance. Moreover, subtasks with varying prior probabilities can be addressed using different models. Additionally, cost savings can be achieved by using simpler, less expensive models for the easier, higher-probability subtasks."
- **Locator:** page_index 10 · p.11 · §6.4 Prior Probability — Derivation
- **Paraphrase:** From the finding that accuracy rises with the prior probability of the correct answer (1/K in their construction), the authors derive a hierarchical variant: split a low-probability task into higher-probability subtasks, solve them in stages, and optionally assign different models to different stages, reserving the expensive model for the hard stage. In their heterogeneous run GPT-3.5-Turbo produces an intermediate answer at K = 8 and GPT-4 then solves for K = 32, improving on GPT-4 alone at K = 32 from 35 to 47 per cent; the homogeneous GPT-3.5-Turbo version improves from 21 to 31 per cent.
- **Relevance:** §5 discussion — spending the expensive model only on the stage that needs it · Cost/accuracy trade-space — heterogeneous multi-stage pipelines; assigning model tiers across pipeline stages · **extends**

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "Nonetheless, such a challenge of escalating costs commonly exists in works requiring multiple LLM calls Wang et al. (2023b); Du et al. (2023). This aligns with recent findings as discussed in Kapoor et al. (2024) that current benchmarks focus narrowly on accuracy, leading to costly agents. Additionally, the lack of adequate holdout sets and standardization in evaluation practices further complicates the development of cost-effective agents."
- **Locator:** page_index 11 · p.12
- **Why:** The strongest argument for reporting a cost/accuracy trade-space comes from the paper that most benefits from ignoring one. Having spent five sections showing that more samples buy more accuracy, the authors close by conceding that escalating cost is the common condition of any multi-call method, that accuracy-only benchmarking is what produces expensive agents, and that inadequate holdout sets and non-standard evaluation compound the problem — the accuracy-only diagnosis being one they endorse from Kapoor et al. (2024) rather than establish. Used honestly the hook cuts both ways: it licenses our insistence on pricing consensus rather than only scoring it, and it sets the bar for our own holdout discipline — the carried-versus-oracle operating points are exactly the "adequate holdout" question this sentence raises.

- **Debate ensemble capped at ten** — The ensemble is scaled to 40 samples everywhere except in the Debate-integrated runs, which are capped at 10 because the communication architecture imposes significant computational overhead. Debate-combined results therefore rest on a quarter of the sampling budget behind every other condition. (page_index 5 · p.6 · §4 Experimental Setup — evaluation protocol)
- **Errors accumulate across steps** — In a task decomposed into steps of equal inherent difficulty, accuracy nonetheless falls as the number of steps grows, because errors from earlier steps accumulate. Applying the method mitigates that decline rather than removing it. (page_index 9 · p.10 · §6.3 Number of Steps — Property 2.2)
- **Voting applied per decomposed step** — The derived Step-wise variant first prompts the model to break the task into steps, then iterates: each round selects the next unprocessed step and runs the sampling-and-voting ensemble to settle that step's result. The vote is therefore taken at every intermediate stage. (page_index 9 · p.10 · §6.3 Number of Steps — Derivation)
- **Step-wise gains rise with difficulty** — Evaluated with the step count fixed at S = 8 and the interval count at K = 4, while the integer range I was tuned from 100 to 400, the step-wise variant improved on the plain ensemble, with reported gains of 15 to 42 per cent that grow as inherent difficulty grows. (page_index 10 · p.11 · §6.3 Number of Steps — Derivation)

(page_index 7 · p.8 · §5.4 Robustness)
