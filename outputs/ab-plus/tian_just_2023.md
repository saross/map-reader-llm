## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Tian, Mitchell and colleagues ask a narrow question with wide consequences: when a commercial application programming interface (API) will not give you log-probabilities, can you just ask the model how confident it is and trust the answer? Across three factual question-answering benchmarks — 1,000 sampled validation questions each from TriviaQA and SciQ, all 817 from TruthfulQA — and five models fine-tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), namely gpt-3.5-turbo, gpt-4, claude-1, claude-2 and Llama-2-70b-chat, verbalised confidence emitted as output tokens is typically better-calibrated than the model's own conditional probabilities, often reducing expected calibration error (ECE) by a relative 50%. Footnote 8 rules out any fine-tuning for verbalisation, so no gradient update is involved — but the prompt is not doing all the work: ECE-t and BS-t are temperature-scaled on held-out folds, and Ling. 1S-opt. fits its expression-to-probability map on held-out calibration questions.

Four findings bear on our design. First, the baseline that loses is close to our architecture. Their 'Label prob.' method does not read log-probabilities at all — it draws n = 10 samples, returns the most common answer under a large-language-model equivalence check, and estimates p(y|x) from that sampled distribution: majority-vote consensus over samples, with the sampled frequency standing in for the score. Footnote 6 adds that n = 20 on gpt-3.5-turbo 'did not meaningfully improve' calibration, so more passes is not an obvious route to a better-calibrated score.

Second, prompting for k guesses before the probability improves calibration, borrowed from 'considering the opposite' in the human-judgement literature. On our reading of Tables 1–5 the gain is conditional: top-4 gives the lowest raw ECE of the Verb. 1S top-k rows on SciQ and TruthfulQA in all five model tables, but is worse than top-2 on TriviaQA in all five.

Third, chain-of-thought does not improve verbalised calibration, in the headline conclusions and again in Appendix Figure 5 — but on gpt-3.5-turbo alone, the only model for which CoT variants were run.

Fourth, calibration is not portable. Claude-1's log probabilities are similar to or better than gpt-3.5-turbo's while it verbalises worse; claude-2 verbalises a little better than gpt-3.5-turbo; Llama-2-70b-chat is weaker again; one- and two-stage prompts 'sometimes differ drastically'. The authors close by asking what makes a model good at this. Gemini is absent, as is any vision task, and they flag factual recall versus reasoning as an open question.

The metric hygiene is worth copying. The paper says outright that ECE 'completely fails to capture' discriminative power — a random binary guesser stating 50% has perfect ECE — and therefore reports temperature-scaled Brier score and selective-classification AUC alongside it. The two can move apart: for claude-1 on SciQ (Table 3), Verb. 1S top-4 improves ECE from 0.216 to 0.151 and ECE-t from 0.149 to 0.057 while AUC falls from 0.786 to 0.667 and BS-t worsens from 0.195 to 0.226. For a pipeline that thresholds confidence to choose an operating point, discrimination is the property that matters, and an ECE-only report can hide its loss.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: calibration and verbalised confidence — this is the reference result licensing a model's stated confidence as a usable score, and the paper that claims priority for it ('Our work is the first to show that verbalized probabilities are often better-calibrated than the model's conditional probabilities for RLHF-LMs'), which makes asking the model rather than reading its sampled answer distribution — exactly the choice between our verbalised confidence and our pass-agreement signal — a documented, if not uniform, win. Two of its findings cut against our defaults rather than for them: chain-of-thought did not improve verbalised calibration (on gpt-3.5-turbo, the only model for which CoT variants were run), and the ability to verbalise calibrated confidence varies enough across the GPT, Claude and Llama families — with one- versus two-stage prompts that 'sometimes differ drastically' — that nothing here transfers to Gemini without our own check. Secondary clusters: consensus / test-time-compute antecedent (the sampled 'Label prob.' baseline, its ten-samples-versus-one cost, and the n = 20 footnote) and methods/metrics (the paper's explicit refusal to report ECE on its own).

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "A trustworthy real-world prediction system should produce well-calibrated confidence scores; that is, its confidence in an answer should be indicative of the likelihood that the answer is correct, enabling deferral to an expert in cases of low-confidence predictions."
- **Locator:** page_index 0 · p.1
- **Why:** States the operational reason calibration matters in the exact shape our deployment takes: confidence is worth having because it lets low-confidence cases be deferred to a human expert. For a 55-map survey where every detection cannot be checked, that is the whole argument for carrying a confidence score at all, and it frames the threshold as a triage decision rather than a scoring convenience. Use it with the caveat this paper itself supplies: deferral only works if the confidences discriminate, which ECE does not measure.
