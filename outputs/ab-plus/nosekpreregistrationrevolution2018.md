## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

The canonical statement of the distinction our registration apparatus exists to preserve. Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven, and Mellor — three of the four employed by the Center for Open Science, as its conflict-of-interest statement declares — argue in a PNAS colloquium paper that research alternates between postdiction (using already-observed data to generate hypotheses about why something occurred) and prediction (acquiring new data to test ideas about what will occur), that hindsight bias and reward structures make the two easy to conflate, and that preregistration is the remedy. The framing sentence worth carrying into D.9 refuses to moralise: preregistration "does not favor prediction over postdiction; its purpose is to make clear which is which". That is the warrant for an analyses register labelling what was registered and what came after the data, and for the retrospective's claim that a falsified H2 direction is more credible for having been registered.

It is a position piece, not an experiment, and it hedges its own evidential base: the case is "theoretically strong as a matter of inductive inference and empirically bolstered by some correlational evidence", but "there is not yet sufficient experimental evidence establishing its superiority for reproducibility". Cite it for the conceptual line, not for an effect size.

The central section of the paper is a menu of nine named challenges to the idealised model, five of which are ours. Challenge 1 (procedure changes mid-study) licenses transparently reported deviations. Challenge 2 (assumption violations discovered during analysis) offers four strategies — staged or sequential preregistration, blinding the dataset, preregistered decision trees, and standard operating procedures — which together are the 2018 antecedent for the moves Gould et al. (2026) gather under registered flexibility, and it attaches the leakage limit that governs a phased design. Challenge 5 (many experiments) describes a preregistration template that fixes the protocol while per-experiment registrations record which parameters change: close to our study-YAML-plus-experiment-intent pattern. Challenge 6 (a programme of research) demands that every outcome of the plan be reported, not only the ones that fired. Challenge 7 (few a priori expectations) gives the seal-and-unseal structure — split the data, explore one part, register the outcomes of that exploration, then unseal — which we read as the shape of our gold-standard-to-55-map calibration transfer, with the carried operating point as the prediction and the oracle point as a postdiction it is honest to label as one.

Two scope cautions. The machinery is null hypothesis significance testing (NHST) throughout — P-value diagnosticity, the garden of forking paths, alpha corrections — and our headline metrics are F1 and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC); what licenses the transfer is the paper's own bracketed concession that no statistical method on its own removes analytic latitude — which wants arguing rather than assuming. And nothing here is computational: no mention of machine learning or language models appears in the extracted text. Applying it to a vision-language model (VLM) evaluation study is an extension licensed by the generality of the argument, not by any instance the paper supplies.

### KP1
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Preregistration does not favor prediction over postdiction; its purpose is to make clear which is which."
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3 · §Preregistration in Practice (opening sentence)
- **Paraphrase:** Preregistration is not an argument for confirmatory over exploratory work; its function is to keep the two distinguishable. - **Relevance:** §D.9 preregistration retrospective; §Methods, analyses register · D.9 retrospective — what the registration bought: the analyses register as a prediction/postdiction label rather than a purity test · **supports**

### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Sealing a dataset and preregistering the outcomes of the discovery process before unsealing converts postdictions from the initial dataset to predictions for the holdout dataset."
- **Locator:** page_index 4 · p.5 · §Challenge 7: Few a Priori Expectations
- **Paraphrase:** In the cross-validation pattern the source describes, the data are split in two: one part is explored freely to build models and predictions, the other is sealed until exploration ends; registering the products of the exploration before unsealing promotes them from postdictions about the explored part to predictions about the held-out part. The source scopes it immediately — where clear predictions are few and the data too scarce to split, "there is no magical solution".
- **Relevance:** §D.9; §Results, gold-standard-to-55-map transfer · Calibration transfer as seal-and-unseal: carried operating point as the registered prediction, oracle point as the postdiction, transfer tax as the gap · **supports**

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Effective application of sequential preregistration is difficult in many research applications. If an earlier stage reveals information about outcomes to be tested at a subsequent stage, then the preregistration is compromised."
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3 · §Challenge 2: Discovery of Assumption Violations During Analysis
- **Paraphrase:** The source offers staged registration as a legitimate strategy — "it is possible to define stages and preregister incrementally", the worked example being a first registration that fixes distributional checks, exclusions, and transformations without revealing outcomes, followed by a second registering the outcome model — and then names its limit: the strategy is hard to apply well, and any leak of outcome information from an earlier stage into a later one voids it.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 (micro-registration recipe; naming and priority) · Grain-not-volume and the phased design: the licence for incremental registration, and the leakage condition that decides when a phase boundary spends the registration's credibility · **complicates**

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Compared with the situation in which Jolene did not preregister at all, preregistration with reported deviations provides substantially greater confidence in the resulting statistical inferences."
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3 · §Challenge 1: Changes to Procedure During Study Administration
- **Paraphrase:** Jolene is the source's worked example: an infant-laboratory researcher who planned 100 observations, could only recruit 60, and had not registered an exclusion for infants who fall asleep. The comparison is against not preregistering at all, not against a deviation-free registration — and the source keeps its own caveat attached, that deviations decided after the data are observed carry "increased risk of bias" even when reported transparently.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 preregistration retrospective (deviations interpretation) · D.9 deviations and errata: the over-bake-with-receipts move, and the counterweight that amendment count is a biased measure because undisclosed drift is invisible · **supports**

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Not only is it essential that the analysis plan be defined blind to the research outcomes, all outcomes of that analysis plan must be reported to avoid the problem of selective reporting."
- **Locator:** page_index 3 · p.4 · §Challenge 6: A Program of Research
- **Paraphrase:** Outcome-blind planning is only half the requirement; the plan's full set of results has to be reported, or selective reporting reintroduces the bias the registration was meant to remove. The source's example is a high-risk programme where one experiment in twenty returns a positive result, every one of them preregistered — and it adds that the benefit depends on preregistrations and results being permanently preserved and accessible.
- **Relevance:** §D.9; §Results (full-matrix reporting); §D.10 future work · Reporting the whole condition matrix, including null and abandoned arms; the disclosed-not-run strand (H14/H15) extends the point beyond what the source covers · **supports**

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "no statistical method on its own avoids researcher opportunity for flexibility in analytical decisions, such as exclusion criteria or the creation of variables"
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2 · §Standard Tools of Statistical Inference Assume Prediction (bracketed note)
- **Paraphrase:** The paper scopes itself to NHST because of that framework's prevalence, concedes that "the opportunities and challenges discussed are somewhat different" under other approaches such as Bayesian methods, and then insists that the underlying problem — researcher latitude over exclusions and variable construction — is not solved by any statistical method on its own.
- **Relevance:** §D.9; §Methods, statistical approach · Scope transfer: importing an NHST-framed preregistration argument into an F1/MCC evaluation study whose inference is bootstrap and permutation based · **complicates**

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Another method is to preregister a decision tree. The decision tree defines the sequence of tests and decision rules at each stage of the sequence."
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3 · §Challenge 2: Discovery of Assumption Violations During Analysis
- **Paraphrase:** A preregistered decision tree fixes in advance both the order of tests and the rule applied at each branch — the source's example is testing a normality assumption and branching to a parametric or nonparametric test on the result. Its caveats travel with it: trees suit cases where the range of possible analyses is easily described, biases can be preregistered into one (registering a sequence of exclusion rules and stopping at P < 0.05), and the source pairs trees with standard operating procedures, which it says have "more general application than a decision tree".
- **Relevance:** §D.9 (naming/reframe ruling) · D.9 naming and priority: registered decision rules as pre-2026 prior art for what later work names registered flexibility · **supports**
