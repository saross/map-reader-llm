## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Snell, Lee, Xu and Kumar ask what a fixed but non-trivial budget of inference-time computation buys on a hard prompt, answering with a sweep over two mechanisms: search against a process reward model (PRM) verifier, and refinement of the proposal distribution by a model finetuned to revise its own answers. Everything runs on the MATH benchmark (the 12k-train / 500-test split of Lightman et al.) with PaLM 2-S* (Codey) as the base model — a 2024, text-only, single-final-answer setting, so every number below travels with that scoping.

The paper's abstraction is our architecture's vocabulary: test-time compute is a proposal distribution plus a verifier that aggregates or selects across its samples, with best-of-N the simplest instance, our consensus-over-passes plus adversarial verifier another. Its central claim is that no fixed setting of that machinery is best. Efficacy varies with the difficulty of the prompt as judged from the base model's own pass@1 — estimated from 2048 samples per question, binned into five quantiles — so the 'compute-optimal' policy selects a strategy per difficulty bin. Doing so outperforms parallel best-of-N at up to 4x less compute for revisions (64 generations versus 256) and nearly outperforms it for search (16 versus 64); the discussion states the aggregate gain as a factor of 2–4x, the abstract as 'more than 4×'. For us the load-bearing consequence is the shape, not the multiplier: a uniform strategy across tiles is the baseline this paper beats, and it beats it by varying strategy with difficulty at a common budget, not by spending unevenly.

Three findings cut against optimism. Beam search, the stronger optimiser of the two search methods, degrades on easy questions as the budget grows while best-of-N does not, which the authors read as exploitation of the PRM signal — a failure mode of selection pressure against a verifier, to audit on easy tiles rather than hard ones. On the hardest difficulty bin no method makes meaningful progress, which the discussion generalises to 'small gains on hard problems'; more passes do not rescue instances the proposer cannot reach. And the difficulty statistic that makes the scheme work assumes oracle access to a correctness check, which deployment lacks; the substitute, binning on averaged PRM scores over the same 2048 samples, is itself costly, and though the two curves largely overlap, in the search setting some of that benefit diminishes at higher budgets under predicted difficulty where oracle bins keep improving — an oracle-versus-carried gap of the same structure as our transfer taxes.

Two limits on transfer. Revision and verification here are induced by capability-specific finetuning, because prompting off-the-shelf models was not effective at either; a prompted VLM inherits unweighted consensus over parallel samples more cleanly than either the sequential branch or verifier-weighted selection, though the authors expected future models to acquire these capabilities directly. And the exchange with pretraining is directional: test-time compute beats a ~14x larger model on easy and medium questions and at low inference load, but not on hard questions or at high load.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: consensus / test-time-compute antecedent — this is the canonical formalisation of the trade-space our pass-budget experiments live in, and it supplies both the proposer/verifier vocabulary we use and the finding that the best strategy is instance-dependent rather than global. It supports the design in the abstract (repeated sampling against a verifier is a genuine scaling axis, and can substitute for a larger model under stated conditions) while complicating our instantiation of it in three specific ways: a uniform strategy across instances is the baseline it beats, its gains thin to nothing on the hardest instances, and its difficulty-conditioning needs either an oracle or the paper's substitute, a model-predicted estimate that is itself expensive (2048 samples per question). Secondary clusters are the cost/accuracy trade-space and calibration transfer, but the scoping must travel with any citation: this is text-only mathematical reasoning with capability-finetuned PaLM 2 models in 2024, so its structure transfers to us and none of its numbers do.

## Overflow (paraphrase only; each rests on a byte-checked span)

Verified secondary material that did not fit the summary band. The verbatim spans behind these paraphrases are held in the working copy (`_work/snell_scaling_2024.overflow.json`), not published; page anchors are to the extracted page cache (structured 2026-09-03; model `claude-opus-5` requested).

- **Verifier flip rate in both directions** — Snell et al. measure how often a self-correction pass destroys a correct answer, not only how often it rescues a wrong one. Because their revision model was trained solely on sequences with incorrect answers in context, at test time it may sample a correct answer and revise it away: roughly 38% of correct answers are converted back to incorrect ones under a naïve approach. Their remedy is not a better reviser but an aggregator — sequential majority voting or verifier-based selection over the whole sequence of revisions. (page_index 11 · p.12 · §6.1 Setup: Training and Using Revision Models)
- **Score-aggregation rule is empirical** — The rule for collapsing a verifier's per-step scores into a single whole-answer score is an empirical choice, not a settled one: taking the last step's score outperformed the two alternatives Snell et al. tested, reversing prior work (Lightman et al.; Wang et al.) that had found the minimum to be the best aggregator. They attribute the reversal to their verifier having been trained on soft Monte Carlo return labels, which behave very differently from binary correctness labels, so an aggregation rule selected under one supervision regime need not carry to another. (page_index 23 · p.24 · Appendix E — Comparing PRM Aggregation Strategies)
- **Confidence-weighted answer voting** — For choosing among candidate answers, Snell et al. follow Li et al. in using “best-of-N weighted” selection rather than standard best-of-N: the verifier's correctness scores are marginalised across every solution that reaches the same final answer, and the answer carrying the greatest total is selected. Selection across samples is therefore confidence-weighted voting rather than an argmax over individual candidates. (page_index 6 · p.7 · §5.1 Training Verifiers Amenable to Search — answer aggregation)
- **Selection and evaluation on separate folds** — Snell et al. keep the data used to choose a strategy separate from the data used to report its performance: within each difficulty bin of the test set they run two-fold cross-validation, selecting the best-performing strategy on one fold, measuring it on the other, then reversing the roles and averaging the two test folds. They state the reason explicitly — avoiding the confound of using the same test set both to compute the difficulty bins and to select the compute-optimal strategy. (page_index 5 · p.6 · §3.2 Estimating Question Difficulty for Compute-Optimal Scaling)
- **Borrowed verifier labels did not transfer** — A process reward model trained on another group's released human-labelled dataset proved easy to exploit under even naïve best-of-N sampling, which Snell et al. hypothesise is a result of distribution shift between that dataset's GPT-4 generated samples and their own PaLM 2 models. Rather than pay for fresh crowd-worker labels on their own models — a process they call expensive — they supervised the verifier without human labels, estimating per-step correctness from Monte Carlo rollouts run from each step of the solution. (page_index 6 · p.7 · §5.1 Training Verifiers Amenable to Search — PRM training)
- **Verifier coupled to its proposer** — The same coupling appears within the paper: applied to outputs from the revision model, an outcome reward model trained on the revision model's own outputs outperformed the process reward model trained on the PaLM 2-S* base model's outputs, which the authors again put down to distribution shift. The better verifier here is the one matched to the generator whose outputs it is scoring. (page_index 26 · p.27 · Appendix J — Revision Model Verifier Training)
- **Pricing an unequal-cost method** — To compare search methods fairly, Snell et al. price each by what it actually consumes rather than by its nominal N. A generation is one sampled answer from the base model, so for beam search and best-of-N the budget is the number of beams or N respectively; lookahead search samples k additional steps at every step of the search, and is therefore costed at N × (k + 1) samples. (page_index 8 · p.9 · §5.3 Analysis Results — protocol for estimating the cost of each method)
- **Strongest search loses at matched cost** — Costed that way, the most compute-hungry search method loses: lookahead search generally underperforms the other methods at the same generation budget, which the authors attribute to the additional computation incurred by simulating the lookahead rollouts. (page_index 8 · p.9 · §5.3 Analysis Results — search method comparison)
- **Better proposer, worse sequential gain** — Optimising the proposer further broke the sequential axis rather than improving it: after the revision model was retrained with ReSTEM, additional sequential revisions substantially hurt performance as the sequential-to-parallel ratio was varied. The authors hypothesise that the online data ReSTEM generates exacerbates spurious correlations in the revision data, causing the optimised model to fail to learn the revision task. (page_index 26 · p.27 · Appendix K — ReSTEM Revision Model Experiments)
- **Cheaper difficulty estimator left untested** — Snell et al. name a cheaper substitute for their sampling-based difficulty estimate but do not test it: finetuning a model to predict correctness directly from the question, which they leave to future work. They are blunt that the procedure they did use is extremely costly because it requires generating many samples, is not accounted for in their analysis, and would be problematic in a practical production setting. (page_index 21 · p.22 · Appendix C — Unsupervised Difficulty Bins)
