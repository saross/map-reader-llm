## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Chen and colleagues detect kurgans as an object-detection problem — bounding boxes in 0.8 m pan-sharpened Gaofen-2 imagery over the Russian Altai — and define themselves against Caspari and Crespo, whose CNN they characterise as classifying image subsets rather than detecting small objects in large scenes (p.2). That makes this the closest published object-level analogue to our task in a remote-sensing modality. The reference set is 844 validation mounds across four sub-regions chosen from a much larger surveyed area whose field database holds more than 16,000 relics including some 4,200 stone mounds, with features that have disappeared or become indistinguishable removed and everything under 5 m in diameter sieved out (p.3); evaluation is four-fold cross-validation with one area held out per round (p.9). Five off-the-shelf detectors were compared; the best, Cascade R-CNN, reached average precision 36.3, and three domain-specific additions — the near-infrared band, background-only negatives collected outside the study areas, and denser anchors with a dynamically sized mini-batch that lifts the positive-to-negative ratio from 1:69 to 1:5 (p.12) — took it to 45.3 (p.9).

The metric trap is in the subscripts. Recall50 and Precision85 are detection-score thresholds, not intersection-over-union thresholds (p.11), and the criterion by which a predicted box is scored correct against the field data is never stated in the text, so recall 53.9 and precision 53.6 at threshold 0.5 are not commensurable with our matched object-level F1 at 20 m and 50 m radii. No F1, no MCC, no interval estimates.

The deployment is what maps onto our 55-map run. Retrained on all four surveyed areas, the model was carried to 1,750 km² near Uvs Lake in Mongolia — an actual search area of about 2,320 km² once scene overlap is counted — in roughly 50 minutes of computing time (p.14), on the single desktop with an RTX 2080 Ti that ran all the experiments (p.11). Adjudication of the yield was expert visual inspection by two co-author archaeologists, with fieldwork confirmation still outstanding: a curator-adjudicated reference rather than field truth, which is the distinction our ground-truth section draws. Interpreting the detections took about six hours; the unassisted comparison is left hedged and unquantified as 'much more than 6 h' (p.14).

The paper then shifts criterion, defending the method on density maps that reproduce the spatial distribution despite low precision and recall (pp.13, 18). For a survey-planning deliverable that is a fair defence; for an inventory it is not, and the difference is exactly a choice of match radius. Its residual warning for us is that the whole-Altai inventory the abstract gestures at is explicitly out of scope, needing more than 1,000 GF-2 scenes (p.17).

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Archaeological prospection prior art in its object-detection form: the same target class as ours, burial mounds, but localised with bounding boxes in 0.8 m satellite imagery rather than read as cartographic symbols, and scored by average precision at detection-score thresholds rather than by matched object-level F1 — so it sets the attested performance bar for supervised mound detection (best AP 45.3; recall 53.9 and precision 53.6 at threshold 0.5) without being directly commensurable with our numbers. Its second use to us is as a deployment precedent: a detector calibrated on four surveyed areas carried to an unsurveyed 1,750 km² region, where the yield had to be adjudicated by eye and the method was defended on spatial distribution rather than per-object accuracy — the shape of the carried-versus-oracle problem our transfer taxes quantify. Secondarily it supplies the annotation-budget and ground-truth contrast we need: a visibility-pruned reference of 844 mounds behind a bespoke supervised pipeline, against our zero-training prompted vision-language model.

- **Quote (verbatim):** "However, we also observed that there are a few burial mounds that were only detected in one specific image, but not in another. - **Locator:** page_index 18 · p.19 · §5 Discussion — merging results from overlapping images
- **Paraphrase:** Where four scenes overlapped, detection was usually consistent across images (same page) but not always, and the authors assert that merging the per-image results will certainly raise recall, while the extra false detections are reported as observed. The union was taken with non-maximum suppression and the authors leave better merging to future work. This is the consensus-over-passes trade-off in a remote-sensing guise — repeated looks at the same ground recover objects that any single look misses, at a precision cost — but it is an unquantified observation here, with the diversity coming from different acquisitions rather than repeated sampling of one model.

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Second, many stone mounds have very low contrast with their background in the images and this makes them very difficult to detect visually, even for trained experts. Third, there are many other ground objects in the Altai mountains that look like stone mounds. Although we constructed a set of typical background images, false detections are still high, and separating these from true mounds is very difficult when relying only on satellite images. This is still an open problem."
- **Locator:** page_index 17 · p.18 · §5 Discussion — causes of low precision
- **Paraphrase:** Two of the four causes the authors give for low precision are that many mounds have very low contrast against their background — hard even for trained experts to see — and that other ground objects in the region look like mounds. The look-alike problem survived a training set explicitly enriched with background-only negatives, and the authors call separating look-alikes from true mounds on satellite imagery alone an open problem. Both halves position our task on the difficulty ladder: a map symbol is drawn to be legible, so the visibility half largely does not transfer — degraded or overprinted sheets erode that contrast, though rarely to the point of invisibility — while the confusable-neighbour half transfers directly, our distractors being the other symbol families on the same sheet.
- **Relevance:** §2 related work — where symbol reading sits on the difficulty ladder · Difficulty ladder: area segmentation to point symbols · **supports**

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "Our method performed well by recalling more than half of the known mounds. Although the precision was still not high, the density maps that were generated from the detection results, corresponded well to the spatial distribution of the mounds."
- **Locator:** page_index 18 · p.19
- **Why:** The honest self-assessment of what supervised mound detection delivered in 2021: recall a little over half, precision conceded to be low, and the defence resting on the density map rather than the detection list. It works as a hook only with that logic intact — 'performed well' is a judgement against a survey-planning purpose, not a claim about per-object accuracy — and used honestly it sets up the contrast our study draws, which is to report matched object-level F1 and MCC at stated radii and let the operating point, not the deliverable, absorb the precision-recall trade.
