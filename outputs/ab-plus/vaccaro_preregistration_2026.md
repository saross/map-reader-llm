## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Vaccaro's ICML 2026 paper (PMLR 306) is the nearest machine-learning-side prior art to the citing paper's preregistration apparatus — an argument and a call to action rather than a study. Single-authored (Michelle Vaccaro, MIT IDSS) despite its first-person plural, it argues that preregistration should be extended to “experiments with AI agents”, catalogues the researcher degrees of freedom they introduce (Table 1), proposes a template, and calls on conferences, journals, and funding agencies to normalise the practice. Its only empirical content is a simulation she labels “illustrative, not a comprehensive study of anchoring in LLMs”: nine models from three families (Gemini, OpenAI, Llama) crossed with three system-prompt framings, three anchor distances, three delivery methods, five questions, and two outlier rules — 2,430 specifications, whose anchoring indices “range from negative values (suggesting reverse anchoring) through zero (no effect) to values exceeding 1.0”, against the 0.3–0.6 anchoring range her Figure 3 caption takes from the human literature.

Scope is where the fit needs care. Her paradigm is “in silico” behavioural experiments — agents as proxies for human participants and as behavioural subjects in their own right — a field §4.7 scopes as agents “both as objects of study and as tools for studying humans”. Our vision-language models are neither — measuring instruments applied to a map corpus, which that two-role framing does not cover, though Alternative View 3 concedes a registration can fix estimands, metrics, and decision thresholds “for prediction or ML-style models”, a case she never works out. Yet almost every degree of freedom she catalogues transfers unchanged, and three land on live design questions. “Inference budget” is named as a p-hacking surface — giving some conditions more “thinking room” — which is our reasoning-mode manipulation; “Aggregation method” (“Choosing how to aggregate across runs, agents, or items post-hoc”) is our greedy-consensus-versus-WBF choice; and “Multiple comparisons” (“Testing many metrics (accuracy, win-rate, rubric score, Elo, pass@k) and reporting the best”) is the discipline that reporting F1 and MCC together enforces.

Three template provisions bind directly. §4.1 requires exact model identifiers and version checkpoints (“gpt-4-0125-preview rather than simply ‘GPT-4’”) and verbatim prompt text, and adds in weaker language that “it is important to include” the API version and access date. §4.2 requires disclosure of pilot-testing history — prompt iterations explored, models tested, whether preliminary results informed the hypotheses — the provision a calibrate-on-a-gold-standard-then-deploy workflow must answer to. §4.4–4.5 require the specification space, in studies claiming robustness or generalisation, to be defined in advance and reported in full, and accommodate staged designs through decision rules fixed beforehand.

For the D.9 naming ruling this is a second occupant of the adaptive/staged cell alongside Gould et al. (2026), the first from machine learning. What it leaves unoccupied is execution: no worked instance outside the behavioural paradigm; a template deferred to Supplementary Materials, outside this cache; and, across all fourteen pages, no hit for “automat*” and no proposal that language models help author or check a registration — though §4.3 makes LLM-based judges a registrable object.

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Researchers may preregister an initial design with explicit decision rules for subsequent stages"
- **Locator:** page_index 5 · p.6 · §4.5 Handling staged and adaptive designs
- **Paraphrase:** The template offers a staged preregistration option — borrowed from registered-reports frameworks (Chambers, 2013) — in which an initial design carries explicit rules governing what happens at later stages; the worked example is a floor/ceiling threshold rule on pilot accuracy, and the stated constraint is that such rules “must be specified in advance, not invented post hoc”. The legitimating case given is sequential decision-making such as using pilot data to calibrate stimulus difficulty or set sample sizes.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — adaptive design and the naming ruling · Adaptive/phased registration — second occupant of the cell, from ML rather than ecology · **complicates**

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "whatever space of specifications a researcher plans to explore must be defined in advance, and results from the full space must be reported"
- **Locator:** page_index 5 · p.6 · §4.4 Addressing robustness and generalization
- **Paraphrase:** For studies claiming robustness or generalisation, the template's core principle is that the whole planned specification space is declared beforehand and every result in it reported, including null and contrary ones — otherwise robustness checks become selectively reported post-hoc exercises. The scope is explicitly robustness and generalisation sections, not every analysis in a study.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 — completeness of reported conditions; methods/metrics hygiene · Reporting the full condition matrix rather than the winning slice — carried versus oracle operating points · **complicates**

### KP6

- **Paraphrase:** Answering the objection that preregistration privileges null-hypothesis significance testing, the paper replies that a registration can equally fix estimands, loss functions, evaluation metrics, and decision thresholds for predictive or ML-style work; it summarises the position as preregistration being “a commitment device for research degrees of freedom, not a commitment to NHST”. - **Relevance:** §D.9 — warrant for a metric-and-threshold registration; methods/metrics · Preregistering metrics and operating thresholds instead of significance tests · **supports**

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "the final experiment makes only that single path reproducible; it does not reveal the garden of forking paths that preceded it"
- **Locator:** page_index 7 · p.8 · §6 Alternative Views — View 6 (open-sourcing is enough)
- **Paraphrase:** Replying to the objection that open-sourcing code is the right credibility mechanism for computational research, the paper concedes open-sourcing settles reproducibility but argues it exposes only the path taken, leaving the prompt variants, models, temperature settings, and parsing rules abandoned along the way invisible — the distinction it draws between reproducibility and credibility. It adds that detection cost otherwise falls on reviewers and replicators, whom it expects not to discharge it.
