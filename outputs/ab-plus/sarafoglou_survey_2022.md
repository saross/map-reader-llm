## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Sarafoglou and colleagues supply the empirical base rate for what preregistration costs and buys, from researchers who have done it. They surveyed 355 researchers — 299 who had published both preregistered and non-preregistered work, 56 only non-preregistered work — on nine aspects of the research process distilled from a free-text pilot. On a 1–7 scale with 4 as neutral, the experienced group rated seven of the nine aspects above neutral, led by the analysis plan (M = 6.01); only work-related stress (M = 3.73 [3.59, 3.87]) and total project duration (M = 3.07 [2.93, 3.21]) fell below it. Read the anchors before borrowing those two: 1 is "was increased" / "was longer", so the low means denote more stress and longer projects. That is the title's arithmetic — better science but more work — and the sample-wide, self-reported counterpart to the single-case labour overruns that §D.9 engages.

Experience flips the verdict rather than merely softening it. Among those who had preregistered, 88% would recommend the practice and 83% would keep using it; among those who had not, 45% would recommend it and 7% would take it up. That asymmetry matters most to us: the anticipated cost is sharpest in exactly the audience a preregistered study outside psychology must persuade. Three scope conditions travel with it. The evidence is self-report: the authors say plainly it cannot demonstrate whether the perceived secondary effects match the actual ones. The sampling is asymmetric: 65.43% in the preregistration group (299 of 457 delivered) against 2.80% in the non-preregistration group (56 of 1999 sent, not of the 1853 delivered). And the groups were not asked the same question — past experience for the preregistration group, future expectations for the other — which the authors say may have reduced comparability. The sample is psychology throughout, and self-selection is the authors' own biggest generalisability concern.

Three details bear on our registration apparatus. First, the authors argue the secondary benefits depend largely on document quality: the thoroughness of the protocol forces the careful thinking, so a detailed analyses register, not the bare act of registering, earns the workflow gains. That is their discussion-section reading, not a survey finding — no item asked about document quality. Second, the paper models inline deviation disclosure rather than a separate log: it states the blanket policy twice, in the disclosures and again in the data-accessibility statement, that any deviations are mentioned in the manuscript, then flags three at the point of use. Third, the free-text appendix records the grievance a phased design answers: eleven respondents on creativity, exploration and compliance, one reporting it is sometimes impossible to know how data should be analysed before seeing its structure. Less welcomely, thirteen raised disadvantages for fields outside psychology and for descriptive and exploratory designs, some reporting that where the practice is unfamiliar (animal research is the example given) it makes studies harder, not easier, to publish. The authors caution the free-text summary rests on 78 of 355 respondents and is not necessarily representative.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

The empirical warrant behind our preregistration retrospective: not a template and not an argument for registering, but survey evidence on what registering does to a workflow, from 299 researchers who have done it. Its primary cluster is preregistration and open-science methodology, and it supplies three things §D.9 needs — base rates for the trade (better analysis plans, longer projects, more stress), the experienced-versus-inexperienced asymmetry that frames adoption in a field where preregistration is rare, and, in its free-text appendix, the practitioner grievance that motivates adaptive or phased registration. It is perception data from psychology alone, self-selected, and by the authors' own statement unable to establish that the perceived effects are the real ones, so it grounds claims about the experience of registering and never about its measured effects.
