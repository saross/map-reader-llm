## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Niculiță presents a single-author, open-source pipeline that extracts burial mounds (tumuli) from a LiDAR bare-earth digital elevation model (DEM) — produced at 0.5 m and resampled to 5 m — over two adjacent 100 km² blocks of the Jijia Hills in north-eastern Romania: the northern block trains, the southern validates externally. The architecture is a cascade, not a single classifier: local elevation maxima are found with focal windows tested from 3 × 3 to 25 × 25 pixels; a peak is kept only where a local-convexity watershed seed falls in its 3 × 3 neighbourhood; the corresponding convexity segments become mound candidates; and a Random Forest classifies those segments, fitted first on all 751 shape and geomorphometric descriptors and finally on 72. That shape — a cheap high-recall proposer feeding a discriminative stage that must survive extreme imbalance (the burial-mound class is 0.6% for the northern study area and 0.2% for the southern) — is our proposer-plus-verifier split in a classical machine-learning idiom, and the author sets aside template matching and convolutional networks on grounds of study-area size and computational load, noting that the workflow could be run in a tiled scheme on PCs of 4–8 cores and 10–24 GB.

Two things repay close reading. The first is the transfer test. Fitting happens in the north; the abstract's headline is that 93% of burial-mound segments in the external southern dataset are identified, at the cost of 42 false positives to be checked and two segments missed — our carried-versus-oracle question posed across geography rather than across map sheets, scoped by the abstract to areas 'in a similar geomorphological and archaeological setting', with the discussion adding that at a different spatial resolution the fitted model is unusable and must be refitted.

The second is metric hygiene, where the paper is both witness and warning. Table 1 reports sensitivity, false-positive rate and overall accuracy; accuracy reads 1 for six of the twelve configurations, whose sensitivities range from 0.59 to 0.95, because roughly 10,600 negative segments swamp the arithmetic. Niculiță says as much in the discussion — overall accuracy is 'not necessarily a good measure in practice' — yet no precision-bearing metric is ever reported: no F1 and no Matthews correlation coefficient appear anywhere, and precision surfaces once in the prose but never as a number. Recomputed from the headline row's own confusion matrix (TP 25, TN 10,540, FP 42, FN 2), the operating point is precision 0.37, F1 0.53 and MCC 0.59 — our arithmetic, not his. Sensitivity is also relative to the 27 burial-mound segments that reach the classifier (Table 1's TP plus FN), not the 29 mounds mapped in the south, which puts end-to-end recall nearer 25/29, about 86% — again our arithmetic.

The reference data is unusually strong: mounds delineated on the DEM were then visited, and the field check overturned only two — though the author warns that mounds of different origin converge morphologically, so shape alone cannot fix what built one.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Archaeological prospection prior art, and specifically its non-CNN branch: the same target class as ours — burial mounds — but recovered as convex landforms in a LiDAR DEM rather than as cartographic symbols on a scanned map, which puts it at the far end of our difficulty ladder in substrate (real topography, not a draughtsman's convention) while sharing our imbalance regime and our cascade shape. Its primary use to us is as the attested cost of a classical machine-learning mound detector under genuine external validation in an adjacent block of the same geomorphological and archaeological setting, and as a metric-hygiene exhibit: a headline of sensitivity 0.93 — over the 27 burial-mound segments that reach the classifier, not the 29 mounds mapped in the south — with false-positive rate 0.004 and accuracy 1 corresponds, on our arithmetic from the paper's own confusion matrix, to precision 0.37 and F1 0.53 — the figure our object-level results actually have to be compared against. Secondarily it is annotation-budget prior art, since the stated deliverable is not a map but a shortlist for an operator to check, with the area to be searched reduced to a claimed 1% of the study area.
