## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Garcia-Molsosa and colleagues offer a proof of concept for extracting archaeologically relevant features from two colonial map series with deep-learning instance segmentation: the Survey of India 100 to 1-mile sheets (1:63,360) over the Multan district of Punjab in Pakistan, and the French Levant 1:50,000 series for Syria, read from British Second World War copies. Orengo is a co-author, tying the work to the satellite mound-detection line it cites (Orengo et al., 2020). For us it is the closest published relative: archaeological targets read off scanned historical maps rather than remote-sensing imagery, scored against a prior manual extraction, at map-series scale.

The architecture is where the contrast bites. Segmentation runs on Picterra, a commercial platform wrapping a U-Net, and the authors train one detector per representation rather than one classifier for the series — three for Survey of India mounds (contours and hachures in the 1900s editions, discontinuous form lines in the 1930s), one for the Latin-character toponym 'Tell', one for the composite 'L'-shaped ruins sign. Supervision is small and per detector: 71 features from 26 training areas in eight maps (D1a), 66 from 19 areas in five (D1b), 122 from 11 areas in six (D2), 39 from eight areas in three (D3), 235 from eight areas in four (D4). Multiply that ledger by the dozens of symbol families a Soviet sheet carries and the annotation budget, not the model, becomes the binding constraint a prompt-driven design is trying to remove.

The results split along the difficulty ladder we posit. On mound topography the detectors are strong — false positives at 12%, 4% and 3% of features detected, and 6, 0 and 1 manually identified features missed. On the Levant series, where the targets are text and a symbol assembled from simple 'L' strokes, false positives reach 44% and 40%. The authors attribute this to the simplicity and confusability of those signs and to the smaller Levant training set.

Three cautions travel with the numbers. Precision on the Survey of India side is bought by a post-hoc size threshold carried over from field experience in Haryana: 13,130 raw detections become 638 high-probability features above 2 ha, and the paper reports miss rates rising to 10% and 6% once that threshold is applied. The reference standard is a previous manual extraction which Table 1's footnote concedes does not reconcile with the automated output. And precision, recall and F1 are never reported as such, although the conclusions name 'counting precision and recall' as the thing to watch; the two percentage columns of Table 1 are their complements over different denominators (false positives against automatic detections, misses against manual identifications), so no single accuracy figure can be recovered from them. Costs are one working day (8 h) of training and detection for 53 maps against several weeks of manual assessment, though the authors note that preprocessing, meaning georeferencing and feature-type assessment, sits outside that figure and 'represented the most significant amount of time'. The code is unshareable because the platform is proprietary.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Historical-map extraction lineage in its most literal form — the same substrate as ours (scanned topographic map series), the same target family (mound representations), and an explicit invitation in its conclusions to carry the approach onto Soviet series — with archaeological prospection prior art as the secondary cluster, since the satellite mound-detection line stands immediately behind it in the author list. Its method is the foil our design is measured against: one trained detector per symbol representation, each with its own annotation budget, precision rescued by a size threshold carried over from fieldwork in another country (Haryana, in India, onto Multan in Pakistan), and a proprietary cloud platform standing in for the computer-science collaborator that a bespoke CNN would demand. What it gives us that no remote-sensing paper can is a difficulty ladder measured on map symbols themselves — false positives of 3–12% of automatic detections on mound topography against 40–44% on text and composite signs, though the authors attribute part of that gap to the Levant's much smaller training set (three and four training maps) rather than to symbol difficulty alone — together with the authors' own statement that map features, unlike the lidar-derived mounds of most archaeological deep learning, are irregular within a single class and therefore need far more training data.

## Key points (salience-ranked to the citing paper)

### KP1
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Rather than employing a single detector to classify the whole map series, we developed different detectors focusing on specific elements of interest. This strategy allowed us to have a more focused training process in which only a particular element per detector was tagged, avoiding confusion between classes."
- **Locator:** page_index 4 · p.5 · §2.2 CNN-based DL segmentation of features of interest in digitized historical maps
- **Paraphrase:** The authors do not build one classifier for the map series; they train a separate detector for each way a feature is drawn, so that each training set contains a single kind of mark and the classes cannot be confused. Five detectors are trained in this study. That design choice is what makes annotation cost scale with the number of symbol representations rather than with the number of maps.

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "features of interest in historical maps, even within the same object class, present inconsistent and irregular shapes. Their detection requires a much larger quantity of training data and the use of data augmentation techniques."
- **Locator:** page_index 4 · p.5 · §2 Methods: DL-based segmentation — fourth factor limiting the use of historical maps
- **Paraphrase:** The authors' own statement of why historical maps are harder than the lidar-derived topography most archaeological deep learning works on: a single object class is drawn inconsistently, so the model must learn an irregular family of shapes rather than one, which demands considerably more training data and augmentation. This is the difficulty ladder argued from the map side rather than the sensor side, and by authors with a foot in both camps.
- **Relevance:** §2 related work — positioning map-symbol detection against remote-sensing mound detection · Difficulty ladder: area segmentation to point symbols · **supports**

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Within the 47 maps of the SoI analysed, 13,130 features were identified through the DL process before using the size threshold, 322 features in the early 1900s maps and 12,808 in the 1930s maps. Applying the size threshold resulted in 638 high probability features (162 in the early 1900s maps and 476 in the 1930s maps)."
- **Locator:** page_index 7 · p.8 · §3 Results
- **Paraphrase:** Before thresholding, the deep-learning process returns 13,130 features across the 47 Survey of India sheets; a size filter cuts that to the 638 treated as high probability, and those 638 are the only ones evaluated in Table 1. The threshold is 2 ha, chosen from field experience in Haryana where map features of roughly 200 m diameter correlated with artefact scatters while smaller ones typically did not — a calibration carried across a national border, from Indian fieldwork onto the Pakistani sheets of the test area. It also costs recall: the paper reports post-threshold miss rates of 10% for the early 1900s detectors and 6% for D2.
- **Relevance:** §4 architecture; §6 transfer — carried versus oracle operating points · Calibration transfer and operating points — a threshold carried from a small field sample to a larger deployment · **complicates**

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Manual identification does not correspond exactly to automated detected features plus missed features—variation ranges from 0 (D1b and D3) to 8 (D2) cases for several reasons: missed during the manual inspection, features close to each other joined by the detectors or small features that fall under the threshold."
- **Locator:** page_index 8 · p.9 · §4 Discussion — Table 1, footnote a
- **Paraphrase:** The evaluation's reference standard is a prior manual extraction, and the paper concedes it does not reconcile with the automated results: the manual count is not simply the detector's true positives plus its misses, the gap running from 0 cases (D1b, D3) to 8 (D2). Three causes are named — features the manual inspection itself missed, adjacent features merged into one by the detector, and features falling below the size threshold. The reference is therefore fallible on its own account — the first of those causes is the manual pass missing features, and §4 adds that manual extraction made by a group of analysts is less coherent than the detectors' output where interpretations were not uniformized — and part of the remaining disagreement is a matching artefact rather than a detection error.
- **Relevance:** §3 methods — what a manually extracted reference standard can and cannot certify · Ground-truth epistemics — curator reference data versus adjudicated reference data · **complicates**

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "The use of these techniques for small areas composed by a few maps is not recommended as it will be difficult to obtain enough training data to develop an efficient detector and the time required to do this may exceed that which would be needed for expert-led manual detection."
- **Locator:** page_index 10 · p.11 · §5 Conclusions — the range and scale of the map series available
- **Paraphrase:** The authors set their own boundary condition: below some corpus size the method is not worth using, because assembling enough training data for a detector costs more than an expert would spend doing the extraction by hand. The floor is set by training-data acquisition, not by inference cost — which is precisely the floor a zero-training, prompted model removes.
- **Relevance:** §1 introduction; §7 cost analysis — why a training-free detector changes the economics · Cost, accessibility, and who can actually run the method · **supports**

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "The rectified maps provided maximum RMSE values of 26.8 m."
- **Locator:** page_index 4 · p.5 · §2.1 Georeferencing of high-resolution digitized historical maps
- **Paraphrase:** Georeferencing the Survey of India sheets against modern imagery, with at least 20 ground control points per map and unreliable points eliminated, leaves a maximum residual of 26.8 m. The Levant sheets, controlled from printed grid references precise only to the nearest 100 m, come in at a maximum of 65.5 m. Positional error of that order is the floor under any distance-based match between a map-derived detection and a ground reference, and it is of the same magnitude as the match radii an object-level evaluation would plausibly choose.

- **Probability as an area threshold** — What the paper calls probability is a single binary split on feature area: detections larger than 2 ha are classed high-probability, those smaller low-probability, and only the high-probability group is carried into the evaluation. Where the paper elsewhere speaks of assigning probabilities to the sites it has found, this one area cut is what it means — there is no calibrated score and no per-detection confidence. (page_index 6 · p.7 · §2.3 Map series and training of the algorithm — size-based threshold)
- **D1b is not an independent result** — Detector 1b is not a third mound detector but a narrowed re-run of Detector 1a on the same early 1900s material, restricted to contours and excluding hachures, trained to test whether more specialised detectors do better within a single map series. Its row in the accuracy table therefore cannot be read as an independent data point alongside D1a: treating the two as separate rungs on a difficulty ladder would double-count the same maps. (page_index 5 · p.6 · §2.3 Map series and training of the algorithm — detector list)
- **Independent gazetteer recall** — Beyond the project's own manual extraction, the paper reports a second and independent reference standard for the Survey of India study area: a published gazetteer of 135 archaeological sites depicted as mounds, of which the algorithm recovered all but three. This is the paper's cleanest recall statement, because the reference was compiled by others and prior to the detection work. (page_index 8 · p.9 · §4 Discussion)
- **Shared stroke vocabulary drives false positives** — The authors' diagnosis of the Levant failure is a shared stroke vocabulary rather than a shortage of signal: the same continuous black lines that draw the letters and the ruins symbol also draw many unrelated map features. The errors follow that vocabulary — most toponym false positives are similar letters or letter combinations, and the ruins symbol's simple 'L' components resemble other features. This is the map-symbol form of the confusable-distractor problem. (page_index 8 · p.9 · §3 Results — Levant series)
- **Signs are detected, not sites** — The conclusions are explicit that the object of detection is the cartographic sign, not the archaeological site: sites themselves are rarely detected, and what the method documents are proxies from which information about sites can be extracted. Any accuracy figure in the paper is therefore an accuracy against map marks, and inherits whatever relation those marks bear to the ground. (page_index 10 · p.11 · §5 Conclusions — detection and masking capacity)
- **Surveyor perception bounds recall** — Whether a site appears on the map at all was the surveyor's decision, and the authors treat that decision as highly variable between surveyors. This sets a ceiling no map-reading method can pass: features absent from the sheet cannot be recovered from it, so recall measured against a map-derived reference and recall measured against a site inventory are different quantities. (page_index 4 · p.5 · §2 Methods — third factor limiting the use of historical maps)
- **Headline site count is unreconciled** — The discussion's headline claim is 911 potential archaeological sites with probabilities assigned to how far each is likely to correspond to an actual site. The figure appears only in this summary sentence and does not reconcile with the component counts the paper reports — 638 high-probability Survey of India features plus 121 'Tell' and 325 ruins-symbol detections in the Levant come to 1,084, and no stated subtraction of false positives reaches 911. Cite the components, not the headline. (page_index 9 · p.10 · §4 Discussion — closing paragraph)
- **Timing denominator does not match the corpus** — The headline cost claim is one working day — 8 hours of training and detection — against several weeks for an experienced operator working manually, and the authors expect the per-map cost to fall as the corpus grows. The denominator is given as 53 maps, which does not match the corpus described elsewhere in the paper (47 Survey of India sheets plus 7 Levant sheets, or 54), so the per-map figure is approximate. (page_index 8 · p.9 · §3 Results — time invested)
- **Method released without code** — The data availability statement withholds the code because the training and detection ran on a proprietary cloud platform; the training data and per-detector results are released, but only to readers who create a free account on that same commercial platform. The accessibility argument the paper makes for ready-made platforms is therefore paid for with an unshareable method and a vendor-gated dataset. (page_index 10 · p.11 · Data availability statement)
- **Field validation still required** — The authors close their assessment of the method's usefulness by insisting that field validation remains necessary before any detected location can be called archaeological. The output is a prioritised hypothesis set for survey design, not a site list. (page_index 8 · p.9 · §4 Discussion)
