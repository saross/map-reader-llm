## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

By its own account the first empirical study of analysis blinding in the social and behavioural sciences, and the one randomised comparison of preregistration against a rival that D.9 must reckon with. Inside the Many-Analysts Religion Project, 120 teams answered the same two questions on the same data set (10,535 participants, 24 countries); block randomisation left 61 in a preregistration condition and 59 in an analysis-blinding condition, where teams built an executable script against data whose key outcome — well-being — had been shuffled within countries. Five teams (four merged, one self-switcher) were assigned outside the randomisation. Four preregistered Bayesian hypotheses; one survived. Blinding did not save time: strong evidence for the null (BF0- = 13.19), and in Stage 1 blinding teams took about twice as long (M = 19.11 h against 8.43 h). What blinding bought was adherence — a model-estimated deviation probability of 38% for preregistering teams against 20% for blinding (BFr0 = 71.40), and fewer aspects deviated on when they did deviate.

That is what our retrospective must answer. D.9's arc — over-baked waterfall registration, grain-not-volume, LLM-supported just-in-time re-registration — treats friction as the binding constraint; Sarafoglou et al. evidence a second route to the same end that does not touch friction, and their null on hours is a caution against assuming that a lighter-touch rigour method pays for itself in time. Two things keep the contrast in our favour. Their own scope limit: blinding needs a data manager independent of the analysts, and in very small groups they say there is often no guarantee it was done effectively; preregistrations are time-stamped. And their preferred recipe is a hybrid — preregister the study, finalise the analysis on blinded data — which is what they did themselves, and what CMT-0106's outcome-blind execution already instantiates here.

Their deviation-measurement result is the sharper gift. Two of the authors independently coded each team's deviations; agreement between the self-reported and the independently coded records was poor (ICC = .43), with 44 coder-identified deviations against 50 self-reported over 118 teams, and they judge the self-reports the more accurate record. That is D.9's "amendment count is a biased measure" counterweight with an empirical anchor, though it cuts our way only on the authors' own reading: the two records disagree in both directions rather than audit simply undercounting — independent coding found more deviations than self-report in the two commonest categories (exclusion criteria 15 against 11; included variables 15 against 9) and fewer in three others — and the independently coded data produced a stronger condition difference (BFr0 = 357.18), not a blunter one.

Where deviations land supports grain-not-volume: the blinding advantage sat in exclusion criteria and operationalisation of the independent variable — the bindings that cannot be fixed without seeing the data. Two scope limits travel with all of it: teams registered only an analysis plan, not design, sampling, or materials, and the study deliberately carries no unregistered baseline condition, so it says nothing about either method against ordinary practice.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

The evidenced alternative that D.9's just-in-time recipe must be positioned against — and, read closely, the source of that section's best supporting evidence. Sarafoglou et al. show that analysis blinding buys adherence rather than time (model-estimated deviation probability 38% against 20%, BFr0 = 71.40; strong evidence for the null on hours worked, BF0- = 13.19), yet concede that in very small research groups there is often no guarantee that blinding has been done effectively and recommend a hybrid — preregister the study, finalise the analysis on blinded data — that this project already performed once in CMT-0106. Their measurement result, that self-reported deviations outnumber independently coded ones (ICC = .43; 50 against 44), gives the "amendment count is a biased measure" counterweight the empirical anchor it currently lacks.
