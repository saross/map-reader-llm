## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Van Miltenburg, van der Lee, and Krahmer make the case for preregistration in NLP and, more usefully for us, supply the forms. It is a NAACL-HLT 2021 position paper: it rehearses Munafò et al.'s (2017) manifesto for reproducible science, observes that NLP has taken up most of its recommendations — methodology tutorials, preprints and open review, Dodge et al.'s (2019) reproducibility checklist, data statements, model cards, datasheets — and that preregistration alone is rarely used, then spends the rest of the paper closing that one gap. The rarity claim is scoped rather than sweeping: a keyword search of the ACL Anthology for 'preregister' or 'preregistration' turned up four papers, two of them by authors of this paper (footnote 2, page_index 1).

Three things bear directly on us. The disciplinary licence comes first: Appendix A.3 asserts that an NLP engineering experiment is a social-science experiment in which the subjects are models and the performance data is model output, so the standard question bank transfers with little modification. Their engineering form asks for goal, hypotheses or key assumptions, (in)dependent variables and how they will be measured, conditions, software libraries, hardware, parameter settings, dataset and train/val/test split, and — item 13(a) — "what metric(s) (including implementation) will you use, and how will they be configured?". Read as a checklist against our registration, most of that 15-item list has a counterpart — registered hypotheses, an analyses register, metric specification at implementation-and-configuration grain — though the items that presuppose a trained model (parameter settings, train/val/test split) have no clean analogue in a zero-shot evaluation. Second, the data-availability ruling: §A.1 concedes it is unclear what 'the data' is in NLP, distinguishes three kinds (training/validation/test sets, model output, human judgments), and answers its own "So what should we do?" with "Still preregister!" — legitimate where the corpus already exists provided system outputs and evaluation scores do not, with transparency about data sharing letting the timeline before and after registration be reconstructed. Third, deviations: §2 treats forced changes as normal so long as the change and its reasons are specified in the final report, and §4 holds registered reports to the same standard.

What they do not do is execute any of it. There is no case study; the appendix calls its forms preliminary, a starting point for discussion, and leaves their completeness and fit to future work. Their caveats travel with the recommendation — flexibility gaps, unpreventable fraud, and limited applicability (§7, after Van 't Veer and Giner-Sorolla 2016), Szollosi et al.'s (2020) point that transparency is not theory development, and their own closing caveat that there is "no silver bullet to cure sloppy science". One structural gift: §5 recommends that, where collecting more data is feasible, a positive result be followed with a pre-registered replication (collecting a new test set being one way), to establish how far trained models generalise to unseen data — the shape of a gold-standard-then-deployment design, arrived at as a remedy for pre-existing data rather than as calibration transfer.
