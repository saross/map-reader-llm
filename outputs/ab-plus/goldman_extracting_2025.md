## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Goldman and colleagues report the Artificial Intelligence for Critical Mineral Assessment (AI4CMA) competition — a 12-week challenge run in 2022 by the US Geological Survey with DARPA, the Jet Propulsion Laboratory and MITRE, written up in 2025. Two tasks: fully automated georeferencing of scanned geological and topographic maps, and extraction of features named in each map's legend. It is a competition report rather than a method paper — winning algorithms are described only by family — but for our difficulty ladder it is the historical-map extraction lineage's most useful source of numbers, because it scores three feature geometries on one corpus under one protocol.

The ladder is the finding. On the 32-map evaluation set — 8,772 polygon, 456 line and 792 point features — the best median F1 was 0.774 for polygons, 0.576 for lines and 0.350 for points. Three different teams produced those bests, so no single system spanned the ladder: polygons went to a modified U-Net, lines to a transformer detector, and points to classical image cross-correlation rather than any learned model. Point symbols — our object class — scored lowest of the three geometries by a wide margin and were the one deep learning handled worst.

Two comparability cautions travel with that 0.350. Scoring is pixel-level: teams submitted a binary presence raster per legend feature and F1 came from predicted against true pixel pairs; the paper describes no match radius, and an off-centre detection is penalised twice over. Our object-level F1 at 20 m and 50 m and our tile-level F1 and MCC use more forgiving denominators; 0.350 cannot sit beside our numbers unqualified. Second, the ranking score averages the three per-type medians with double weight on polygons, so the prize-winning overall figures (0.512, 0.456, 0.444) lean on the apparently easiest geometry. The georeferencing half is off our task but carries the same lesson: the 1.1 km headline median RMSE is the winner's median not on the 100-map evaluation set but on a 44-map subset selected after the fact as those where at least one team beat the supplied 'clue' coordinate (clue median there 17.1 km); on more than half the evaluation set no team beat the clue.

The paradigm is legend-as-vocabulary: models must identify new symbol classes from the map legend rather than a fixed class list — the open-set framing we answer with a natural-language prompt, though here the 'prompt' is a box drawn round each legend item by USGS staff, per map. The diagnosis behind it is heterogeneity — no standardised symbology across map provenances and publication dates — which our standardised Soviet series largely escapes; but the paper lists other difficulties standardisation leaves untouched (greyscale sheets, indistinguishable point and line symbols, pixelated text, dense point symbols, annotations over features), so 0.350 is a poorly constrained comparator rather than a ceiling we can assume we clear. The cost baseline is stated plainly: weeks of manual vectorisation per map, and existing tools whose output needs enough correction to save no time at all.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Historical-map extraction lineage, and the closest thing that lineage has to a controlled difficulty ladder: one corpus, one scoring protocol (bar a difficulty weighting applied to polygon pixels only), three feature geometries, with point symbols last at a best median F1 of 0.350 against 0.774 for polygons — the ordering our own area-to-point ladder predicts, arrived at independently and by non-VLM methods. It is prior art for the task shape rather than the technique: the extraction is legend-driven and open-set, which is our open-vocabulary framing, but the 'prompt' is a human-drawn box per legend item per map, and the winning systems were a modified U-Net, a transformer detector and image cross-correlation. Cite it for the ladder and for the manual-cost baseline (weeks per map; existing tools that save no time after correction), but never place its 0.350 beside our object-level F1 without stating that AI4CMA scored pixels against a binary raster with no match tolerance, and that its overall prize scores double-weight polygons.

### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Accuracy was evaluated by calculating the differences between the predicted and true pixel pairs to generate an F1 score"
- **Locator:** page_index 6 · p.7 · §2.2.3 Feature extraction guidelines, metrics, and competition schedule
- **Paraphrase:** F1 is computed pixel-by-pixel between a submitted binary presence raster and the ground-truth raster for each legend feature (page_index 4: one raster per feature, 1 for presence, 0 for absence). The paper describes no object matching and no tolerance radius — the scoring notebook itself is not reproduced — so on its stated protocol a point symbol detected but off-centre is penalised twice over, its pixels counting as false positives while the reference pixels count as false negatives. How heavy that penalty is depends on the rasterised footprint of a point symbol, which the paper does not state. This makes the 0.350 incommensurable with an object-level F1 at a 20 m or 50 m match radius, and with tile-level presence/absence F1 or MCC: the denominators and the matching regime are different, not merely the models.
- **Relevance:** §3 methods / §5 discussion — comparability of published extraction F1 · Metric hygiene and reporting comparability · **complicates**

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "machine learning solutions need be able to identify new symbol classes at training time (i.e., from the map legend)"
- **Locator:** page_index 3 · p.4 · §2.2.1 Existing feature extraction tools
- **Paraphrase:** The authors argue that extraction has to learn its symbol vocabulary from the map's own legend rather than from a fixed, pre-defined class list — the open-set framing, reached from cartographic first principles rather than from the vision-language literature. In AI4CMA that framing was operationalised by hand: USGS personnel identified each legend item, drew a box around the symbol and assigned a feature identifier (page_index 3), so the per-map 'prompt' is a human annotation supplied for every feature on every map — a recurring, per-map annotation cost the paper does not price.
- **Relevance:** §2 related work — open-vocabulary framing / §4 architecture · Open-vocabulary / open-set detection framing; annotation budgets · **supports**

- **Designed evaluation distribution shift** — The georeferencing evaluation set was deliberately drawn from a harder, more deployment-realistic distribution than the data that preceded it: the authors describe its maps as more challenging than the training and validation material, and it was made up entirely (100 %) of maps taken from reports, chosen to represent the mix of map types typically used for mineral resource assessment. (page_index 1 · p.2 · §2.1.2 Georeferencing challenge dataset)
- **Clue coordinate as do-nothing baseline** — The 'clue' coordinate supplied with each map is a do-nothing baseline rather than a competing method: the root-mean-square error obtained from the clue alone stands for the case where no automated georeferencing was attempted, or where no ground control points were found. Beating the clue therefore means only doing better than not attempting the task. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §3.1 Map georeferencing challenge results and discussion)
- **One team drives the georeferencing subset** — Successes against the clue baseline are concentrated in a single system: one team beat the clue on 39 of the 44 maps in the analysed subset, while every other team beat it on between 2 and 14. The subset therefore reflects one system's reach far more than the field's. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §3.1 Map georeferencing challenge results and discussion)
- **Corpus scale behind annotation cost** — The corpus that legend-driven extraction is meant to serve is large: the National Geologic Map Database holds thousands of maps, each carrying dozens of distinct point, polyline, and polygon features. Any cost incurred per map or per legend feature is multiplied by those numbers. (page_index 3 · p.4 · §2.2.1 Existing feature extraction tools)
- **Scoring code released to entrants** — The competition's evaluation metric was handed to entrants as a runnable Jupyter notebook rather than described only in prose, so the scoring implementation was fixed and in competitors' hands before the results were produced. (page_index 4 · p.5 · §2.2.3 Feature extraction guidelines, metrics, and competition schedule)
- **Line and point wins concentrated in one team** — High polygon scores were spread across several teams, but a single team dominated both line and point extraction. The best point-symbol score is therefore one system's result rather than a level several entrants reached. (page_index 12 · p.13 · §3.2 Map feature extraction challenge results and discussion)
- **Organisers' baseline was classical** — The organisers supplied a worked baseline solution whose per-geometry recipe was entirely classical: matching points to templates with OpenCV, edge detection for lines, and colour matching for polygons, each emitting a binary raster. At the point-symbol end, the technique the competition started from was template matching rather than anything learned. (page_index 4 · p.5 · §2.2.3 Feature extraction guidelines, metrics, and competition schedule)
- **Automation needs multi-model pipelines** — The authors conclude that automating feature extraction will probably require flexible pipelines able to employ different models, rather than a single model applied across the whole task. (page_index 12 · p.13 · §3.2 Map feature extraction challenge results and discussion)
- **Mine-symbol ground truth on topographic quadrangles** — Ground truth for mine-related features on historical topographic maps was not built for the competition but taken from an existing USGS data release, Horton and San Juan's (2016) 'Prospect- and Mine-Related Features from U.S. Geological Survey 7.5- and 15-Minute Quadrangle Maps of the United States'. That portion of the reference data therefore inherits the conventions of a pre-existing national dataset rather than those of a purpose-built annotation exercise. (page_index 3 · p.4 · §2.2.2 Feature extraction challenge dataset)
- **Ground-truth verification procedure** — Reference vectors were verified by one documented check: the vectors were inspected for proper alignment against the map image, and their attributes were checked against the descriptions in the map legend. This is an alignment-and-attribute check by the curators, not an adjudication among independent annotators. (page_index 3 · p.4 · §2.2.2 Feature extraction challenge dataset)
- **GPU requirement for deep-learning entries** — Access to powerful graphics processing units (GPUs) was necessary for the deep-learning models used in the competition, making specialised hardware a precondition for those approaches. (page_index 12 · p.13 · §3.2 Map feature extraction challenge results and discussion)
- **Feature-extraction corpus split** — The feature-extraction corpus is 283 map images of areas across the United States, split into 169 for training, 82 for validation, and 32 for final evaluation — the last of these being the set the published F1 scores are computed on. (page_index 3 · p.4 · §2.2.2 Feature extraction challenge dataset)
