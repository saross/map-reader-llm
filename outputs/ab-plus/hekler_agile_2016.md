## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Hekler and six co-authors propose "agile science": a products-and-process framework for behaviour-change research, published as a Practice and Public Health Policy piece in Translational Behavioral Medicine (2016;6:317-328). Their target is the classic four-phase biomedical pipeline (discovery, pilot, efficacy, effectiveness), which they argue defers questions of fit, feasibility and context to the end. Alongside it they name three shareable product types - behaviour change modules, computational models and personalisation algorithms - and a two-phase process: a generate phase producing multiple "contender" operations plus feasibility evidence, and an evaluate phase producing effect-size estimates and causal inferences. Products from both phases, failures included, go into a curated knowledge base early and often.

For the citing paper the value is the phase architecture and its vocabulary, not the behavioural-science content. Three transfers are close. Generate/evaluate is a clean antecedent for a pilot-then-production ladder: early phases explore a configuration space, later phases estimate performance under a fixed protocol. "Contender" is a ready-made label for the epistemic status of a configuration chosen on a small gold standard but not yet evaluated at deployment scale: generate-phase products "are not evidence-based", and relabelling to "an empirically supported product" waits on clinically meaningful effect sizes in the evaluate phase (page_index 8). And we read their charge that the four-phase model "ignores variations of units, treatments, outcomes, and settings until the end of the process" as the conceptual case for measuring a transfer tax rather than assuming generalisation from the calibration set.

Two caveats must travel with any citation. First, "preregistration" does not occur in the extracted text - nor do "confirmatory", "exploratory", "blind" or "transparency" (all 12 pages searched). Agile science is an antecedent for phased, adaptive design and for early artefact release; it is not a preregistration methodology and offers no mechanism for committing to analyses in advance. Second, its evidential status is minimal: it is explicitly "a first draft process (v0.1)", the abstract hedges ("If correct, agile science could enable a more robust knowledge base for behavior change."), and the case study is an ongoing suite of physical-activity projects rather than a test of the process - the summary concedes the authors are "actively working toward testing the assumptions outlined" (page_index 10). Cite it for framing and vocabulary, not as evidence that phased designs work.

The open-science overlap is the strongest. Their argument is for releasing operations - concrete operational definitions - before effect-size estimation, against a culture that treats interventions as "secret sauce". Substitute prompt configurations, system instructions and aggregation rules for "operations" and the argument transfers intact, reason included: external-validity claims need variation in who runs an operation, on what data, toward what outcome. They name GitHub and the Open Science Framework as tools that "already exist" for that sharing (page_index 8) - an infrastructural optimism worth contrasting with later adaptive-preregistration work. One caution for us: their sunk-cost argument is a case for staying willing to change course when warranted (page_index 10) - it cuts toward logging deviations, not toward taking them undocumented.

- **Factorial designs within MOST** — Among the optimisation designs the paper draws from the multiphase optimisation strategy (MOST), the factorial and fractional factorial design is the one presented as a resource-efficient way to examine both the main effect of each intervention component and the interaction effects among components. The characterisation is the authors summarising a cited source (reference 15) rather than a result of their own. (page_index 1 · p.2 · Evidence-based practice — optimisation methods)
- **Early studies test feasibility** — Reporting from work it cites rather than from its own data, the paper holds that early studies often test the feasibility of methods — recruitment, randomisation, retention, and assessment — rather than testing hypotheses, and that a further distinction should be drawn between testing the feasibility of the operations and testing the research methods. Both claims are attributed (references 48 and 54) and the hedge "often" is the paper's own. (page_index 9 · p.10 · Future directions)
- **Parallel versus serial iteration** — The design-research finding behind the paper's parallel-development argument comes from two studies by Dow and colleagues that the paper reports rather than conducts: an earlier one on iteration and a subsequent one comparing parallel with serial development. In the subsequent study participants were randomly assigned to conditions, and those assigned to work on several ideas at once were more open to incorporating feedback and produced better end products than the single-concept group — a result about human designers, not about sampling or aggregation. (page_index 10 · p.11 · Future directions)
- **Generate-phase feasibility criteria** — Feasibility testing in the generate phase is assessed against a named seven-criterion framework adopted from Bowen and colleagues: acceptability, demand, implementation, practicality, adaptability, integration, and limited efficacy. It is explicitly feasibility "for real world use", and it sits alongside the phase's other stated purpose of producing multiple contender operations across all three product types. (page_index 6 · p.7 · Agile science process — generate phase)
- **Criteria for a good module** — Four adjectives — smallest, meaningful, self-contained, and repurposable — are offered as the criteria for a good module, and of these only "smallest" is treated as needing justification: striving for small modules is what enables repurposability. The modules being defined are behaviour-change intervention components, so the criteria are stated for intervention design rather than for software artefacts in general. (page_index 4 · p.5 · Agile science product types — behaviour change modules)
- **Incentives, not tooling** — Where the paper names a barrier to early-and-often sharing, it is incentives rather than infrastructure: such sharing, and particularly the sharing of generate-phase products, is not well incentivised in the current research enterprise. This is the counterpart to the paper's claim that the tools for sharing already exist (page_index 8), so the bottleneck it identifies is cultural and economic rather than technical. (page_index 10 · p.11 · Future directions)
- **Funding and competing interests** — The work was funded primarily by a grant from the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation (principal investigator Hekler, grant 71995), with the example projects funded by the National Science Foundation and the National Heart Lung and Blood Institute. The first author declares a research grant from Google; the six co-authors declare no conflicts of interest. (page_index 10 · p.11 · Compliance with ethical standards)
