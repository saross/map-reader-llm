## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Guyot, Hubert-Moy and Lorho detect Neolithic burial mounds in the megalithic landscape of Carnac (Brittany) from airborne LiDAR, and their contribution is a representation more than a classifier: a Multi-scale Topographic Position (MSTP) composite stacking maximum deviation from mean elevation over micro (1–10 m), meso (10–100 m) and macro (100–1000 m) window ranges into an RGB image for visual interpretation. The 120-tree Random Forest is fitted not on that composite but on the underlying three-band stack of signed maximum-deviation values, whose sign records whether a pixel dominates or is dominated by its neighbourhood. It is the LiDAR and Random Forest counterpart to the CNN prospection prior art we cite: our target class, burial mounds, but a different substrate (an elevation model, not a scanned map), a different unit of analysis (the pixel, not the symbol), and a different object (a surviving landform, not a cartographer's sign for one).

The headline numbers are pixel-level and near-ceiling: Cohen's kappa 0.98, with precision and recall of 0.98 for the “burial mound” class, from a matrix of 2,952 true-positive, 41 false-positive, 46 false-negative and 22,126 true-negative cells. Those cells are pixels from a random 70/30 split of 80,000 signatures taken from 50 sample squares of 100 m² inside one 1 km² sub-area, 12 per cent of them — six squares, on our arithmetic — labelled “burial mound”. Training and evaluation pixels are neighbours within the same fifty squares, and on the positive side within the same handful of monuments; what is measured is pixel purity on known ground, not detection of unknown objects.

The genuine generalisation result is given in prose, never as a metric: trained on Kerlescan and applied to the unseen Lann Granvillarec sub-area, the model recovered five of the six monuments known there to the French Regional Archaeological Service, at probability above 0.9. The decisive error on each side is epistemic rather than perceptual, which is what makes the paper useful to our curator-versus-adjudicated distinction. The miss is a barrow destroyed by house construction that remains in the reference database; of the other high-probability zones, three are construction of the past thirty years and one, on ground check, proved a previously unknown monument, now registered as the Tumulus of Coët Cougam. A curator's register both retains destroyed features and omits real ones — and most positions in it, the conclusions note, are land parcel centroids of variable accuracy, an empirical warrant for generous match radii.

Class separability was so high, once the multi-scale transform was in place, that the authors called the remaining challenge fairly simple and declined to evaluate a CNN at all; the near-ceiling score is credited by its own authors to feature engineering and topographic prominence, not to classifier strength. Their stated objective was a straightforward method reproducible by archaeologists, avoiding the high-performance computing and hyperparameter search deep learning demands — the accessibility argument that also motivates a prompted vision-language model, reached from the opposite direction: they simplify the model, we remove the training corpus.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Archaeological prospection prior art in the strict sense — the same target class as ours, burial mounds, detected automatically — but in LiDAR-derived elevation data and evaluated per pixel rather than per object, which places it on a different rung of our difficulty ladder: a surviving landform read from terrain, not a conventional sign read from a map sheet. Its two main uses to us are as the Random Forest counterweight to the CNN prospection line, showing what a deliberately simple pipeline achieves on a small annotation budget, and as a unit-of-analysis caution, since the 0.98 figures are pixel-level results from a random hold-out of pixels drawn from the same fifty labelled squares used for training, while the only object-level score in the paper is five of six known monuments recovered in an unseen sub-area. Secondarily it is a ground-truth epistemics source: the destroyed-but-recorded barrow, the apparent false positive that proved a real monument, and the parcel-centroid site positions each speak to our curator-versus-adjudicated reference data and to our choice of match radii.

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "Rather than highlighting individual topographic anomalies, the multi-scalar approach allows archaeological features to be examined not only as individual objects, but within their broader spatial context."
- **Locator:** page_index 0 · p.1
- **Why:** The paper's core move in one line: read the context around a feature rather than the feature alone. It frames our own bet that a map tile carries more than the symbol under inspection — neighbouring signs, contours, legend conventions — but the analogy has to be handled honestly, because their context is engineered by multi-scale windowing over an elevation model, while ours arrives latent in a pretrained model's single pass over an image. The shared claim is that isolated-anomaly detection is the weaker framing; the difference is where the context comes from, and only one of the two is under the analyst's explicit control.
