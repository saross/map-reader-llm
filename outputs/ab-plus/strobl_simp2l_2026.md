## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Strobl, Chalmers and Henninger propose SIMP2L, a three-part template making a simulation study's whole life-cycle transparent: a Statement of Intent completed — and optionally preregistered — at t1, before piloting; a Piloting Documentation with a spreadsheet, maintained at t2 and updated through the writing and review loop; and a Final Documentation completed at t3, which they place at manuscript acceptance. It incorporates most of the ADEMP-based template of Siepe et al. (2024) but adds code validity checks, aggregation guidance, reporting of all explored conditions, oracle-condition disclosure, and generative-AI use.

The diagnosis, more than the template, earns this source its place. They argue that exploitable researcher degrees of freedom (RDF) in empirical work bite mainly at the analysis stage, whereas in simulation research they bite at design and piloting, because a researcher can add or remove data-generating processes (DGPs) cheaply — "cherry tree planting" rather than cherry picking — so the main threat is selective reporting of DGPs and outcome measures, not irreproducibility within a given DGP, given enough replications. That maps onto a VLM detection pipeline better than the empirical-psychology preregistration literature does: our exploitable freedom sits in which prompts, pass counts, aggregation rules, thresholds and match radii ever reach a reported table, not in the statistics applied afterwards. Their remedy is a piloting record — an open-lab-notebook analogue — listing every explored setting with reasons for inclusion or exclusion, plus a criterion for questionable exclusions: those that systematically change the perceived performance of the methods.

Three items land directly on our reporting. Oracle conditions — their term for conditions where a method is informed by DGP properties unknown for empirical data — can still be scientifically valuable, as benchmarks or to isolate a property, but must be made transparent: a name and a rationale for our oracle operating points. A-priori code-validity expectations are asked for because HARKing can happen involuntarily when we rationalise a bug to ourselves. And they advise against aggregating across DGPs without accounting for their properties, recommending meta learning — results described conditional on those properties — which on our reading is a live hazard for F1 and MCC pooled over heterogeneous map sheets.

Two limits travel with the source. SIMP2L is preregistration-optional: part 1 "can be used" for preregistration in the spirit of Siepe et al. (2026), but they hold documentation may achieve the discoverability goal more realistically, particularly for novices. And they concede the template is fakeable — a pragmatic step towards transparency, version 1.0, not a mechanism for enforcing it — naming version-controlled code from project start, with milestone indicators and recorded reasons for design changes, as an alternative offering "more rigorous control" that may discourage some users, particularly novices — a route their own Part 2 nonetheless accepts in place of the spreadsheet (page_index 29). Nothing here is empirically evaluated: the template is illustrated with a running regression example, not tested, and its scope is simulation-based methodological research, so any transfer to a VLM evaluation is ours to argue, not theirs.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: preregistration and open-science methodology, in its simulation-methodology branch — a close analogue in the metascience literature to a study whose exploitable degrees of freedom live in design and configuration rather than in analysis. Strobl et al. supply three things the preregistration retrospective can borrow outright: a disclosure norm and a vocabulary for oracle conditions, a file-drawer argument for reporting every explored configuration with reasons, and a criterion separating defensible from questionable exclusions; they also supply one caution, against aggregating results across heterogeneous conditions without conditioning on their properties. They are, however, deliberately preregistration-optional and admittedly fakeable, and they name version-controlled code with milestone indicators as a more rigorous route set beside their own; their Part 2 explicitly accepts a version-controlled repository tracked from the project's start instead of the piloting spreadsheet (page_index 29), so a git-tracked repository with an errata log and recorded deviations is a route the template sanctions rather than a departure from it.

## Key points (salience-ranked to the citing paper)

### KP1
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Oracle conditions in simulation studies can still be scientifically valuable, for example as benchmarks or to isolate specific properties of a method, but should be made transparent."
- **Locator:** page_index 6 · p.7 · §2.2 Part 2 of the template: "Piloting Documentation" — Honest Reporting
- **Paraphrase:** Conditions in which a method is handed information that would be unavailable in a real application are not disallowed — they can serve as benchmarks or isolate a property — but they must be disclosed. The authors define such oracle conditions as "any simulation conditions in which one or more methods are informed by properties of the data generating process (DGP) that would not be known for empirical data" (footnote 1, page_index 1; repeated in the template item, page_index 30), and give as their reason that using DGP information to select a model "may therefore yield overly optimistic performance estimates", so that if this is not made transparent "readers might overestimate the performance of the method(s)" (page_index 30).
- **Relevance:** § methods — operating-point transfer (carried versus oracle) and § preregistration retrospective · Oracle-informed operating points: an established disclosure norm and a name for them · **supports**

### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Compared to the "cherry picking" observed in the analysis stage of empirical research, this means simulation researchers are able to conduct what could be termed "cherry tree planting" in the design stage."
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2 · §1 Introduction
- **Paraphrase:** Where empirical researchers exploit the freedom available when analysing a fixed dataset, simulation researchers can cheaply add or remove whole data-generating processes, so their equivalent abuse happens earlier, when the study is being designed. The authors draw the consequence in the next sentence: the main threat in comparative simulation research is not a lack of reproducibility within a DGP — as long as the number of replications is high enough — but selective reporting of DGPs and outcome measures that favour a preferred method.
- **Relevance:** § preregistration retrospective — what the registration had to bind, and when · Where the exploitable degrees of freedom actually live in a configured pipeline study · **supports**

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Because not reporting conditions that were explored during piloting is the simulation analogue of the file drawer problem in empirical research, we argue that all explored DGPs, settings, methods, and outcome metrics should be documented together with the reasons for including or excluding them from the final study."
- **Locator:** page_index 5 · p.6 · §2.2 Piloting, writing and revision
- **Paraphrase:** Silently dropping conditions tried during piloting is the design-stage counterpart of the file drawer, so every explored data-generating process, setting, method and outcome metric should be recorded with the reason it was kept or dropped. The authors are careful that this is not an indictment of piloting itself, which they call legitimate and necessary — their example is that it makes no sense to compare the power of tests only in settings where every test achieves 100% power.
- **Relevance:** § preregistration retrospective — recorded deviations and the configuration sweep record · Explored-but-unreported configurations: the file-drawer analogue at the design stage · **supports**

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Accordingly, it makes perfect sense to report aggregated results across all data sets sampled from one DGP. However, it does not make sense to aggregate results across DGPs without accounting for the properties of these DGPs"
- **Locator:** page_index 3 · p.4 · §2.1 Early stages of planning a simulation study
- **Paraphrase:** Pooling within a condition is fine once the raw distributions have been checked for skewness and anomalies caused by programming errors, because the individual datasets are a random sample from that condition's generating process; pooling across conditions is not, because the set of conditions cannot be sampled randomly or representatively — there is no definable population of them. Their proposed remedy is meta learning, which they define broadly as any description of results conditional on the properties of the generating process, and the same advice reappears as a template instruction: "We generally advise against aggregating results across conditions or outcome metrics" (page_index 32).
- **Relevance:** § methods — evaluation protocol (pooled tile- and object-level F1 and MCC) and § limitations · Metric hygiene: aggregation across heterogeneous conditions without conditioning on their properties · **complicates**

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "The difference between these two scenarios is that in the first one, but not in the second, the decision to leave out results is made in a way that leads to systematic changes in the perceived performance of the methods."
- **Locator:** page_index 6 · p.7 · §2.2 Piloting, writing and revision
- **Paraphrase:** Not every unreported condition is a questionable practice: the line falls between exclusions that systematically shift how well the compared methods appear to perform and exclusions that do not. The two scenarios being contrasted are trying many competitors and metrics and reporting only the combination that flatters a favourite method (questionable) versus discarding settings in which every method reaches 100% power (unproblematic).
- **Relevance:** § preregistration retrospective — justifying configuration exclusions · Which dropped conditions are reportable housekeeping and which are questionable practice · **supports**

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "HARKing can happen involuntarily not only for hypotheses adressing the research question, but also for making the results of programming bugs plausible to ourselves."
- **Locator:** page_index 4 · p.5 · §2.1 Part 1 of the template: "Statement of Intent" — Code Validity Checks
- **Paraphrase:** Hypothesising after results are known can happen without intent, and not only about the research question: a researcher can also talk themselves into a post-hoc story that makes buggy output look sensible. The authors' remedy is a registration item — state in advance the conditions whose behaviour is already known from theory or prior work, and state how the results would show the expectation was refuted, since a refuted expectation can indicate a programming error. (The source spells "adressing"; the quote preserves it.)
- **Relevance:** § preregistration retrospective — what a registration for a software pipeline should have carried · A-priori expectations as pipeline-bug detection, registered alongside the hypotheses · **extends**

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Alternative ways of documentation include version-controlling the simulation code from the beginning of a project together with milestone indicators and documented reasons for design changes. While such approaches provide more rigorous control, they may also discourage some users, particularly simulation novices, from adopting transparent practices."
- **Locator:** page_index 8 · p.9 · §2.4 Is the template bulletproof?
- **Paraphrase:** The authors name a stricter alternative to their own template — version-controlling the code from the project's start, with milestone markers and recorded reasons for every design change — and hedge it only on adoption grounds: such approaches provide "more rigorous control" but "may also discourage some users, particularly simulation novices, from adopting transparent practices". They do not decline the route. Part 2 of the template offers it in place of the piloting spreadsheet — "Instead of using the spreadsheet, it is also possible, for example, to document the piloting stage by tracking your simulation code in a version-controlled repository from the very start of the project, together with comments explaining the reasons for in- or exclusion of design choices" — and asks authors to "document the location of this repository here" (page_index 29). The hedge sits inside their admission that a template cannot guarantee honest preregistration or complete reporting, both being easy to fake; they therefore offer SIMP2L as "a pragmatic step towards greater transparency rather than a mechanism for enforcing it".
- **Relevance:** § preregistration retrospective — the errata log and recorded-deviations apparatus · Execution mechanism: a git-tracked registration trail as the template's own sanctioned alternative to the piloting spreadsheet · **extends**
