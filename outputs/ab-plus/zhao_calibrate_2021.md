## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Zhao, Wallace, Feng, Klein and Singh show that few-shot in-context learning with GPT-3 is unstable in ways unrelated to the model's competence, then propose a data-free correction. A prompt has three components — format, training examples, and their permutation — and each moves accuracy substantially. On 4-shot SST-2 with GPT-3 2.7B, holding the example set fixed and reordering it moves accuracy from near chance (54.3%) to near state-of-the-art (93.4%). The instability does not wash out with more data: variance stays high at 16 examples, and extra examples can hurt (DBPedia 0-shot to 1-shot, mean 36.0% to 25.9%). The diagnosis is three biases — majority label, recency, and common token — whose net effect the authors characterise as a shift in the output distribution, not a loss of discriminative signal. The remedy, contextual calibration, estimates that shift by running a content-free input ("N/A", "[MASK]", the empty string, averaged) through the same prompt and fitting a diagonal affine transform that makes its prediction uniform across answers. It costs a few extra queries per prompt, needs no labelled data, and generally raises mean and worst-case accuracy across the eleven datasets (twenty-two rows) of Table 1 — classification, fact retrieval, slot filling — on three sizes of GPT-3, and on GPT-2 1.5B in Table 4.

For us the paper is more useful as diagnosis than method. Our proposer emits structured detections, not probabilities over a closed label set, so the transform does not port; what ports is the finding that a systematic, prompt-induced shift can be measured and removed without extra ground truth. Two results prefigure our operating-point work. Figure 5 shows a sentiment prompt whose default 50% threshold produces frequent false positives, while an oracle threshold of p(Positive) = 0.68 would give 94% validation accuracy: the ranking was largely intact, and the decision point was wrong — our carried-versus-oracle framing at the scale of one prompt. Section 5.2 then finds contextual calibration "surprisingly close" to an oracle calibration fitted on the validation set, the same shape as our transfer taxes, though read off Figure 8 for AGNews on GPT-3 175B alone and unquantified. Footnote 4 is worth carrying into our terminology: their "calibration" means removing a bias in the output distribution, explicitly not statistical calibration in the confidence-accuracy sense our verbalised-confidence thread uses.

Three cautions. Table 1's standard deviations are over five random training sets at a fixed format, so they understate total prompt variance, which elsewhere spans formats and permutations too. The headline "up to 30.0% absolute" is §5.1's claim about "average and worst-case" accuracy, and the abstract's about GPT-3 and GPT-2 jointly; it matches AGNews GPT-3 175B 0-shot exactly (43.9 to 73.9), but two Table 1 cells and two Table 4 GPT-2 cells beat it, so it is the paper's phrase, not a ceiling — our arithmetic, not theirs. And gains concentrate where baselines are weak: by our count nine of Table 1's eighty-eight GPT-3 cells fall after calibration, mostly by under a point, and mostly from already-high baselines.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: few-shot / in-context learning and annotation budget — the paper that isolated example ordering as a first-order source of variance rather than a nuisance, and that — unlike the past work on prompt format and example choice it cites in §7 — analysed why the instability occurs; that is the empirical warrant for our repeated-sampling design and for treating any single-configuration score as an estimate with a spread. Secondarily it is a calibration-transfer antecedent: its Figure 5 threshold observation and its Figure 8 oracle comparison are our carried-versus-oracle operating points in miniature, one prompt and one dataset at a time. The distance to guard is that its remedy assumes a probability vector over a closed label set and its "calibration" explicitly excludes the confidence-accuracy sense we use elsewhere, so we should cite the diagnosis and the oracle framing rather than the transform.

## Key points (salience-ranked to the citing paper)

### KP1
- **Quote (verbatim):** "varying the permutation of the training examples can cause accuracy to go from near chance (54.3%) to near state-of-the-art (93.4%)"
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3 · §3 Accuracy Varies Highly Across Prompts
- **Paraphrase:** With the prompt format and the four training examples held fixed, reordering them alone swings GPT-3 2.7B's SST-2 accuracy across roughly 39 points, from barely above chance to near the state of the art. The number is the extreme of a distribution, not a typical gap: Figure 2's setting is ten random 4-shot example sets with every permutation of each evaluated. The authors' point is that ordering — normally an afterthought in supervised learning — can matter as much as, or more than, which examples are chosen.
- **Relevance:** §4 architecture — why a single prompt configuration's score is not a stable estimate · Few-shot / in-context learning — prompt-order sensitivity and configuration variance · **supports**

### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "we compare its accuracy to an "oracle calibration" method that uses the validation set to find the best possible diagonal W. We evaluate this oracle on AGNews, and find that contextual calibration is surprisingly close to it (Figure 8)."
- **Locator:** page_index 6 · p.7 · §5.2 Ablations on Contextual Calibration
- **Paraphrase:** A calibration fitted with no labelled data comes close to an oracle calibration that is allowed to fit the same diagonal transform on the validation set — a data-free operating point approaching an oracle one. The claim is narrow and should travel with its scope: one dataset (AGNews), GPT-3 175B per the Figure 8 caption, and "surprisingly close" read off a plot rather than quantified anywhere in the text.
- **Relevance:** §5 results — transfer taxes and the cost of having no held-out labels at deployment · Calibration transfer — carried versus oracle operating points · **extends**

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Since the default 50% threshold is used to make predictions, this results in frequent false positives. Importantly, note that if we could optimally set the classification threshold (p(Positive) = 0.68 in this case), the classifier would be highly accurate (94% on the validation set)."
- **Locator:** page_index 4 · p.5 · §4 What Causes the High Variance? — The Impact of Biases on Model Predictions
- **Paraphrase:** For the sentiment prompt visualised in Figure 5 the model's scores separate the classes well; what fails is the decision rule. The default 0.5 cut yields frequent false positives, whereas a cut at 0.68 would reach 94% on the validation set. Both the threshold and the 94% are oracle quantities chosen on that same validation data, for one prompt on one dataset — a ceiling a perfectly transferred threshold would reach, not an achieved operating point.
- **Relevance:** §5 results — carried versus oracle operating points and the transfer tax · Calibration transfer — operating-point selection and threshold sweeps · **supports**

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "This goal is distinct from statistical calibration (Brier, 1950; Guo et al., 2017), i.e., aligning a model's confidence estimate with its true accuracy."
- **Locator:** page_index 4 · p.5 · §5 Contextual Calibration — footnote 4
- **Paraphrase:** The authors state plainly that their "calibration" is not calibration in the confidence-accuracy sense. Their target is a bias in the output distribution, corrected by an affine transform so that a content-free input scores uniformly across answers; whether the resulting probabilities track empirical accuracy is neither claimed nor measured.
- **Relevance:** §2 related work and §3 terminology — disambiguating our two senses of 'calibration' · Calibration / verbalised confidence — terminological hygiene · **complicates**

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "We use labeling budgets of 0–8 examples"
- **Locator:** page_index 5 · p.6 · §5.1 Results for Contextual Calibration
- **Paraphrase:** The main sweep — Table 1, all datasets and model sizes — runs at zero to eight shots; Figures 1 and 8 add a 16-shot point for AGNews and DBPedia. The stated reason is not annotation cost but inference cost: the authors say going beyond eight shots made querying the OpenAI API prohibitively expensive. In 2021, on this model family, the binding constraint on shot count was the API bill rather than the labelling effort — a distinction our own annotation-budget framing has to keep separate, since the two costs scale differently and are paid by different people.
- **Relevance:** §5 discussion — what a shot budget buys, and which cost actually binds · Annotation budgets and the cost/accuracy trade-space · **complicates**

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "The variance remains high even when we use 16 training examples. Moreover, adding more training examples can sometimes hurt accuracy (e.g., mean accuracy drops from 36.0% to 25.9% for DBPedia 0-shot to 1-shot)."
- **Locator:** page_index 2 · p.3 · §3 Accuracy Varies Highly Across Prompts — the variance persists with more data and larger models
- **Paraphrase:** Buying more in-context examples does not reliably buy stability, and can cost accuracy: on DBPedia the mean falls from 36.0% at 0-shot to 25.9% at 1-shot. The sentence does not name the model size; Table 1's DBPedia GPT-3 2.7B row carries exactly those two figures. The authors attribute this particular drop to majority-label bias — the model repeating the class of the single example it was shown.
- **Relevance:** §5 discussion — budget-scaling assumptions in few-shot prompting · Annotation budgets — non-monotonic returns to example count · **complicates**

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "For classification tasks, ˆp is the set of probabilities that are associated with each label name, renormalized to one."
- **Locator:** page_index 4 · p.5 · §5 Contextual Calibration
- **Paraphrase:** The transform operates on a probability vector over a small, closed set of label tokens; for generation tasks it operates on the first-token distribution, and only the first token is calibrated. It therefore presupposes a task shaped as next-token classification with API access to output probabilities — conditions a vision-language model emitting structured detections over an image does not meet. The diagnosis transfers to our setting; the remedy does not.
- **Relevance:** §4 architecture — why we borrow the diagnosis rather than the transform · Few-shot / in-context learning — what transfers from text-LM findings to VLM detection · **complicates**

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "small but non-trivial decisions such as calibration can greatly influence results. This makes it difficult to correctly develop and compare new methods"
- **Locator:** page_index 7 · p.8
- **Why:** The authors' own reason why few-shot results are hard to compare across studies: the decisions that move the numbers are small but non-trivial, and they change the answer. That is the case for preregistering a prompt configuration and an analysis plan before the numbers exist, and it is a sharper motivation for our apparatus than any methodological source arguing the point in the abstract. Used honestly it also cuts at us: our own consensus, thinking-mode, and threshold settings are exactly the class of small decision the quote indicts, which is why they are registered rather than merely reported.

## Overflow (paraphrase only; each rests on a byte-checked span)

Verified secondary material that did not fit the summary band. The verbatim spans behind these paraphrases are held in the working copy (`_work/zhao_calibrate_2021.overflow.json`), not published; page anchors are to the extracted page cache (structured 2026-09-03; model `claude-opus-5` requested).

- **Content-free input as hyper-parameter** — The correction carries a tuning choice of its own. On 1-shot SST-2 the accuracy after calibration ranges from 69.1 (using “the”) to 79.4 (using “the man.”) against an uncalibrated baseline of 66.5, so the spread across content-free inputs is of the same order as the gain over not calibrating at all; on 0-shot AGNews the range is 57.3 to 66.5 against a 48.5 baseline. The three-input ensemble the authors adopt (“N/A”, “[MASK]” and the empty string) beats each of its three members on both datasets, but on SST-2 its 79.0 is still below two single gibberish or filler inputs. (page_index 10 · p.11 · Appendix A, Table 3 — choice of content-free input)
- **Ensemble tuned on AGNews, reused** — The three-way ensemble of content-free inputs used throughout the paper was selected because it worked best on a single dataset, AGNews, and was then reused unchanged for every other dataset. The method's one free parameter is therefore fixed on a subset and carried to the rest of the corpus, so results elsewhere are gains under a transferred setting rather than a per-dataset optimum. (page_index 4 · p.5 · §5 Contextual Calibration — footnote 8)
- **Small model beats large baseline** — Calibration sometimes lets GPT-3 2.7B outperform the GPT-3 175B baseline — by up to 19.3% — despite being more than fifty times smaller. Both hedges travel with the claim: “sometimes”, with no count of how often it happens, and “up to”, marking an extreme rather than a typical case. The comparison is against the large model's uncalibrated baseline condition, not against a calibrated 175B. (page_index 5 · p.6 · §5.1 Results for Contextual Calibration)
- **Mitigates, not eliminates, prompt engineering** — The authors' own statement of what a calibration step buys: it does not remove the need to engineer prompts, but it mitigates that need by making the accuracy of the best, average and worst-case prompts more similar to one another, and higher. Calibration is offered as a variance-narrowing correction applied on top of prompt design, not as a substitute for it. (page_index 6 · p.7 · §6 Discussion — Does Calibration Eliminate the Need to Engineer Prompts?)
- **Cells beyond the 30.0 headline** — Two DBPedia cells in Table 1 gain more than the paper's headline “up to 30.0% absolute”. Reading each cell as a mean followed by its standard deviation, GPT-3 175B at 0-shot moves from a 22.0 baseline to 59.7 calibrated and GPT-3 2.7B at 1-shot from 25.9 to 61.6 — gains of 37.7 and 35.7 points by our arithmetic on the table, not figures the paper states. The aggregation basis on which 30.0 is a maximum is not given in the text, so it should be cited as the paper's phrase rather than treated as a ceiling. (page_index 5 · p.6 · §5.1 — Table 1, DBPedia rows)
- **Largest regression: GPT-2 on CB** — Calibration is not risk-free. On the CB dataset with GPT-2, 0-shot accuracy falls from a 44.6 baseline to 17.9 after contextual calibration — a drop of 26.7 points by our arithmetic, reading each cell as a mean followed by its standard deviation. It is the largest single regression in either results table, and the paper's prose does not single it out. (page_index 11 · p.12 · Appendix A, Table 4 (GPT-2) — CB row)
- **GPT-2 result scope hedge** — The scope word attached to the GPT-2 replication is “most”: the authors report that contextual calibration works out of the box for GPT-2, improving mean accuracy and reducing variance for most tasks. The hedge should travel with any citation of the GPT-2 result — the claim made is not that it helps on every task. (page_index 6 · p.7 · §5.1 Results for Contextual Calibration — Improves GPT-2)
- **Alternative parameterisation per task family** — A footnote reports a second parameterisation — setting the bias vector to the negative of the content-free probabilities and leaving the weight matrix as the identity — which empirically gives higher accuracy than the main-text solution on generation tasks, where the probability vector is high-dimensional, while the main-text solution is better for classification. The reported gains therefore rest on a parameterisation chosen per task family, a degree of freedom the main text does not foreground. (page_index 4 · p.5 · §5 Contextual Calibration — footnote 7)
- **Uncalibrated probing underestimates accuracy** — The authors' recommendation to the probing literature: studies that measure a model's factual or commonsense knowledge by asking it to complete a natural-language prompt may be underestimating its accuracy, and future work should apply contextual calibration. On that argument an uncalibrated zero-shot score is a lower bound on capability rather than a measurement of it. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §7 Related Work — Few-shot Learning with Language Models)
- **Common token bias, quantified** — Label names are not neutral. On DBPedia, a balanced fourteen-way topic classification task, GPT-3 predicts the “book” class eleven times more often than the “artist” class, and there is a moderate correlation (r = 0.67) between how frequent a label name is and how often its class is predicted. The bias attaches to the wording of the classes, so a labelling scheme can induce it independently of the data. (page_index 4 · p.5 · §4 What Causes the High Variance? — Common Token Bias)
- **Majority label bias in generation** — Majority-label bias appears in generation as well as classification: on the 4-shot LAMA validation set with GPT-3 2.7B, 50.2% of the model's predictions simply repeat one of the four answers shown in the prompt, against a correct repeat rate of 24.7%. About half the outputs are prompt echoes where only about a quarter should be. (page_index 3 · p.4 · §4 What Causes the High Variance? — Majority Label Bias)
- **Recency bias by prompt position** — Recency bias is quantified for generation too: on 4-shot LAMA the training answers nearer the end of the prompt are more likely to be repeated, and the model overpredicts the answer from the first, second, third and fourth training example by 8.5%, 8.3%, 14.3% and 16.1% respectively. The effect is roughly twice as large at the last position as at the first, so where an example sits in the prompt — not only what it says — shapes the output distribution. (page_index 3 · p.4 · §4 What Causes the High Variance? — Recency Bias)
