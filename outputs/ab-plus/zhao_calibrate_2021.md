## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Zhao, Wallace, Feng, Klein and Singh show that few-shot in-context learning with GPT-3 is unstable in ways unrelated to the model's competence, then propose a data-free correction. A prompt has three components — format, training examples, and their permutation — and each moves accuracy substantially. On 4-shot SST-2 with GPT-3 2.7B, holding the example set fixed and reordering it moves accuracy from near chance (54.3%) to near state-of-the-art (93.4%). The instability does not wash out with more data: variance stays high at 16 examples, and extra examples can hurt (DBPedia 0-shot to 1-shot, mean 36.0% to 25.9%). The diagnosis is three biases — majority label, recency, and common token — whose net effect the authors characterise as a shift in the output distribution, not a loss of discriminative signal. The remedy, contextual calibration, estimates that shift by running a content-free input ("N/A", "[MASK]", the empty string, averaged) through the same prompt and fitting a diagonal affine transform that makes its prediction uniform across answers. It costs a few extra queries per prompt, needs no labelled data, and generally raises mean and worst-case accuracy across the eleven datasets (twenty-two rows) of Table 1 — classification, fact retrieval, slot filling — on three sizes of GPT-3, and on GPT-2 1.5B in Table 4.

For us the paper is more useful as diagnosis than method. Our proposer emits structured detections, not probabilities over a closed label set, so the transform does not port; what ports is the finding that a systematic, prompt-induced shift can be measured and removed without extra ground truth. Two results prefigure our operating-point work. Figure 5 shows a sentiment prompt whose default 50% threshold produces frequent false positives, while an oracle threshold of p(Positive) = 0.68 would give 94% validation accuracy: the ranking was largely intact, and the decision point was wrong — our carried-versus-oracle framing at the scale of one prompt. Section 5.2 then finds contextual calibration "surprisingly close" to an oracle calibration fitted on the validation set, the same shape as our transfer taxes, though read off Figure 8 for AGNews on GPT-3 175B alone and unquantified. Footnote 4 is worth carrying into our terminology: their "calibration" means removing a bias in the output distribution, explicitly not statistical calibration in the confidence-accuracy sense our verbalised-confidence thread uses.

Three cautions. Table 1's standard deviations are over five random training sets at a fixed format, so they understate total prompt variance, which elsewhere spans formats and permutations too. The headline "up to 30.0% absolute" is §5.1's claim about "average and worst-case" accuracy, and the abstract's about GPT-3 and GPT-2 jointly; it matches AGNews GPT-3 175B 0-shot exactly (43.9 to 73.9), but two Table 1 cells and two Table 4 GPT-2 cells beat it, so it is the paper's phrase, not a ceiling — our arithmetic, not theirs. And gains concentrate where baselines are weak: by our count nine of Table 1's eighty-eight GPT-3 cells fall after calibration, mostly by under a point, and mostly from already-high baselines.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: few-shot / in-context learning and annotation budget — the paper that isolated example ordering as a first-order source of variance rather than a nuisance, and that — unlike the past work on prompt format and example choice it cites in §7 — analysed why the instability occurs; that is the empirical warrant for our repeated-sampling design and for treating any single-configuration score as an estimate with a spread. Secondarily it is a calibration-transfer antecedent: its Figure 5 threshold observation and its Figure 8 oracle comparison are our carried-versus-oracle operating points in miniature, one prompt and one dataset at a time. The distance to guard is that its remedy assumes a probability vector over a closed label set and its "calibration" explicitly excludes the confidence-accuracy sense we use elsewhere, so we should cite the diagnosis and the oracle framing rather than the transform.

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "small but non-trivial decisions such as calibration can greatly influence results. This makes it difficult to correctly develop and compare new methods"
- **Locator:** page_index 7 · p.8
- **Why:** The authors' own reason why few-shot results are hard to compare across studies: the decisions that move the numbers are small but non-trivial, and they change the answer. That is the case for preregistering a prompt configuration and an analysis plan before the numbers exist, and it is a sharper motivation for our apparatus than any methodological source arguing the point in the abstract. Used honestly it also cuts at us: our own consensus, thinking-mode, and threshold settings are exactly the class of small decision the quote indicts, which is why they are registered rather than merely reported.
