## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Sobotkova, Kristensen-McLachlan, Mallon and Ross set out to detect burial mounds in IKONOS satellite imagery of the Kazanlak Valley, Bulgaria, with a pre-trained ResNet-50 plus low-touch additional training, and publish the failure. It is the closest prior art we have: the same research programme, the same target class, and the same commitment to validating model output against pedestrian-survey field data — 773 mounds recorded by the Tundzha Regional Archaeological Project (TRAP) across 85 sq km in 2009–2011. The modality differs (fused panchromatic/multispectral satellite imagery, not scanned topographic maps), which is what makes the comparison informative.

The load-bearing finding is the gap between self-report and field validation. The 2021 run, trained on all 773 MOUND cutouts, reported F1 = 0.87 on its own MOUND/NOT MOUND test data; swept across 150 × 150 m tiles at a 60 per cent probability threshold it flagged 148 tiles in the study area, of which 19 (12.8 per cent) held a mound, and it located 38 of 773 known mounds — 4.9 per cent. The 2022 run, retrained on the 249 cutouts where a mound was discernible to the naked eye, reported F1 = 0.62 and did worse in the field: 288 flagged tiles, 15 of them true positives (5.2 per cent), 21 of 773 mounds (2.7 per cent). Tile containment makes those figures a ceiling rather than a floor: a mound counted as found if its ground-truth point fell anywhere inside a flagged 150 m square, and single tiles absorbed 1–9 mounds. The model was also run over both IKONOS scenes 'inclusive of training data' (p8), though the deployment grid re-tiles that imagery rather than re-scoring the mound-centred training cutouts. The terminology is local too: 'false positives (87.1%)' is the share of flagged tiles that were empty, and the 95.3 per cent 'false negative rate' is 381 of the 400 tiles that did contain mounds (definitions p9, counts p10).

The diagnosis is stated cleanly: the model keyed on bright lines and edges — forest margins, roads, shorelines — rather than round shapes, flagged parts of the Koprinka reservoir above 60 per cent in the 2022 run (p11 — the 2021 run avoided it, p10), and missed the largest royal mounds in the valley. The authors' ruling, that external validation against field data is a required part of a CNN workflow, is the antecedent our carried-versus-oracle operating points and transfer taxes formalise. Their cost figure is as scarce as it is useful: about 135 person-hours across two digital archaeologists, a machine-learning specialist and a junior developer, with the corrective retraining adding 20 h for a worse result (p15). Their comparator is human — experts, or even novice volunteers, would have been more reliable — the aside our student baseline answers with data. They also supply the frame for reading everyone else's numbers: of 70 Web of Science papers on ML/AI in archaeological remote sensing, 44 abstracts (63 per cent) mention no negative aspect at all (p6).

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Archaeological prospection prior art at the closest possible range — the same research group, the same burial-mound target, and field data from the same Bulgarian survey programme — but a pre-trained CNN over heterogeneous satellite imagery rather than a prompted VLM over standardised map symbols (a substrate contrast the source draws itself on p2, where map symbols are 'far less complex than the earth's surface as reflected in satellite imagery', though it warns that without heavy annotation investment even maps yield 'only the most consistent map features'), and its central result is negative: F1 = 0.87 on the model's own test data alongside 38 of 773 mounds found in the field. It is the paper that makes our external-validation apparatus non-negotiable rather than fastidious, and it supplies the two comparators the citing paper needs — a cost anchor (about 135 person-hours) and a human baseline the authors themselves expect to win. It also sets a comparability trap we must not walk into: its detections are scored by containment in a 150 m tile, so its percentages are not commensurable with our object-level matching at 20 m and 50 m, and any side-by-side reporting has to say so.
