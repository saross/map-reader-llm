## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Chen and colleagues apply off-the-shelf deep-learning object detectors to kurgan detection in the Altai Mountains, and the paper is the direct descendant of the prospection prior art our related-work section already leans on: Caspari is a co-author, and the introduction positions the study explicitly after Caspari and Crespo's patch classification, which “cannot precisely locate kurgans in the images”, and after Chen et al. (2021), the first CNN object detector aimed at the same target. The data are Gaofen-2 optical scenes pan-sharpened to 80 cm; the supervision is 1,087 labelled 512 × 512 samples drawn from six field-surveyed areas; the evaluation is four-fold cross-validation at a 4:1:1 split of training, validation and test data; the comparison is a thirty-row table crossing five detection algorithms with five backbones, focal loss tested on the Swin-large row of each. Transformer backbones achieve comparable or better performance than CNN backbones at similar parameter counts — the authors hedge this as holding in most cases with a few exceptions — performance rises with model size, and focal loss improves every metric in each Swin-large comparison. The best configuration, DDQ with a Swin-large backbone and focal loss, reaches AR 0.804, AP 0.681, Recall50 0.858, Precision50 0.917 and F2 0.869.

Three features matter to us more than the headline numbers. First, the paper deploys. The selected model runs over about 2,000 km2 at Dayan Lake and about 3,200 km2 in the Heiliutan Basin in roughly 32 and 55 minutes on a single RTX 2080Ti, returning 315 and 427 detections. This is the closest published analogue to our 55-map deployment, and its operating point is asserted — anything scoring above 0.5 confidence counts as a kurgan — rather than carried from a measured curve, so the paper has no transfer tax to report. Second, deployment ground truth is layered rather than binary: an archaeologist with more than twenty years of Altai experience checked every detection in Google Earth Pro and assigned confidence levels, endorsing 257 and 334 as possible kurgans, of which 49 at Heiliutan matched field data; the same review produced a list of likely misses, 96 and 135, of which 46 at Heiliutan matched field data. On our arithmetic that is nearly as many field-attested mounds missed as found, against a held-out Recall50 of 0.858 measured on curated samples of clear mounds over 10 m — not a like-for-like comparison, but the shape of a transfer tax. The paper states plainly that this validation was based mainly on visual inspection instead of field verification, and that a field check remains crucial for definitive confirmation.

Third, the metric argument runs against ours. The authors reject precision-first evaluation on a workload argument — deleting a false positive is a click, finding a false negative is a whole-scene search — and report F2, which weights recall twice as heavily as precision. Anyone importing their numbers must import that too: F2 0.869 is not an F1, and the “50” subscripts mark a confidence threshold, not an IoU threshold.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Archaeological prospection prior art in its most current form: the same object as ours, burial mounds, detected automatically, but in 80 cm optical satellite imagery as supervised bounding-box detection rather than symbol localisation on a map sheet — the modality our approach is positioned against, and the successor to Caspari and Crespo 2019 in the same research lineage. Its principal use to us is as the attested picture of what a fine-tuned detector costs and delivers at deployment scale, and as an unusually honest account of what happens when held-out metrics meet an unsurveyed region: expert adjudication with confidence levels, nearly as many field-attested mounds missed (46 at Heiliutan) as field-attested mounds found (49), and no fresh field verification behind either list — the paper's own account is that validation in the two unexplored regions was based mainly on visual inspection instead of field verification. Secondarily it is a metrics source that argues the opposite weighting to ours — F2 over F1, recall over precision, on an explicit archaeologist-workload argument — which our discussion should meet rather than ignore.

- **Quote (verbatim):** "finding mistakes in the detected objects (false positives) is much easier than identifying missed kurgans (false negatives) during the final visual checks. ## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "Instead of a time-consuming visual search in every location, by using our method, archaeologists can efficiently remove most false detections and identify missed kurgans with quick visual checks in just a few targeted key areas."
- **Locator:** page_index 14 · p.15
- **Why:** The screening-tool value proposition stated by a prospection team in 2026: the detector is not an oracle but a way of concentrating expert attention. It is the same claim our paper wants to make for a prompted VLM, which is precisely why it should be quoted with its own evidence attached — in this study the targeted key areas held 135 objects at Heiliutan that the reviewing archaeologist judged likely kurgans and the model had missed, 46 of them field-attested and the remainder awaiting on-site confirmation, and the missed ones sat within 1 km of a detection in 83.7% of cases. The hook works honestly in two directions: it supports the screening framing, and it shows that the screening framing survives only because the misses cluster near the hits.
