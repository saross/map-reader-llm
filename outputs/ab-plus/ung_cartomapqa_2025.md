## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

CartoMapQA is a SIGSPATIAL '25 benchmark asking how well vision-language models read cartographic maps — the perceptual substrate our study assumes rather than measures. Ung and colleagues build 2,251 questions over 853 OpenStreetMap-derived maps of San Jose, Manchester, and Melbourne, and evaluate fifteen models zero-shot — five open-source families, mostly in lightweight and large-scale variants, alongside GPT-4V, GPT-4o, o1, o3, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet — across six hierarchically ordered tasks: isolated map-feature semantics (MFS), presence, counting and name-listing for one feature type on full maps (STMF) and the same counting and name-listing across several types at once (MTMF), route-length estimation from a scale bar (RLE), map-marker localisation (MML), and shortest-route navigation (SRN).

Three results bear on us directly. The first is the symbol-semantics ceiling: on 463 five-option questions about map features shown in isolation, Gemini 2.5 Pro — the family our study uses, here the gemini-2.5-pro-preview-03-25 checkpoint — leads with 0.652 overall against a 0.194 random baseline, scoring 0.820 on symbols but 0.389 on areas and 0.411 on lines. That category ordering inverts our difficulty ladder, though on a different task: naming a cropped colour patch out of context is not segmenting an area on a full sheet, so it is a caution about the ladder's generality rather than a refutation.

Second, the closest analogue to our tile-level detection is STMF's presence request, where Gemini 2.5 Pro reaches accuracy 0.843 and macro-F1 0.842 — macro-averaged over both classes of the binary presence judgement, with the prevalence never stated, so not comparable to a positive-class F1 on a rare target. Counting degrades predictably: accurate at true counts of one or two, undercounting as the count rises.

Third, the failure taxonomy. Auditing half the incorrect name-listing answers from Gemini 2.5 Pro and Qwen-VL 2.5 72B, the authors find hallucination rare — one or two occurrences per model — while misrecognition of semantically adjacent types and OCR errors dominate. That argues for a verifier aimed at type confusion rather than fabrication; the OCR errors are on clean English renders, so Cyrillic on scanned sheets should be worse.

Every number here comes off an easier substrate than ours. The maps are Folium-rendered OpenStreetMap tilesets at 1,366 × 768 pixels from three English-speaking countries, chosen so that language comprehension does not influence the evaluation; ground truth is OpenStreetMap-derived, with the feature counts and names, and all generated routes, checked by a human annotator. The navigation task collapses even so: the best model, o3, matches the shortest route in 33.8 per cent of cases with 37.8 per cent connectivity, and accuracy rises with zoom level, which the authors read as reduced context helping and we read as an argument for tiling. On modality the paper is suggestive rather than decisive — it observes that some models compensate for visual weakness through stronger text-based reasoning, and characterises the earlier MapEval benchmark as finding image inputs worse than curated textual or API context.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Historical-map extraction lineage at its modern-map end: CartoMapQA measures with a benchmark what that literature attempts as an extraction pipeline — whether a model can name the symbols, count the features, and read the labels on a cartographic map — but on born-digital OpenStreetMap renders rather than scanned historical sheets, which makes every score here a best-case reference rather than a comparison. Its secondary standing is as an open-vocabulary / VLM-detection alternative: a zero-shot measurement of the perceptual substrate our consensus-and-verifier architecture is built on top of, and it reports that substrate as shaky — 0.652 overall on isolated feature semantics for the strongest model, on clean maps, in English. The most usable finding for us is the failure taxonomy rather than any single score, because it says what an adversarial verifier should be looking for: type confusion and misread text, not invention.

### KP3

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "even the best-performing models tend to produce accurate counts primarily when the ground-truth is 1 or 2, but exhibit a tendency to undercount as the true value increases"
- **Locator:** page_index 6 · p.7 · §4.3 Main results — Single-Type Map Feature (STMF), counting request
