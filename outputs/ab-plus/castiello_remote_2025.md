## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Castiello, Landauer and Saintenoy fine-tune a ConvNeXt V2 classifier, pre-trained on ImageNet, to find pre-Hispanic circular stone structures — roundhouses and corrals — in 0.5 m/pixel WorldView-2 imagery over the Azapa valley in northern Chile. Supervision comes from an existing hand-digitised photointerpretation geodatabase: 1034 circular structures, of which 813 (over ca. 182 km²) train the model and 221 (over 8.75 km²) are held out, plus approximately 8000 randomly placed negative patches; the training set was ground-truthed in the field in 2022. Detection is a dense grid search rather than a detector head: a 64 × 64 px patch is scored at every point of a 6 m grid, producing a probability surface, not boxes.

Three features make it, in our judgement, unusually close to our evaluation protocol. First, the operating point is chosen by an explicit threshold sweep: precision, recall, F1 and MCC computed at every threshold from 0 to 1 in 0.05 steps, with 0.85 optimal under both, each reaching 0.68. Second, a hit is defined by a distance tolerance — at least one above-threshold grid point within 6 m of a structure's circular boundary — the object-level matching decision we take at 20 m and 50 m, here set equal to the grid step. Third, Table 1 reports two recalls side by side on different denominators: 187 of 221 structures identified (85%) alongside grid-point precision 0.70 and recall 0.67. Reading “85% detection” and “F1 0.68” as the same quantity would be the class-relative-versus-aggregate trap in a new guise; the paper is careful enough to print both.

Two caveats travel with those numbers. The 0.85 threshold is selected on the same 8.75 km² southwest region whose metrics are reported, so 0.68 is an oracle operating point in our vocabulary, with no cost measured for carrying it to unseen ground; the authors present it instead as a dial the archaeologist sets against available fieldwork time. And the reference set is not closed: high-probability grid points in areas never surveyed turned out to contain real circular structures, verified in subsequent fieldwork and, the authors say, available to enrich the original dataset, so an unquantified share of the 324 false-positive grid points at 0.85 were gaps in the curator's list rather than model errors — the curator-versus-adjudicated ground-truth problem our own gold standard has.

The paper also supplies a lower rung of the difficulty ladder. A 64 × 64 px patch spans 32 m at 0.5 m/pixel against structures averaging 6 m across, so the procedure flags high-probability neighbourhoods rather than pinpointing individual features and seems, the authors judge, somewhat biased towards structures in close proximity, making clusters easier to find than isolated occurrences; most missed structures appeared to be in poor conservation condition, with collapsed walls and sometimes vegetation cover. Its stated advantages — efficiency with limited training data, reproducibility, potential for rapid large-area mapping — are the CNN case at its strongest; among its future-work suggestions is that newer architectures, possibly stemming from foundation models, be tried next.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Archaeological prospection prior art, and — our judgement, not a claim of this source — the closest thing in that literature to our evaluation protocol rather than to our architecture: a supervised CNN whose operating point is chosen by a threshold sweep reported in F1 and MCC together, and whose hits are scored by a distance tolerance from a structure's boundary rather than by tile overlap. Its value to us lies less in the model — ConvNeXt V2 trained on 813 hand-digitised structures, which is precisely the annotation budget a prompted approach is trying not to spend — than in the shape of that evaluation, which our own object-level numbers can be held against condition for condition. It also supplies two cautions we should state about our own reporting and not only about theirs: a threshold tuned on the region it is scored on is an oracle point, and precision computed against a curator's list understates the model wherever the list is incomplete, as these authors' own follow-up fieldwork showed.
