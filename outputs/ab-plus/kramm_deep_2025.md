## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Kramm and colleagues extract Kenya's mid-twentieth-century road network from 533 scanned topographic sheets at 1:50,000 and 1:100,000 and publish it as a Nature Scientific Data descriptor: 56,238 km of roads in three classes, openly available through the CRC/TRR228 database. It is the historical-map extraction lineage at national scale, and architecturally the supervised counterfactual to ours. Roads on these sheets are drawn with 20 distinct symbols, and because one road class is rendered differently across editions and sources, each symbol had to be trained and classified separately — 20 individual classifications for what is semantically one feature family. The authors name symbol count as the dominant cost driver, since steps 3 to 7 of the workflow repeat per symbol at several hours to two days of training each. That per-symbol tax is what a prompted VLM is meant not to pay; our corpus inverts the ratio, with burial mounds one symbol family among dozens on a comparatively homogeneous series.

Three evaluation details transfer directly. First, metric hygiene. The paper reports an average overall accuracy of 99.2% beside an average F1 of 0.84 and IoU of 0.73, and Fig. 7's average confusion matrix (page_index 10) shows why the two diverge: ground-truth road covers 2.55% of the evaluated sheet area, both datasets buffered to 20 m, so a classifier predicting no road at all would score 97.45% overall accuracy. The headline sits about 1.75 points above that baseline; F1 and IoU carry the signal — the reason we report MCC beside F1. A subtler trap sits in Table 2: its 'Average' column is not the mean of the seven per-sheet values (F1 0.87, IoU 0.78) but reproduces to two decimals from the averaged confusion matrix — as do the printed precision and recall averages and the 99.2% headline accuracy, against a per-sheet mean of 99.1. These readings are our arithmetic on the paper's numbers, not claims the paper makes.

Second, the in-training 90/10 split reports mIoU of 0.992 or higher for every trained model, against IoU 0.73 on seven independently digitised sheets. The two are not the same measurement — a mean-IoU over a 10% test split of tiles from the same maps versus a road-class score against fresh ground truth — and the paper never equates them; but the distance between them is our transfer-tax argument visible in someone else's data.

Third, candour about the human residue. The reported metrics score the uncorrected classifier output, whereas the published dataset is post-processed: roughly 25% of roads needed manual adjustment and post-processing alone took about two months. Positional accuracy is scale-dependent by an order of magnitude — RMSE 17.8 m at 1:50,000 against 245.1 m at 1:100,000 — which bears on what a match radius in metres means once a historical sheet is georeferenced against external reference data. The paper keeps that error apart from its classification metrics, whose tolerance is a 20 m buffer on two same-map datasets; annotation on the image itself largely cancels the map's own positional error.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Historical-map extraction lineage in its most industrial form: a supervised CNN pipeline run at national scale over one feature family, published as a data descriptor rather than a methods paper and therefore unusually candid about what the pipeline costs in GPU time and human months. Its principal use to us is as an unusually explicit statement of the per-symbol supervision tax our zero-shot design avoids — 20 separately trained classifiers for a single feature family of three road classes, with effort explicitly multiplied by symbol count — and, secondarily, as a metrics cautionary tale: on a problem where ground-truth road is 2.55% of the evaluated sheet area, its 99.2% average overall accuracy stands about 1.75 points above a predict-nothing baseline of 97.45% — our arithmetic on its Fig. 7 average matrix, not a figure the paper states. It also feeds our methods/metrics and ground-truth threads: the gap between an in-training mIoU of 0.992 and a deployment IoU of 0.73, and reference data digitised to match what the sheet depicts rather than what stood on the ground.

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "For the 100 intersections on the 1:50,000 maps, an RMSE of 17.8 m has been calculated. The calculated MAD of this map scale was 13.0 m. - **Locator:** page_index 7 · p.8 · §Technical Validation — positional accuracy
- **Paraphrase:** Positional agreement between extracted intersections and a current Kenyan Roads Board reference is scale-dependent by an order of magnitude: RMSE 17.8 m and MAD 13.0 m over 100 intersections on 1:50,000 sheets, against RMSE 245.1 m and MAD 202.8 m over 44 intersections on 1:100,000 sheets. The authors note the 1:50,000 result falls inside the USGS 25.4 m standard for that scale and the 1:100,000 result outside the 50.8 m standard, and — since the georeferencing workflow was identical for both — assume the difference stems primarily from the maps themselves, while stating they cannot determine how much is map error and how much georeferencing error (page_index 8-9). This is map-to-modern-world error, which the paper deliberately holds apart from its classification metrics: because ground truth and prediction were both drawn from the same sheet, those metrics assess classification accuracy only and not positional accuracy, and their tolerance is a 20 m buffer applied to both datasets (page_index 6; Table 2 caption, page_index 10). The transferable precedent for our match radii is therefore that buffer rather than the RMSE — where ground truth is annotated on the same image, the map's own positional error largely cancels.

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "In total, the most significant factor influencing the overall effort and processing time is the number of distinct road symbols that need to be classified. The effort required is multiplied by the number of road symbols, as the steps 3 to 7 from Fig. 3 must be completed for each road symbol."
- **Locator:** page_index 9 · p.10
- **Why:** The supervised pipeline's cost stated in the authors' own terms: linear in the size of the symbol vocabulary, because steps 3 to 7 of the workflow repeat for every symbol. It is the cleanest one-sentence setup for our zero-training contrast, but it needs its context to stay honest. What is being multiplied here is 20 symbols for a single feature family across a corpus of mixed editions and provenances, and each multiplication includes ground-truth digitisation plus a training run of several hours to two days on an NVIDIA Quadro P4000 (page_index 9) — though the authors add that training, classification and conversion themselves required only minimal human interaction, being automated in a Python script (page_index 4-5, 10), so what the symbol count multiplies is GPU time plus per-symbol ground-truth digitisation. Our corpus is the mirror image — one symbol family among dozens on a more homogeneous series — so the hook licenses a claim about where supervised extraction's cost lives, not a claim that this pipeline would have been cheap on our maps.

Verified secondary material that did not fit the summary band. The verbatim spans behind these paraphrases are held in the working copy (`_work/kramm_deep_2025.overflow.json`), not published; page anchors are to the extracted page cache (structured 2026-09-03; model `claude-opus-5` requested).

- **Symbol vocabulary by road class** — The 20 separately trained symbols break down six / five / nine across the three road classes: six belong to “Main Road with Bound Surface”, five to “Secondary Road with Loose Surface” and nine to “Dry Weather Road/Motorable Track”. The multiplier on the supervision cost is therefore symbol variation within a three-class vocabulary, not a large class vocabulary. (page_index 5 · p.6 · Fig. 4 caption)
- **Training tiles per road symbol** — Each road symbol was trained on between 900 and 8,400 exported image tiles, the count varying with how much of that road type was available to digitise. That is the per-symbol supervision volume behind the 20 separate classifications. (page_index 4 · p.5 · §Methods — Training & Classification)
- **Supervision floor** — The authors restate a finding from prior work that adequate classification results depend on relatively large training datasets, and that selecting too few roads for training degrades the output. The supervised route has a floor on labelled volume below which it does not work — a floor a zero-shot prompted model does not face. (page_index 9 · p.10 · §Technical Validation — Harmonization and Data Quality)
- **Label buffer set to symbol width** — Training labels were made by buffering the manually digitised road lines by 20 m, a width chosen because it had to fully encompass the road symbol — slightly wider than the symbol itself, and 20 m proved adequate for every symbol used. The same 20 m is later the buffer applied to both datasets at evaluation, so the tolerance that defines agreement is inherited from the label width rather than set independently of the detection protocol. (page_index 4 · p.5 · §Methods — Training & Classification)
- **Scan condition as accuracy covariate** — Per-sheet accuracy tracks the physical state of the sheet as well as the landscape on it: coloured maps score slightly higher than black and white; complex road layouts in mountains (sheet 134-4) or cities (sheet 148-4) lower accuracy; and sheet 119-3, of slightly poorer scan quality with a blueish tint from scanning, returns the lowest accuracies of the seven. Scan condition is a first-class covariate of extraction accuracy, which is a precedent for reporting per-map variation rather than a single corpus-level score. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §Technical Validation)
- **Historical-map extraction lineage** — The paper's related-work survey names five deep-learning precedents for extracting features from historical maps: Can et al. (seven road types from military maps of 1884–1918, CNN); Saeedimoghaddam et al. (region-based CNN for road intersection points on the USGS historical topographic map collection, mostly 1:100,000); Uhl et al. (weakly supervised CNN for roads and buildings on USGS 1:62,500 sheets); Ekim et al. (U-Net++ for five road types on the 1:200,000 Deutsche Heereskarte for Turkey); and Mäyrä et al. (U-Net for roads and other features on scanned historical maps of a small area in Finland). Every one trains a convolutional network on the target map series. (page_index 1 · p.2 · §Background & Summary — related work)
- **Edition drift at sheet boundaries** — Because neighbouring sheets cover different periods and editions, roads sometimes change category or disappear abruptly at a sheet boundary — either because the road did not yet exist or was in a different condition on the earlier sheet, or because the two editions divide their legends differently — and in rare cases the result is an orphaned road with no connection to the rest of the network. Apparent inconsistency of this kind is a property of a heterogeneous map corpus, not a failure of the extraction model. (page_index 9 · p.10 · §Technical Validation — Harmonization and Data Quality)
- **Corpus is a temporal mosaic** — Each extracted road is a snapshot at the publication year of whichever sheet covered that location, so uncertainty about the road's state or existence grows with temporal distance from that year, and does so asymmetrically at the two ends of the 1950s–1980s window: older sheets may miss roads that existed by the 1980s (under-representing the later network), while newer sheets may include roads that did not yet exist in the 1950s (over-representing the earlier one). The authors take all classified roads to have likely existed by the 1980s. (page_index 11 · p.12 · §Historical Road Development in Kenya and Temporal Data Inconsistencies)
- **Undated sheets in the corpus** — 166 of the 1:50,000 sheets and 94 of the 1:100,000 sheets reached the study only as data-frame clips carrying no marginal information, so neither their year of publication nor their source is known. A substantial part of the corpus is therefore undated, which is why the released schema permits a null map year. (page_index 2 · p.3 · §Methods — Collection of Historical Topographic Maps)
- **Map coverage is not territory coverage** — About 5.4% of Kenya could not be classified at all, because no maps covering those areas could be obtained. Coverage of the map corpus is not coverage of the territory, and a completeness figure has to say which of the two it reports. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §Technical Validation — Harmonization and Data Quality)
- **Coverage gaps as broken links** — Roads crossing the unmapped areas may appear in the published dataset as broken links, although the authors judge the missing road mileage likely to be small because most of the absent sheets fall in rural areas. The judgement is offered as a reasoned expectation, not a measurement. (page_index 8 · p.9 · §Technical Validation — Harmonization and Data Quality)
- **Reuse needs a commercial licence** — The Python code released with the paper needs a commercial ArcGIS Pro licence to run, as the authors state explicitly. Any claim that this pipeline is reusable carries that accessibility condition with it. (page_index 11 · p.12 · §Code availability)
