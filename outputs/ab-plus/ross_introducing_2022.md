## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Ross and Ballsun-Stanton's chapter is the citing paper's disciplinary warrant and, sharing an author with it, a self-citation needing care. Three moves carry it. First, a diagnosis: archaeology practises "just-in-time" research, under-planning data collection, management, and analysis, then paying for it in technical debt, in end-of-project cleaning the authors call "lossy", and in datasets not consistent or comparable across a single season at a single project without considerable manual reconciliation. The evidence base is Field Acquired Information Management Systems (FAIMS) practice — customised systems for over sixty workflows at more than forty projects across archaeology, ecology, geoscience, history, and other disciplines since 2014 — from which they name three under-investments: tolerated imprecision in documentation, implicit knowledge transmitted orally, and forms and protocols built too late to test on realistic trial data.

Second, a feasibility argument. Preregistration accommodates deductive and inductive, idiographic and nomothetic, qualitative and quantitative work, and archaeology's abductive, serendipitous character is not an exemption: what is under researcher control — design, questions or hypotheses, methods, interpretive frameworks — can be articulated in advance even when findings cannot, so a "best-effort" preregistration "in an abductive discipline like archaeology should be considered sufficient". That licence is expressly scoped, its warrant being that fieldwork findings are not under researcher control: a computational study reading its deviations log and phased design against it has to argue the scope carries — the same abductive-and-serendipitous premise underwrites the transparency-not-replication concession declined below.

Third, the field baseline. As of 19 March 2020 a search of OSF's 304,904 registrations returned only four non-teaching-related hits for "archaeology" — Marwick (2017a, 2017c), Selden (2016), and Schmid (2019), four registrations from three authors — and all four used the Open-Ended Registration form to deposit data and code supporting papers already written. The authors are explicit that these are not the preregistration of approach and method they advocate. Both the denominator and the date must travel with any restatement, and the search should be re-run: this is a six-year-old count against a registry that has grown, not a claim about the present state of the field.

For prescription the chapter recommends the OSF "Qualitative Research Preregistration" template until archaeology-specific templates exist, walks its numbered fields (points 1–20) with archaeology-specific amendments, and adds two registrable objects drawn from FAIMS experience — data models, and data workflows covering capture, manipulation, and analysis — arguing these do most to defeat just-in-time practice. It also names the axis an adaptive design sits on: stricter registration maximises the benefits, looser is faster and less constraining but less effective against bias. The declined concession, in full: the chapter allows that "the abductive and serendipitous nature of the discipline may often make transparency the goal of preregistration, rather than replication" — a limit attributed to the discipline at large, so reading it as fieldwork-specific is our inference, not the chapter's stated scope. A VLM evaluation study can be reproduced outright, so the citing paper should argue for the stricter bar rather than inherit the lowered one.

- **A priori articulation versus HARKing** — The chapter's named remedy for HARKing — hypothesising after the results are known — is the a priori articulation of hypotheses that preregistration requires, or, as an explicit alternative, a statement that the research is inductive. The parenthesis puts the two on the same footing: declaring a strand inductive in advance counts, for this purpose, as much as registering a hypothesis does. (page_index 4 · p.5 · §1 Preregistration Accommodates Predictive and Postdictive Approaches)
- **No archaeological protocol supplied** — The chapter puts a protocol for archaeological preregistration explicitly beyond its own scope, records that good practice around executing preregistration was still evolving when it was written, and offers in its place guidelines based on practice in other disciplines. What it supplies is therefore a borrowed and provisional starting point rather than a domain-specific instrument. (page_index 8 · p.9 · §4 Introducing Preregistration to Archaeological Practice)
- **TOP Level 2 as the target standard** — Registering the two objects the chapter adds — data models and data workflows — is motivated as helping archaeologists meet emerging standards such as the Transparency and Openness Promotion (TOP) Level 2 Guidelines, which require a full account of the procedures used to collect, preprocess, clean, or generate the data (data provenance) together with a description of procedures sufficient for independent replication of the research (Center for Open Science 2019). (page_index 9 · p.10 · §4 Introducing Preregistration to Archaeological Practice)
- **HARKing prevalence figure is borrowed** — The prevalence figure the chapter cites for HARKing comes from outside archaeology: a 2018 survey of over eight hundred ecologists and evolutionary biologists in which 51 per cent admitted to the practice, attributed to Fraser et al. (2018). It is a self-reported rate in another pair of disciplines, so the denominator, the population surveyed, and the attribution all have to travel with any restatement. (page_index 3 · p.4 · §1 Preregistration Accommodates Predictive and Postdictive Approaches)
- **Method-neutral but publicly binding** — A preregistration regime, on the chapter's account, privileges none of the approaches it has canvassed — deductive or inductive, quantitative or qualitative, idiographic or nomothetic — and instead demands the same thing of all of them: an articulation of, and a public commitment to, a specific research design at the beginning of a project, before research begins. Neutrality about method is thus paired with a strict requirement about timing and publicity. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §3 Preregistration is Suitable for Archaeology)
- **Chapter accepted April 2020** — The preprint's own suggested citation dates acceptance of the chapter to 2 April 2020 and places it in Digital Heritage and Archaeology in Practice, edited by Ethan Watrall and Lynne Goldstein (Gainesville, FL: University Press of Florida). Its argument, and the registry baseline it reports, were therefore fixed at acceptance in April 2020, ahead of the volume's later publication date. (page_index 1 · p.2 · Front matter — Cite this Preprint)
