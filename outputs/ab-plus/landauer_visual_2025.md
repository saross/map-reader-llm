## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Landauer and Klassen ask whether general-purpose visual foundation models can detect archaeological features with no fine-tuning at all. Five experiments: medieval castles in Bavaria (379 coordinates) and Angkorian temples in Cambodia (100) in Bing satellite imagery; English hillforts in LiDAR hillshades (a random 300 of the 1136 Atlas of Hillforts sites with LiDAR coverage); SAM 2.1 delineation of all 1136 hillforts; and SAM 2.1 potsherd segmentation in drone video from an undisclosed Greek site. The first three prompt GPT-4.1 and Gemini 2.0-flash for JSON carrying an object type, a confidence score, a bounding box, and a free-text "Reason" — close enough to our own prompt shape that this is the nearest published precedent for our design, in a different substrate. It serves us better as a foil than a template, for three reasons.

First, in the three prompted-VLM experiments, localisation fails and is abandoned. Bounding boxes are "relatively inaccurate", in some cases not intersecting the remains at all, so the evaluation discards every field except object type and collapses to binary image classification: any detection in a positive image counts as a true positive. Our object-level protocol at 20 m and 50 m is exactly the layer this paper could not evaluate.

Second, the denominators are design choices and the human baselines are mostly estimates. Each of the three quantitative experiments pairs its positives against 1000 random patches — a size fixed by manual-inspection feasibility, which the authors note does not represent real spatial prevalence, so precision and F1 partly reflect that ratio rather than a deployment landscape. The human comparison is estimated from visual inspection for castles (about two-thirds expert recall; a hypothetical specialist F1 of 80%) and for hillforts (expert F1 estimated below 65%); only the Angkor comparison uses measured figures, and those are transferred from the authors' earlier reservoir study (experienced researchers F1 56% to 88%, students approximately 38%). The abstract's "comparable to that of human experts" therefore rests on a largely hypothesised baseline — the gap a measured novice-human arm closes.

Third, operating points diverge sharply between models and are fixed, never swept. Castles: GPT-4.1 F1 67% (precision 71%, recall 64%) against Gemini's 55% (precision 99%, recall 38%, on two false positives). Temples: 45% against 41%. Hillforts: GPT-4.1 recalls 95% but flags 813 of 1000 controls, giving precision 26% and F1 42%, against Gemini's 58% (precision 69%, recall 50%). The authors do record that prompt wording shapes what the models emit — for the Bavarian castle prompt, "confidently" led the model to return only results with confidence scores of 80% or higher — but they attribute the between-model divergence itself to training data, speculating that Gemini saw more LiDAR than GPT-4.1; in the hillfort experiment both models received the identical prompt. They also record that the two models often found different true positives, a cross-model version of the diversity our consensus-over-passes design exploits within one model. Total cloud spend was approximately USD 100, with effort falling from their earlier study's months to days.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Archaeological prospection prior art of the closest available kind for a zero-shot-VLM paper: the same model family (Gemini and GPT), the same prompt-for-JSON-with-a-reason architecture, and the same argument that eliminating training data is the point — but in satellite imagery, LiDAR hillshades and drone video rather than on scanned topographic sheets, and evaluated, in the three prompted-VLM experiments, only at the image-classification layer after bounding boxes proved too imprecise to score. It is therefore both our strongest precedent and our clearest contrast: it establishes that an untuned VLM can find archaeological features at rates its authors read as expert-comparable, while leaving open every question our design turns on — object-level localisation, a swept rather than fixed and unexamined operating point, repeated sampling with consensus, an adversarial verifier in place of sampled manual checking, and a measured rather than estimated human baseline. Secondarily it belongs to the open-vocabulary / VLM-detection-alternative cluster, since its shortlist and its SAM experiments survey the same alternative-model space our paper positions against.

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "archaeological datasets are typically small, often consisting of only a few dozen identified sites, which is insufficient for training foundation models at scale. Second, the process of training or fine-tuning AI models requires significant technical expertise and computational resources—both of which are often unavailable to archaeological research teams."
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2
- **Why:** An independent AI researcher and an archaeologist stating, in 2025, the exact double bind that makes a zero-shot prompted model worth testing at all: too few labelled sites to train on, and too little machine-learning capacity in the average project to do the training. It is a cleaner statement of our own motivation than we would write for ourselves, and it comes from authors who then went and tested the alternative. Used honestly it must carry what their results did to the claim: the untuned models cleared the no-training-data bar but not, in the prompted-VLM experiments, the localisation bar — SAM 2.1 delineation fared better, on the authors' own visual estimate — so the bind is loosened rather than dissolved.
