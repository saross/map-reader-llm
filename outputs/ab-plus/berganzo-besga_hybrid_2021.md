## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Berganzo-Besga and colleagues chain two learned stages to detect burial mounds (tumuli) across the whole of Galicia — 29,574 km2, which they claim as the largest area ever covered by a CNN-based archaeological detector. A YOLOv3 shape detector runs over a LiDAR-derived digital terrain model pre-processed with a multi-scale relief model (MSRM); a random forest (RF) over multitemporal Sentinel-2 imagery yields a binary soil mask; the two rasters are multiplied and the detector is retrained on the product, so it never sees terrain where tumuli cannot occur. Pre-processing gave the largest single AP gain reported — a first approximation rather than an ablation of the final model: training on the raw digital terrain model gave an average precision (AP) of 21.81% (§2.1), MSRM 63.03% (Table 1).

For us it is prospection prior art carrying an unusually rich metric-hygiene lesson. The abstract sets four numbers side by side that are not commensurable. Recall 0.64 and precision 0.97 come from a 104-mound validation set in a single area (67 true positives, 2 false positives, 37 false negatives; Table 4) — and that table's footnote records three further false positives dropped from the count because woodland made them impossible to adjudicate. The 'detection rate of 89.5%' is a different quantity on a different population: the complement of a 10.5% false-positive rate from manually inspecting 3086 of the 10,527 detections in non-forested areas with good aerial visibility. Read as recall it inverts the finding: 35.58% of the test mounds were missed. One further wrinkle: adding the RF stage lowered AP from 71.65% to 66.75% while raising true positives, lowering both error types by one detection each and lifting the deployment yield from 8989 to 10,527 mounds — a curve-summary metric moving against every operating-point metric, which the authors explain rather than bury.

Three further intersections. Architecture: they contrast their upstream filter with Davis et al.'s post-hoc RF check, which confirmed detections but 'did not contribute to reduce the large number of FPs reported' — a caution for any verifier bolted on after the proposer, ours included. Transfer: the test area lacked the soil and land-use variety of the deployment area, and they treat manual validation as the necessary consequence — our carried-versus-oracle problem answered by human inspection rather than transferred calibration. Ground truth: 225 of the 324 confirmed false positives were rock outcrops; some were called false only by context (mounds in golf courses), otherwise indistinguishable from tumuli; and only field survey or test pits could settle the rest. The supervision budget is small: 560 mounds after the RF filter removed 24, split 456 training and 104 validation — the labelled-data cost a zero-training prompted approach avoids; the comparators they cite (Verschoof-van der Vaart et al. precision 0.141, Trier et al. 89% of detections false) mark the precision ceiling this work was measured against, while the authors credit their own gain to MSRM pre-processing, refinement and the RF mask and note in §4.2 that more training data would have improved recall.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Archaeological prospection prior art at deployment scale: the same target class as ours — burial mounds — detected automatically, but in LiDAR-derived terrain rather than as map symbols, and with a two-stage hybrid whose soil-classification stage runs upstream of the detector, suppressing false positives by masking implausible terrain before detection rather than by re-examining candidates after it. Its primary use to us is as a deployment-scale demonstration that constraining a proposer's input with a second model raises yield (8989 to 10,527 mounds) while holding precision, rather than as evidence that a filter lifts precision: the test-set gain is one detection wide (0.96 to 0.97, three false positives to two), it rests on a footnote setting three unadjudicable wooded detections aside (count them and it is 67/72 = 0.93 against the unfiltered 66/69 = 0.96), and AP falls from 71.65% to 66.75%, so the filter's substantive precision case is the deployment-level, qualitative one that all urban areas, roads and roundabouts were eliminated. It is paired with the sharpest headline-metric trap in this cluster: the abstract's '89.5%' is a manually inspected precision-like figure over 3086 of 10,527 detections in non-forested areas, not the recall of 0.64 measured on the 104-mound validation set. Secondarily it supplies calibrated field baselines for our related work (Verschoof-van der Vaart et al. recall 0.796 with precision 0.141; Trier et al. 38% of known tumuli detected with 89% of detections false) and a candid statement that the test area did not represent the deployment area — the transfer problem our carried-versus-oracle operating points quantify.

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "Their common, simple, and regular shape is similar to many other non-archaeological features and therefore studies implementing methods for mound detection in LiDAR-derived and other high-resolution datasets are characterised by a very large presence of false positives (FPs)"
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2
- **Why:** The two-sided property of the target stated by the people who detect it: the regularity that lets a detector be trained on a few hundred examples is the same regularity that fills the output with roundabouts, roofs and rock outcrops. It frames our own problem one level up — a burial-mound symbol on a Soviet topographic sheet is stylised and therefore learnable from few examples, and is for that same reason confusable with the dozens of other small symbols sharing the sheet. Used honestly the hook also marks the difference: their confusers are natural and anthropogenic landforms, ours are other members of a designed symbol vocabulary, so the disambiguating evidence is legend-like rather than contextual.
