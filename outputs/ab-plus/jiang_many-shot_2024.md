## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Jiang, Irvin and colleagues benchmark what happens to multimodal foundation models when the number of in-context demonstrations is scaled by orders of magnitude, across 14 datasets in four image domains (natural, medical, remote-sensing, molecular) and three task families (classification, visual question answering, object localisation). The closed-weights models are GPT-4o, GPT-4(V)-Turbo, and Gemini 1.5 Pro (endpoint 'gemini-1.5-pro-preview-0409'); Llama 3.2-11B-Vision and InternLM-XComposer-2.5 stand in for open weights and, in the paper's second headline, do not benefit from demonstrating examples. Claude 3 Opus was excluded because it accepted at most 20 images per request at the time of writing. For us the relevant model is Gemini, and the relevant finding is that its accuracy improves log-linearly with demonstration count on 9 of the 10 classification datasets, the largest gains being EuroSAT (+38% over zero-shot) and FIVES (+29%) — EuroSAT and UCMerced are satellite land-use classification, the nearest domain neighbours among the benchmark's classification datasets.

Three things scope that result before it can be carried anywhere near our task. First, the tasks are overwhelmingly whole-image classification: only two datasets are localisation, they are scored by mean intersection-over-union, and the paper itself concedes that most large multimodal models cannot yet localise accurately. Our problem is locating one point symbol among dozens of symbol families, which sits past the hard end of this benchmark. Second, the headline numbers are oracle operating points. Table 2 reports performance 'at the optimal demo set size', and the paper describes no held-out split on which that optimum was chosen — so on the only reading its text permits, the optimum is read off the same test-set sweep that supplies the reported figure; 'validation' occurs once in the entire paper, describing the original splits folded into the demonstration pool. The same construction governs the 'optimal batch size'. A study that distinguishes carried from oracle operating points and charges a transfer tax should read the +17% average gain — an average over Table 2's ten classification datasets — as a ceiling, not an achievable number. Third, temperature is zero throughout and the error bars bootstrap over data resampling only, so there is no repeated-sampling or aggregation layer anywhere: this is an ICL-scaling antecedent for us, not a consensus one.

The cost analysis has the most immediate purchase on our trade-space. At Gemini 1.5 Pro preview pricing, a 350-shot HAM10000 query costs $0.8420 per example against $0.0038 zero-shot, and an 810-shot TerraIncognita query $1.8420 against $0.0037 — ratios of roughly 220 and 500, our arithmetic rather than the paper's. Batching 50 queries per request recovers most of it. The batching ablation also yields a result we should sit with: in the zero-shot regime, 49 unlabelled in-domain images shifted accuracy by +3.0% on one dataset and −2.6% on the other, while class-balanced unlabelled context helped both. Context conditioning without any labels is doing real work — which is an annotation-budget finding in disguise.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Few-shot / in-context-learning and annotation-budget prior art, and the only source in our bibliography that prices the demonstration budget for a Gemini model directly: it establishes that Gemini 1.5 Pro converts labelled demonstrations into accuracy roughly log-linearly on remote-sensing classification, and that the conversion costs two to three orders of magnitude more per query than zero-shot unless queries are batched. It bears on our annotation-budget argument as an upper bound rather than a template, because every scaling curve it reports is a whole-image classification or IoU-scored localisation task read at its post-hoc optimum, whereas our detections are point symbols matched at 20 m and 50 m radii and scored at carried operating points. Secondarily it is a calibration source in a sense we must be careful not to conflate with our own — 'domain calibration' and 'class calibration' here mean conditioning the model on in-domain and class-balanced images, not aligning a stated confidence with an observed rate — and, more quietly, a test-time-compute antecedent that spends its inference budget on context length rather than on repeated passes.

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "We set the temperature to zero for all models and a random seed for GPT-4(V)-Turbo and GPT-4o to obtain more deterministic responses."
- **Locator:** page_index 4 · p.5 · §3.1 Models
- **Paraphrase:** Every performance number in the paper comes from a single temperature-zero pass per query; the only repetitions anywhere are re-running a query when the model abstains (page_index 4) and the three timing runs averaged into Table 3 (page_index 7), neither of which aggregates answers; the design suppresses run-to-run variation rather than exploiting it, and the authors claim only 'more deterministic' responses, without testing what residual variation remains. The error bars are correspondingly narrow in scope: standard deviations bootstrapped over 1,000 replicates which, as the same page says, capture 'the variability in data sampling'. Nothing in the study samples a model repeatedly, aggregates across passes, or measures answer diversity — so it supplies no evidence for or against our consensus-over-passes architecture, and its cost figures are single-pass costs that our design multiplies.
