## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Ofosu and Posner audit what preregistration actually delivered in its first six years. They code a representative sample of 195 pre-analysis plans (PAPs) registered on the Evidence in Governance and Politics (EGAP) and American Economic Association (AEA) registries between each registry's initiation and 2016 — 93 of them from projects that had produced a publicly available paper — and supplement the coding with a 2018 survey of potential PAP users (664 invited across the EGAP and Innovations for Poverty Action networks; 155 responses). The design sidesteps causal identification: they ask not whether PAPs make research more credible but whether they are written and used in a way that makes such an improvement possible. Field, survey and lab experiments are 94% of the sample; observational studies 4%.

For §D.9 this is the base-rate table the retrospective has been arguing against without having. On specification the news is better than the rhetoric: 77% of primary dependent variables and 93% of independent/treatment variables were coded as clearly specified (the second rate inflated by treatment dummies, their note 13), 90% of PAPs specified clear hypotheses, and 52.3% (102 of 195) met all four of their "complete PAP" requirements, with another 31.8% meeting three — the glass-half-full verdict (page_index 8–9). Adherence is where it fails. The median paper omitted 25% of its pre-specified hypotheses; more than a third of studies never reported at least one registered hypothesis; 18% of papers tested hypotheses that were never registered, and 82% of those did not say so (page_index 4–5); and of the fourteen model deviations found, one was disclosed.

Read against our own record, that inverts the usual anxiety: an errata log with recorded deviations puts us in a bracket the first PAP generation essentially did not occupy. The honest correction runs the other way — Ofosu and Posner name the identification problem that makes any amendment count a biased measure of drift, since absent a registration there is no record to deviate from.

Two pressure points bear on the forward recipe. Their cost baseline (88% of surveyed researchers spent a week or more per PAP, 26% more than a month, 34% reporting project delay) is the pre-LLM friction the micro-registration claim says has collapsed — useful, and dated 2018. Against it, they flag iterative PAPs as "tricky to implement in practice": without a neutral gatekeeper it is hard to document that iterations were genuinely pre-specified, and their note 28 frames this as innovation on one dimension creating a policing problem on another. That is the objection machine-readable, timestamped registers must answer rather than assume away. Their own advocated remedy — free exploration, clearly labelled as unregistered, with reasons given, alongside mandatory reporting of every pre-specified analysis — is already our practice, which favours reframing the recipe as endorsement-with-citation over proposal.

Two denominator cautions travel with any figure taken from here: figure 2 panel A's hypothesis-count bars sum to 169, not 195, and the "19% of cases" model-deviation rate shares no evident denominator with the fourteen instances.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Squarely in the preregistration and open-science methodology cluster, and — unlike the guidance and template literature around it — the source that supplies measured field base rates: what a representative sample of first-generation pre-analysis plans actually contained, and how faithfully the resulting papers followed them. It sets the bar our §D.9 retrospective is implicitly measured against (deviation disclosed in one of fourteen instances; a median quarter of registered hypotheses unreported), which makes our errata log and recorded deviations a positive departure from the field's early practice rather than a confession — while their identification point, that absent a registration there is no record of what was pre-specified, is exactly why an amendment count is a biased measure of drift in general. It also lodges the strongest available objection to the forward recipe: iterative, updated registrations are the hardest kind to police, and without a neutral gatekeeper researchers cannot easily demonstrate that the iterations were genuinely pre-specified.

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "The more common approach—and the approach we advocate—is to freely undertake exploratory investigations that go beyond the PAP, clearly labeling the results of such investigations in the paper as coming from analyses that were not pre-specified, with an explanation provided for why they were added."
- **Locator:** page_index 7 · p.8 · § Limit Flexibility and Scope for New Discoveries
- **Paraphrase:** Their recommended answer to the hand-tying objection is not a more elaborate registration but a labelling discipline: explore freely beyond the plan, mark the results as unregistered, and say why they were added. They pair it in the same paragraph with mandatory reporting, in the body or appendices, of every analysis that was pre-specified — and they explicitly prefer this to the iterative-PAP route.

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "One cannot compare the degree of fishing and post-hoc hypothesis adjustment in studies implemented with and without PAPs because, absent a PAP, there is no record of the analyses or hypotheses that were pre-specified."
