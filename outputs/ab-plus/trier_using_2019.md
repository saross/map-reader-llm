## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Trier, Cowley and Waldeland test whether a Norwegian convolutional neural network (CNN) prospection approach transfers to Scotland — the method, not a Norwegian-trained model — running ResNet18 (PyTorch, ImageNet pre-trained) retrained on new Arran learning sets over airborne laser scanning (ALS) data visualised as a simplified local relief model at 0.25 m. Published in Archaeological Prospection (26:165–175), it is a proof of concept rather than a benchmark, and its value to us is that failures are reported at the same resolution as successes.

Three things bear on our design. First, one pipeline, three target classes, and outcomes the abstract grades from a manageable mix of false positives and true identifications to the chaotic. The authors had predicted the ordering — huts and small cairns were included because they were expected to be harder than roundhouses — and it held at Machrie Moor, though §6.4 lists roundhouses among the chaotic results in the second block. The variance is class-wise within a block and spatial across blocks, and Table 5 isolates one at a time: at Machrie Moor, terrain fixed, roundhouses ran 11 of 15 known (73%) against 13 false and small cairns 4 of 20 (20%) against 18 false; at Glen Shurig five of 19 shieling huts (26%) were found against 36 false, plus 27 false roundhouses and two false small cairns, neither class having a known example there. No class has known monuments in both blocks, so class and block cannot be separated on recall alone; the spatial signal is that false-positive flood, which the authors attribute to terrain and to the 16 m by 16 m training images against huts under about 3 m by 5 m. Our difficulty ladder and our per-map variance across 55 maps are the same phenomenon.

Second, a metric-hygiene trap sharper than the usual class-relative one. The training table's best figure is 99.46% validation accuracy, at epochs 3 and 4. Table 3 gives 26 roundhouses and 1,842 background samples in validation; on the natural reading their union is that network's validation set of 1,868, and all ten reported accuracies round, at four decimal places, to integer multiples of 1/1868, corroborating the denominator. An always-background classifier would score 1,842/1,868 = 98.61%, so the reported best sits 0.85 percentage points, about 16 additional correct calls, above that trivial baseline. The arithmetic is ours; the paper never presents 99.46% as its result, but it is the number a citing reader will lift.

Third, the paper's remedies are our architecture's antecedents. It recommends adding anthropogenic and natural false positives to the learning set as confusion classes, and states the trade in the same breath — fewer false positives, at the risk of absorbing true structures — the training-time version of what our adversarial verifier does at inference. It asks when cross-class confusion counts as an error. And it costs what we do not pay: a few hundred verified examples per class plus a shared pool of several thousand background samples (Table 3: 9,197), assembled anew for this landscape.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Archaeological prospection prior art in the strictest sense — a deep CNN on airborne laser scanning data, from the group whose earlier template-matching and CNN work our related-work section already leans on — and the best predecessor we have for the claim that detector performance varies enormously with target class and with terrain under one architecture and one workflow: one ResNet18 pipeline over one island gave 73% roundhouse recall against 20% small-cairn recall in the same block, and in a second block a chaotic flood of 27 false roundhouses and 36 false shieling huts, with the authors reporting the failure at the same resolution as the success. They do not exonerate the method, which is not even constant across the classes compared — training crops were 96 × 96 px for roundhouses against 64 × 64 px for huts and cairns (printed p. 171) — and printed p. 173 names the training-image size (16 m by 16 m, against huts under about 3 m by 5 m) as a likely factor in the chaotic outputs, alongside DTM pixel size, ResNet input size, and the natural confusion objects left out of the learning set. Secondarily it is a metric-hygiene source in two ways: the training table's 99.46% validation accuracy sits 0.85 points above an always-background baseline on a 26-positive, 1,842-negative validation set (our arithmetic, corroborated by every reported accuracy rounding to an integer multiple of 1/1868), and both results tables express false identifications as a percentage of known monuments rather than of predictions, which is why the figures run past 100%. Its confusion-class remedy — add the known confusables to the learning set, accepting that this may absorb true structures — is the training-time antecedent of our adversarial verifier pass, and its annotation budget of a few hundred verified examples per class plus several thousand background samples is the cost our zero-training design does not pay.

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "Black boxes in processes of identification and classification are undesirable, whether they are computational or expressed through the archaeologist."
- **Locator:** page_index 8 · p.9
- **Why:** The demand for interpretability turned back on the discipline that makes it. Trier, Cowley and Waldeland raise it mid-way through a proof-of-concept detection study, and their point is that requiring a model to say why it called something a roundhouse obliges archaeologists to say why the curator did. That is precisely the tension in our ground-truth epistemics — curator-supplied and adjudicated reference data disagreeing, with no appeal beyond a second human — and it lets us frame the verifier's natural-language rationale as a two-sided instrument rather than a concession to model opacity. Used honestly it also constrains us: the hook only works if we hold our own reference data to the standard it sets.

- **Three binary detectors, not one** — The four-class design — roundhouse, shieling hut, small cairn and background in one network — was abandoned because performance could not be optimised on all classes at once. The study instead runs three separate binary detectors, each pitting one target class against background, so every class carries its own trained network and its own inference pass over the tiles. (page_index 4 · p.5 · §4.2 Neural network design)
- **Outputs are not probabilities** — Because the three class-versus-background classifiers are independent, their per-pixel outputs are not normalised across classes, and the authors state plainly that a further normalisation would be needed before the values could be treated as probabilities — and that it is not applied. No calibration step, decision threshold or confidence figure is reported anywhere in the paper. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §4.4 Running the neural network on entire tiles)
- **Evaluation reported in counts** — The evaluation compares the automated predictions against the desk- and field-based observations of a single experienced archaeological field worker. Performance is then reported only as correct and false identification counts against known monuments (Table 5) and as per-epoch validation accuracy (Table 4): no precision, recall, F1, Matthews correlation coefficient, confidence interval, cross-validation or repeated run appears anywhere in the paper, so any comparison with this study has to be made in counts. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §5 Results)
- **Prose understates the cairn result** — The running text puts the small-cairn result at fewer than half of the verified locations matched, which is true but much looser than the table it cites: Table 5 records 4 of 20 known small cairns found at Machrie Moor — 20 per cent — against 18 false identifications. The table figure, not the prose hedge, is the one to quote. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §5 Results — Machrie Moor)
- **False-negative count inconsistent** — The Machrie Moor roundhouse result is stated as 11 of 15 known locations found with four false negatives, which is the count the arithmetic supports. Two later statements disagree: the same page goes on to discuss the three roundhouse locations the automatic method missed, and the Figure 5 caption on the preceding page likewise names three that were not identified. Cite four, not three, as the study's false-negative count. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §5 Results — Machrie Moor)
- **A class with no denominator** — In the block where the detector worked best for roundhouses, the shieling-hut channel returned almost nothing but false positives: Table 5 records 23 false identifications at Machrie Moor against no known shieling huts at all, so no recall figure exists for that row. The authors add that some of those false positives are nevertheless real monuments of other kinds, including a chambered cairn. (page_index 7 · p.8 · §5 Results — Machrie Moor)
- **Negatives masked against confusables** — The background class was protected from contamination by masking. Buffer zones were drawn not only around the three target classes but around other recorded monuments — burial cairns, burnt mounds, modern cattle feed stances, enclosures and horse-engine platforms — that could be mistaken for a roundhouse. Background locations were then drawn at random from the tiles containing known monuments and any falling inside the mask were discarded, so the negatives could not silently hold a positive or a near-positive. (page_index 5 · p.6 · §4.3 Training of the neural network)
- **Augmentation multiplies training eightfold** — Table 3's training column counts labelled examples, not training images: horizontal flip and rotation by 0, 90, 180 or 270 degrees multiply them eightfold, while the four remaining transforms — two scalings and two translations — produce only minor variations. The validation images were kept unchanged (printed p. 170), so the reported validation accuracies rest on the unmultiplied counts. (page_index 6 · p.7 · §4.3 Training of the neural network)
- **Negative pool bounded by compute** — The size of the background pool is a two-sided decision rather than a free parameter: a wide range of background examples is said to reduce false positives, but too many make the training phase so slow that it precludes running several training phases at different parameter settings and comparing them. Table 3's 9,197 background samples therefore sit at a compute-bounded compromise, rather than at a value the paper reports having searched for. (page_index 5 · p.6 · §4.3 Training of the neural network)
