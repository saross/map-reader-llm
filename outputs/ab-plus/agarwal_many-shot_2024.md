## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Agarwal, Singh and colleagues at Google DeepMind push in-context learning past the few-shot regime using Gemini 1.5 Pro's one-million-token context. Shot counts run to 8192 on sequential parity and to hundreds or thousands across low-resource translation, summarisation, planning, MATH, GSM8K, GPQA, BIG-Bench Hard, sentiment analysis, code verification and linear classification. Two scope conditions bound our use of it. All tasks are text — text-serialised numbers for the two 'non-natural language' tasks of §4.2 — with no vision or multimodal ICL anywhere in the paper. The study is essentially single-model, a limitation §5 states outright: GPT-4-Turbo, Claude-3-Opus and Gemini 1.5 Flash appear only on translation (Figure 13). The model family, though, is ours.

Its centre of gravity for us is the annotation budget. The abstract names the bottleneck, the available amount of human-generated outputs; two mitigations follow. Reinforced ICL replaces human rationales with model-generated ones filtered by final-answer correctness; Unsupervised ICL removes rationales from the many-shot block, prompting with problems alone, plus a preamble and a zero-shot or short few-shot output-format specification (§3; Figures A.14, A.16). What Reinforced ICL does not save matters most: the filter assumes access to ground-truth final answers, so it buys down reasoning traces, not labels — cheap chain-of-thought exemplars over an unchanged curator-adjudication budget. Unsupervised ICL does cut the label requirement, but is not general: the authors say it may not perform well 'when outputs are critical for specifying the task', and Figure A.17 shows it failing on translation, whose output format — like ours — is much of the specification. Where the methods work the margins are real: averaged over the 8 of BIG-Bench Hard's 23 tasks the authors selected for low false-positive likelihood (long answer strings, or at least six options), human-written 3-shot chain-of-thought reaches 72.1%, Unsupervised ICL 77.1% and Reinforced ICL 83%.

Three robustness findings temper any many-shot ambition of ours. Shot curves are not always monotone — MATH500 ICL peaks near 125 examples then declines, XSum declines past 50, GPQA degrades at 250 — and §5 concedes the authors do not completely understand why. Prompt-order sensitivity survives into the many-shot regime: ten random orderings of one fixed 50-shot MATH prompt swing per-subarea accuracy far more than the average, an aggregation effect our per-map and per-symbol reporting should expect. And a footnote records that 'Gemini 1.5 Pro' named two served models in succession — the February 2024 version the paper evaluates, and the newer one the API has served since April 2024 — which Figure 4 shows behaving differently on planning.

Two positive results speak to our architecture. §A.5 builds a verifier in context, scoring candidate GSM8K code solutions by the normalised probability of a Yes token: at 128 shots, best-of-4 selection narrows the gap between 77.25% pass@1 and 90% pass@4 on 200 test problems — a proof of concept. And §4.1 shows many-shot ICL overriding pre-training label biases that few-shot ICL cannot — the mechanism by which a long prompt might teach a curator's idiosyncratic symbol convention against the model's prior.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Primary cluster: few-shot / in-context learning / annotation budget — this is the reference statement of what scaling in-context examples buys and what it costs, measured in the same model family we prompt. Its use to us is negative as much as positive: Reinforced ICL shows model-generated reasoning traces standing in for human ones while leaving the ground-truth label budget untouched, and the authors expressly caution that Unsupervised ICL 'may not perform well ... when outputs are critical for specifying the task', showing it fail outright on translation (Figure A.17), which we take to be our case (though on BIG-Bench Hard it still beat the human-written 3-shot chain-of-thought baseline) — so the many-shot lever this paper offers a detection study is narrower than the headline suggests, and it is offered for text prompts only. It also supplies two secondary antecedents worth citing in their own right: an in-context verifier scored by token probability rather than verbalised confidence (consensus / test-time compute), and a documented case of one model name serving two behaviourally different systems within three months (a reproducibility hazard our preregistration apparatus has to absorb).

## Key points (salience-ranked to the citing paper)

### KP1
- **Quote (verbatim):** "we select rationales that obtain the correct final answer (we assume access to ground truth final answers), and arrange them into in-context examples containing (problem, rationale) pairs"
- **Locator:** page_index 3 · p.4 · §3 Many-shot Learning without Human-Written Rationales — Reinforced ICL
- **Paraphrase:** Reinforced ICL builds its in-context examples by sampling multiple rationales per training problem from a few-shot or zero-shot chain-of-thought prompt and keeping only those whose final answer is correct. The filter presupposes ground-truth final answers, so the method substitutes model-generated reasoning traces for human-written ones without reducing the number of labelled items required: the rationale budget falls, the label budget does not. The authors flag the residual risk themselves — an incorrect reasoning chain can still reach the correct final answer, and prompting with such a chain would typically harm performance — while reporting that they nevertheless often find model-generated rationales at least as effective as human-written ones.
- **Relevance:** §3 methods — annotation budget and exemplar construction · Annotation budgets — what model-generated exemplars can and cannot replace · **complicates**

### KP2
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Strikingly, an ordering that that excels in one subarea may perform poorly in another, for example, the best Geometry ordering yields weak results on Number Theory. This fluctuation results in a smaller variation in average performance compared to individual subareas."
- **Locator:** page_index 19 · p.20 · §A.6 Is Many-Shot ICL Sensitive to Example Ordering?
- **Paraphrase:** Ten random orderings of the same 50 in-context MATH examples produce accuracies that vary sharply by MATH500 subarea, and the ordering that is best for one subarea can do badly in another. Because the swings are not aligned across subareas, the average over subareas moves less than any single subarea does — aggregation hides the instability rather than removing it. The authors present this as an open reliability challenge for many-shot ICL with long-context models, and suggest prompt-optimisation frameworks such as DSPy as an extension rather than a fix they have tested.
- **Relevance:** §4 architecture and §5 discussion — why stratified (per-map, per-symbol) reporting is not optional · Prompt-order sensitivity and stratum-level variance hidden by aggregation · **supports**

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "For ICL, we observe that the performance improves with more examples in the prompt up to a point, and then declines (with the peak being at about 125 examples)."
- **Locator:** page_index 4 · p.5 · §3.1 Problem-solving: Hendrycks MATH & GSM8K
- **Paraphrase:** On MATH500 with human-written ground-truth solutions, accuracy rises with more in-context examples only up to roughly 125 and falls thereafter. The same non-monotonicity recurs elsewhere in the paper — XSum summarisation declines beyond 50 shots, GPQA degrades at 250, on an evaluation set the authors themselves call small and high-variance — whereas Reinforced ICL plateaus at around 25 examples, about 5% above ICL, without the late drop. In §5 the authors state that they do not completely understand why performance can sometimes degrade with more examples. An exemplar-count budget curve therefore cannot be assumed monotone, and the turning point is task-specific.
- **Relevance:** §5 discussion — cost/accuracy trade-space and operating-point selection · Non-monotone returns to exemplar count — budget curves that turn over · **complicates**

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Best-of-4 selection with 128-shot bridges the gap between Pass@1 accuracy of 77.25% and Pass@4 accuracy of 90% with Gemini 1.0 Pro model."
- **Locator:** page_index 19 · p.20 · §A.5 Reward Modelling with Many-Shot ICL: Learning Code Verifiers — Figure A.4
- **Paraphrase:** A verifier built entirely in context — each prompt example is a (problem, solution) pair followed by 'Is the solution correct?' and a Yes or No token, with the verifier score taken as the normalised probability of the Yes token — ranks four candidate solutions per problem. With 128 in-context examples, best-of-4 selection over Gemini 1.0 Pro code solutions on 200 GSM8K test problems partially bridges the interval between 77.25% pass@1 and 90% pass@4 (Figure A.4's accuracy axis tops out near 82%). The authors present this as a proof of concept; the score is a token probability read from logits, not a verbalised confidence, and the four candidate solutions per problem come from Gemini 1.0 Pro, an earlier model than the Gemini 1.5 Pro that §5 says the paper mainly evaluates.
- **Relevance:** §4 architecture — the adversarial verifier pass · Consensus and test-time compute — verifier-over-candidates antecedents · **supports**

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "With few shots, test accuracy with replacement labels is much lower than with default labels. This suggests that with few-shot ICL, the model struggles to overcome its pre-existing biases from pre-training. However, as the number of shots increases, performance on flipped and abstract labels dramatically improves, approaching that of default labels."
- **Locator:** page_index 6 · p.7 · §4.1 Overcoming Pre-training Biases with Many-Shot ICL
- **Paraphrase:** On Financial PhraseBank sentiment analysis with labels either rotated ('flipped') or replaced by 'A', 'B', 'C' ('abstract'), few-shot ICL performs far worse than with the default labels, and many-shot ICL closes most of that distance, approaching the default-label level. Model confidence in the predicted label follows the same arc for flipped labels — an initial drop, then a sharp rise to a plateau — which the authors read as a period of overcoming pre-training bias, and which they tentatively attribute ('we posit') to an 'early ascent' effect in which a few shots retrieve the wrong skill. The confidence measured here is a label probability, not a stated confidence.
- **Relevance:** §3 methods — teaching a curator's symbol convention against the model's prior · Overriding model priors with in-context supervision; confidence dynamics under bias override · **extends**

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "While supervised finetuning (SFT) is the dominant LLM paradigm when making use of hundreds or thousands of examples, it is computationally expensive in terms of training. In contrast, many-shot ICL does not require any training, however it has a larger inference cost, which can be substantially reduced with KV caching"
- **Locator:** page_index 7 · p.8 · §4.3 Many-Shot ICL vs. Supervised Fine-Tuning
- **Paraphrase:** Fine-tuning is the standard way to exploit hundreds or thousands of examples but costs training compute; many-shot ICL trades that for inference cost, which key-value caching reduces substantially. The head-to-head is run on low-resource translation only, at matched example counts (Figure 11, mean over 3 seeds, standard deviation 0.1% to 0.5%), and the paper's own verdict is that performance is quite close for Bemba while SFT has a slight edge for Kurdish. Read from the flattened figure text in legend order, at 997 examples English to Bemba scores 47.7 chrF2++ for SFT against 47.2 for many-shot ICL and English to Kurdish 46.5 against 44, from 1-shot base-model scores of 28.3 and 39.5. §4.4 adds that with KV caching runtime grows linearly rather than quadratically with shot count.
- **Relevance:** §1 introduction and §5 discussion — why a prompted VLM rather than a trained detector · Cost/accuracy trade-space — prompted inference versus a trained model · **supports**

- **Quote (verbatim):** "This corresponds to original version in the Gemini 1.5 Tech Report, released in February 2024. We note that the Gemini 1.5 Pro API now serves a newer version starting from April 2024."
- **Locator:** page_index 1 · p.2 · §2 Scaling In-Context Learning — footnote 2

## Optional framing hook (not counted in the salience cap)

- **Quote (verbatim):** "While promising, many-shot ICL can be bottlenecked by the available amount of human-generated outputs."
- **Locator:** page_index 0 · p.1
- **Why:** The annotation-budget constraint in one line, and in a vocabulary borrowed from machine learning rather than archaeology: once context windows stop binding, what limits in-context supervision is the supply of human-produced outputs. For a study whose gold standard is curator-adjudicated mound positions on a handful of maps, that is our own constraint restated. Used honestly the hook has to carry its sequel: the paper's two answers to the bottleneck save different things. Reinforced ICL replaces human rationales but filters on ground-truth final answers (§3), so the label budget is untouched. Unsupervised ICL does cut the label budget — its many-shot block is unsolved inputs only, with outputs retained just to specify the format — but the authors caution it may not perform well where outputs are critical for specifying the task, and Figure A.17 shows it failing on translation.
