## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Huang and colleagues train a supervised object detector — ASPP-YOLOv4, a YOLOv4 variant in which atrous spatial pyramid pooling replaces spatial pyramid pooling — to recognise and locate point symbols on scanned topographic maps (STMs). In substrate and target it is the closest published analogue to our task: scanned sheets on which symbols stick to contours and background, small structurally simple targets, and a requirement to return class and position. It is also the clearest statement of what the supervised route costs. Two corpora were annotated by hand: 1,909 scanned map images at 1:50,000 holding 3,717 single symbols, split train/validation/test at approximately 8:1:1, plus 2,505 vectorised map images holding 7,471 single symbols (page_index 4). The vocabulary is ten classes, named in the source only by pinyin identifiers (sanjiaodian, kuangjing, shiyoujing, jinianbei and the rest — triangulation point, mine shaft, oil well and monument on the obvious readings, which the paper does not gloss). No archaeological feature class appears, and the closing limitations add that every symbol in the dataset points north, that undirected symbols would need rotation invariance the model lacks, and that only ten classes were collected.

Headline performance is mAP 98.11% and mIoU 0.876 on the authors' own test set, with average precision at an IoU threshold of 0.5 (page_index 9), and the paper claims to be the first to employ DCNNs for recognising point symbols. Its own related-work section qualifies that: it cites an AlexNet transfer-learning point-symbol classifier [29], a DCNN plus a graph convolutional network for geological map symbols [30], and deep-learning symbol-and-text recognition on piping and instrumentation diagrams [31] (page_index 2). The defensible reading is first DCNN detector returning class and position jointly for STM point symbols. Three conditions bound the headline. It is in-domain, the scanned corpus supplying both training and test. The margins are thin — faster R-CNN reaches 97.81 mAP and 0.874 mIoU against 98.11 and 0.876, and the paper itself says all the models achieved similar high accuracies — with no confidence intervals, no repeated runs, and no Matthews correlation coefficient anywhere. And robustness fails in exactly the ways scanned maps break: a model trained on vectorised maps and tested on scanned ones recalls 48.05% of symbols, rising to 65.61% with Gaussian blur and colour jitter augmentation, which the authors still call inadequate generalisation, contradicted by their own Conclusions' claim of 'strong generalization ability' (page_index 20); and rotating the test set by 5° drops mAP to 54.56 unless small-angle rotation augmentation is trained in, after which it recovers to 95.90.

The most transferable detail is architectural rather than numerical. Huang et al. tile large sheets — 'first cut, then zoom, detect, and finally stitch', 208 × 208 crops zero-padded and enlarged 2× to 416 × 416 (page_index 15) — and then report that the same symbol is recognised twice at tile seams, requiring further screening of the results. That is our cross-tile de-duplication problem reached from the supervised side — tiling generates false positives independently of the detector.

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

Historical-map extraction lineage, and the single closest prior art to our task in substrate and target: point symbols on scanned topographic sheets, located as well as classified, by a detector purpose-built for the medium. It clears the way for a zero-shot claim rather than blocking one — the method needs 3,717 hand-annotated scanned-map symbols across ten fixed classes, per-class supervised training, and an augmentation regime tuned to two specific scanned-map defects (style shift, small-angle tilt), and none of its ten classes is an archaeological feature of the kind we target. The same team hand-annotated a further 7,471 symbols across 2,505 vectorised map images; the paper calls that corpus 'another training set' and trains on it alone for the cross-style ablation, rather than establishing that the headline model required it. Its use to us is threefold: the attested price of the supervised route on our own substrate, an early instance of our transfer-tax pattern in the vectorised-to-scanned ablation (recall 48.05% before augmentation, 65.61% after), and the tile-seam duplicate problem stated as an unsolved limitation by authors who had to confront it.

- **Locator:** page_index 0 · p.1
- **Why:** A cartographer's statement of why our task is hard, in the source's own vocabulary: symbols do not sit on the map, they stick to it, and their structures are too singular to separate on shape alone.
