## Summary (Claude's synthesis — advisory, unverified)

Willroth and Atherton answer one narrow, practical question: once a preregistration has been departed from — which the Claesen et al. (2021) audit they cite found in 93% of 27 preregistered Psychological Science studies — how should the departure be written down? Their answer is a Preregistration Deviations Table, one deviation per row, with three drop-down fields (type, reason, timing) and three narrative columns (the original preregistration wording, a description of the deviation, and a reader-impact statement), plus a separate section for "unregistered steps" — decisions the preregistration left ambiguous or unspecified. Table 2 (page_index 8) enumerates ten deviation types, from study design through analytic approach to inferential criteria; Table 3 (page_index 9) enumerates six reasons, from typo or oversight to coauthor miscommunication. Both vocabularies are written for psychological studies and map onto our protocol-errata register only in part — analytic approach, data preparation, variable operationalisation, and inferential criteria travel; sample size and study design do not transfer to a tile corpus — so borrowing the schema means declaring the mapping.

The empirical spine is thin: a preregistered survey of editors at 16 psychology journals (170 emailed, 44 consented, 34 after preregistered exclusions). On a 1 (significant negative impact) to 5 (significant positive impact) scale, editors rated a *disclosed* deviation at M = 3.3 (SD = 1.3, range = 1–5) — neutral to slightly positive — and the same deviation *left unreported and identified during peer review* at M = 1.3 (SD = 0.5, range = 1–3). Among seven factors they ranked authors' transparency top. These are stated perceptions from a small, psychology-only sample, not measured outcomes; taken as such, they are the cleanest external warrant for D.9's counterweight that the liability is the undisclosed deviation, not the disclosed one — though the vignette varied disclosure, not volume, and the authors themselves warn that "when numerous substantive deviations occur, readers may choose to evaluate the evidence in a similar manner to how they would evaluate exploratory or unregistered research" (page_index 8).

Three of their rules bear directly on our design. Timing is a graded axis running from before data collection to after the preregistered analysis's results are known, and "data-dependent deviations increase risk of bias" (their citation: Hardwicke & Wagenmakers, 2023) — an axis our protocol-errata rows do not carry (they record date, type and impact, often files or a commit, but never reason or timing). Once results are known the registration must not be updated; the deviation is reported in the article instead. And where a deviation displaced a planned analysis they ask authors to "include the results of the preregistered analyses alongside the deviated analyses" (page_index 6) — the report-both discipline our primary-versus-alternative-aggregation reporting already practises. Their prevention list reaches the adaptive-preregistration thread from the other side: the fourth item is to anticipate forking paths and preregister decision trees (page_index 10) — on our reading, registered flexibility under another name inside a static frame, though they present it as deviation prevention, citing Nosek et al. (2018).

## Positioning annotation (interpretive)

The execution-side complement to the adaptive-preregistration literature, and the closest thing to a citable standard for what our protocol-errata register should contain: primary cluster preregistration and open-science methodology, with a secondary reach into methods/metrics through its reader-impact and report-both rules. Willroth and Atherton take the registration as fixed and standardise the *reporting* of departures from it — where Gould et al. move the registration itself — so the two sit either side of our recipe rather than in competition, and their "once results are known, preregistrations should not be updated" is the constraint any phased or interim-registration design has to answer. What they offer us is a schema (type, reason, timing, original wording, description, reader impact) plus the unregistered-steps category; what they cannot offer is any evidence from outside psychology, or from a computational study, that the schema's type and reason vocabularies fit.

- **Paraphrase:** Each row of their Preregistration Deviations Table is classified on three fixed axes — type, reason, and timing — chosen from closed menus; the same table adds three free-text columns for the original preregistration wording, a description of the deviation, and a reader-impact statement. The note to Table 1 allows a narrative account to replace a drop-down when more than one type or reason applies.

### KP3
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Deviations can occur at any point in the research process, including before data were collected, during data collection, after data collection but before data were accessed, after data were accessed but before results of preregistered approach were known, and after results of preregistered approach were known."
- **Locator:** page_index 8 · p.9 · §Timing of deviation
- **Paraphrase:** Timing is a five-point ordered axis whose last two steps turn on what the researcher had seen: data accessed but preregistered results not yet known, versus results known. The same paragraph gives the reason the axis is reported at all — "data-dependent deviations increase risk of bias" — attributed to Hardwicke and Wagenmakers (2023), not established here.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 deviations and the retrospective; §D.8 limitations · Timing relative to results-known as the bias-bearing axis · **supports**

### KP4
- **Quote (verbatim):** "We encourage researchers to use the Preregistration Deviations Table to report all deviations no matter the number."
- **Locator:** page_index 11 · p.12 · §When are there too many deviations?
- **Paraphrase:** Asked how many deviations are too many, they decline to set a threshold and instead instruct authors to report every one regardless of count, on the stated rule of thumb that it is better to over-report than to under-report. They add that a preregistration should be disclosed even where the authors feel the deviations were numerous enough to disqualify the study from counting as preregistered. The same section nonetheless ties volume to bias — risk of bias rises "particularly when deviations occur after looking at the data" — and concedes that a preregistration-badge claim "might be misleading" once the number of deviations and data-dependent decisions renders risk of bias high.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 deviations and the retrospective (grain-not-volume) · Volume of deviations: the over-bake-with-receipts counterweight · **supports**

### KP5
- **Quote (verbatim):** "editors indicated that the reported deviations would significantly negatively affect their perception of the manuscript, on average (M = 1.3, SD = 0.5, range = 1–3)"
- **Locator:** page_index 3 · p.4 · §Results — Editorial perceptions
- **Paraphrase:** In the scenario where the authors did NOT report the deviation and it was instead caught during peer review, editors' mean rating was M = 1.3 (SD = 0.5, range = 1–3) on a 1 (significant negative impact) to 5 (significant positive impact) scale — against M = 3.3 (SD = 1.3, range = 1–5) for the same deviation disclosed by the authors. The sentence's own wording says "the reported deviations" although the scenario is the unreported one. On the parallel 1 (not at all) to 3 (a lot) scale for effect on editorial decisions the same two scenarios drew M = 1.9 (SD = 0.5, range = 1–3) disclosed and M = 2.6 (SD = 0.5, range = 2–3) undisclosed — the same direction, but disclosure does not make a deviation decision-neutral; in the disclosed scenario 38% of editors answered "it depends" on the perception question and 59% on the decision question. All four means are stated perceptions and self-reports from the 34-editor sample, not observed editorial outcomes.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 deviations and the retrospective · Disclosure pays: external warrant for a full errata log · **supports**

### KP6
- **Quote (verbatim):** "Once results are known, preregistrations should not be updated."
- **Locator:** page_index 11 · p.12 · §When to deviate versus update versus create a new preregistration?
- **Paraphrase:** Registry update features (they instance OSF's time-stamped update, which its support page scopes to "events outside your control" and "unexpected anomalies") are for the pre-results period; after results are known the change belongs in the article's deviations table, not in the registration. For major planned changes they allow a supplemental registration or coregistration, and a wholly new study warrants a new preregistration disclosing that it postdates the first study's results.
- **Relevance:** §D.9 deviations and the retrospective (micro-registration recipe) · Update-versus-deviate: the constraint on a phased or interim-registration design · **complicates**

### KP7
- **Quote (verbatim):** "We define a deviation as any discrepancy between what the authors said they would do in the preregistration and what the authors actually did in the final article."
- **Locator:** page_index 0 · p.1 · §Introduction
- **Paraphrase:** Their working definition is maximally inclusive on one side and bounded on the other: any discrepancy counts, but the comparison is between the preregistration and what was done in the final article — not between the preregistration and every intermediate state of the work. Their later guidance pushes the inclusive side further, asking authors to report anything that could be perceived as a deviation (page_index 9).
- **Relevance:** §D.9 deviations and the retrospective; protocol-errata register · Scope of the recorded-deviations register · **supports**
