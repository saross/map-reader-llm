## TL;DR

Across the three strands, "good" published results split cleanly by *what is being matched*: pixel-level segmentation of **area or line** features on historical maps clusters at F1 ≈ 0.84–0.98, whereas **object-level detection of small, discrete features** — mound symbols on map series, point symbols on geological maps, mounds and kurgans in remote-sensing imagery — clusters far lower, at F1 ≈ 0.35–0.73 in held-out or large-area settings, with only one study (Garcia‐Molsosa et al. 2021, 10.1002/arp.1807) reporting implied F1 above 0.9, and that against a weak reference standard. The three must-reads are Berganzo-Besga et al. (2023) 10.1038/s41598-023-38190-x (the only published held-out, object-level, low-density benchmark for mound symbols on a historical map series: F1 0.642–0.724 hachure, 0.706–0.938 form-line), Garcia‐Molsosa et al. (2021) 10.1002/arp.1807 (the direct methodological ancestor), and Landauer & Klassen (2025) 10.3390/geomatics5040052 (the only published zero-shot vision-language-model archaeological detection study, F1 0.41–0.67). The biggest gap: **no published work applies a vision-language model to archaeological symbol detection on historical topographic maps at all** — strand 3 as posed is empty, and the nearest neighbours are either VLM-on-imagery (Landauer & Klassen) or LLM-assisted-labelling-on-maps (Yuan & Sester 2025, arXiv), neither of which is the same task.

## Landscape

The literature relevant to this positioning question is not one field but three, using incompatible measuring sticks.

**Historical-map feature extraction** is dominated by pixel-level semantic segmentation of *area* and *line* features — wetlands, buildings, roads, mine extents. Reported F1 and Dice values run 0.84–0.98 and are, on their face, well above the paper's numbers. They also measure a different thing: a wetland hatch or a building footprint occupies thousands of pixels with strong local texture, and per-pixel agreement over such a feature is far more forgiving than "did you find this ~1 mm symbol, and is it within 20 m of where it should be". Ekim et al. (2021) make the point unintentionally and vividly: overall accuracy 98.73%, IoU 41.99%, F1 46.61%, from one model on one dataset.

Within that field, a much smaller sub-literature attacks *discrete* features. Arundel et al. (2022) on spot elevations, Vassányi & Gede (2021) on point symbols, and Saxton et al. (2024) and Goldman et al. (2025) on geological point features are the whole of it that I could find with metrics. The competition-median figure — F1 0.35 for points against 0.77 for polygons, on identical maps under identical scoring (Goldman et al. 2025) — is the cleanest published statement of how much harder the discrete case is. Saxton et al.'s 0.73 is the best-system point result on that data.

**Mound detection from historical maps specifically** has exactly two serious published entries, from overlapping author teams: Garcia‐Molsosa et al. (2021) and Berganzo-Besga et al. (2023), both on the Survey of India series. The first reports very high implied precision and recall against manual re-reading of a small number of sheets; the second scales to 645 heterogeneous sheets, adopts an explicitly low-density evaluation regime, adds a genuine held-out test set, and reports F1 of 0.64–0.94 depending on symbol type and on how similar the test maps are to the training maps. The trajectory between the two papers — from ~0.92–0.98 on curated similar sheets to 0.64–0.72 on the general case — is the most instructive result in the corpus for positioning a new number.

**Remote-sensing mound and kurgan detection** is the most populous strand and the one where realistic numbers are lowest. Two independent groups document the same collapse when moving from curated test tiles to real landscapes: Trier et al. (2021) go from an implied F1 ≈ 0.81 on 150 m tiles that each contain a target to F1 ≈ 0.17 for grave mounds over 67 km²; Verschoof-van der Vaart et al. (2020) go from ~70% on a small non-random test set to ~50% on a large random one. At continental scale, Landauer et al. (2025) report F1 0.34–0.38 over 180,000 km². The best entries — Berganzo-Besga et al. (2021) at P 0.97 / R 0.64, Canedo et al. (2025) at F1 0.59–0.66 — get there principally through aggressive false-positive suppression. And Sobotkova et al. (2024), on Bulgarian burial mounds validated against field data, is a documented failure with true-positive rates of 5–13%.

**VLM baselines** barely exist. Landauer & Klassen (2025) is the only published zero-shot vision-language detection study in archaeology I could locate, and it explicitly abandons localisation scoring because the models' bounding boxes were too imprecise, falling back to per-tile binary classification with F1 between 0.41 and 0.67. Yuan & Sester (2025) is the only work applying a language model to historical map content, using the LLM as a *coarse label generator* feeding a conventional segmentation pipeline, evaluated by IoU on area classes. Ung et al. (2025) establishes the field's prior: VLMs are expected to be poor at cartographic symbology.

- **Mound symbols on historical maps** (3 members: rows 1, 2, 37). The direct lineage — two automated studies plus the human-digitisation baseline. A *thin* cluster: the paper is entering a near-empty niche, which is good for novelty and bad for having comparators.
- **Point symbols on historical maps** (4 members: rows 3, 4, 5, 6). Cross-domain but protocol-compatible: discrete small symbols, object-level scoring, degraded historical cartography. The natural source of the "what does good look like for point features" number.
- **Area/line features on historical maps** (8 members: rows 7–14). Dense and convergent, all pixel-level in the 0.76–0.98 band. Cite as a contrast class, not a comparator.
- **Mound/kurgan detection in remote sensing** (14 members: rows 15–25, 28, 29, 30). Largest cluster and the nearest-neighbour archaeology literature by feature class. Highly heterogeneous in protocol; converges on landscape-scale precision as the binding constraint.

## Synthesis: where does 0.815–0.890 sit?

**Against the most similar task — object-level detection of mound symbols on a historical topographic map series — 0.815 to 0.890 sits at or slightly above the top of the published held-out cluster.** The only directly comparable held-out numbers are Berganzo-Besga et al. (2023): F1 0.642–0.724 for hachure mounds and 0.706–0.938 for form-line mounds on their low-density test set, the spread driven by how similar the test maps were to the training maps. The paper's 0.890 exceeds every point on the hachure curve and sits in the upper half of the form-line curve; 0.815 sits above the whole hachure curve and mid-range on form-line. Garcia‐Molsosa et al. (2021) imply higher figures still (P ≈ 0.88–0.97 with R ≈ 0.96–1.00), but against manual re-reading of a small set of similar sheets rather than a held-out generalisation test.

**Against point-feature extraction on degraded historical cartography more broadly, 0.815–0.890 sits clearly above the cluster.** Saxton et al. (2024) report median F1 0.73 for point features with abundant annotated training data; the AI4CMA competition median was 0.35 (Goldman et al. 2025). Vassányi & Gede (2021) report 90% accuracy on point symbols, but on a cleaner map series with an unspecified metric definition.

**Against the remote-sensing mound and kurgan literature, 0.815–0.890 sits well above the realistic-deployment cluster and above most curated-set results too.** Realistic large-area figures are F1 ≈ 0.17 for grave mounds (Trier et al. 2021, derived), 0.34–0.38 for hillforts over 180,000 km² (Landauer et al. 2025), ~0.50 for barrows on a random test set (Verschoof-van der Vaart et al. 2020), 0.59–0.66 for hillforts (Canedo et al. 2025), and ≈ 0.54 for burial mounds on an external area (Niculiță 2020, derived). Best curated results are ≈ 0.77–0.81 (Berganzo-Besga et al. 2021, derived; Trier et al. 2021 on curated tiles, derived) and 0.49–0.79 for barrows (Verschoof-van der Vaart & Lambers 2019). **This comparison is the least legitimate of the three**, because detecting a cartographic symbol drawn by a surveyor is a fundamentally easier perceptual problem than detecting an eroded earthwork in a DEM. It supports a claim about *archaeological utility*, not about *algorithmic difficulty*.

**Against zero-shot vision-language baselines, 0.815–0.890 sits far above.** Landauer & Klassen (2025) report F1 0.41–0.67 across three feature classes — achieved only after abandoning localisation and scoring at tile level. If the paper's numbers are object-level with a spatial matching criterion, they are earned under a stricter rule.

**Against area- and line-feature segmentation on historical maps, 0.815–0.890 sits below the cluster** (0.84–0.98: Kramm et al. 2025 at 0.84, Ståhl & Weimann 2022 at 0.886, Maxwell et al. 2020 at Dice 0.902, O'Hara et al. 2024 at 0.982, Heitzler & Hurni 2020 at IoU 0.882 / AP 0.9855). **These are not comparators**, and the paper should say so explicitly rather than let a reviewer notice an apparent shortfall. The Goldman et al. (2025) points-versus-polygons contrast (0.35 vs 0.77 under identical scoring) makes this argument in one line.

### What the paper can defensibly claim

- That its results are **at the upper end of, or above, published held-out results for object-level detection of discrete archaeological features on historical map series** — citing Berganzo-Besga et al. (2023) as the benchmark and stating the protocol differences.
- That they are **substantially above published point-feature extraction benchmarks on degraded historical cartography** — citing Saxton et al. (2024) and Goldman et al. (2025).
- That they are **above the realistic large-area performance of CNN-based archaeological prospection** — citing Trier et al. (2021), Verschoof-van der Vaart et al. (2020), Landauer et al. (2025), and Canedo et al. (2025) — while conceding that the underlying perceptual task differs.
- That **no published vision-language model result on archaeological or cartographic detection approaches these figures**, the closest being Landauer & Klassen (2025) at F1 0.41–0.67 under a more permissive tile-level protocol.

### What the paper should not claim

- Not that its F1 is comparable to, or should be read against, pixel-level segmentation F1 for wetlands, buildings, roads, or mine extents. Different denominators.
- Not that its numbers beat Garcia‐Molsosa et al. (2021) without noting that theirs were measured against manual map reading on a small, stylistically homogeneous set of sheets.
- Not a bare "state of the art" claim on the basis of cross-protocol comparison. The buffer-matching criterion, the ground-truth provenance (expert versus student digitisation), the corpus class prevalence, and the corrected-F1 adjustment all differ from every comparator, and a reviewer who knows this literature will test each one.
