## TL;DR

The mechanism behind "micro-registration" — shrinking the unit of registration below the project, and authoring each unit outcome-blind at the analysis boundary — **already has a name and a small but real literature**, most sharply in Gould et al. (2026), who call it *adaptive preregistration* and whose *interim preregistrations* are close to a one-to-one match for the just-in-time mini-registration ([10.1111/2041-210x.70311](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210x.70311)); the clinical-trials statistical analysis plan (SAP) tradition (Gamble et al. 2017, [10.1001/jama.2017.18556](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.18556); Hemming et al. 2020, [10.1186/s13063-020-04828-8](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13063-020-04828-8)) is a mature, high-citation precedent for analysis-level registration finalised before outcomes are unblinded. What is **not** anticipated anywhere I could find is the LLM-support element and the explicit waterfall-to-agile framing applied to registration itself — the "agile science" framing exists (Hekler et al. 2016, [10.1007/s13142-016-0395-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13142-016-0395-7)) but has never been pointed at preregistration. **Biggest gap: nobody has written about LLM-assisted preregistration authoring at all** — the entire "LLM + preregistration" literature is about preregistering *studies of* language models, not using them to *write* registrations, so D.6's most defensible novelty claim is the one the paper is probably treating as incidental.

## Landscape

The preregistration methods literature has spent roughly a decade circling the problem the proposal addresses — that a single, front-loaded registration cannot anticipate a long analytical project — and has produced three broad families of response, none of which had converged on a shared name until very recently.

The **repair family** treats front-loading as fixed and improves the handling of what goes wrong afterwards: Lakens (2024) argues deviations are not uniformly bad and can *increase* severity; Willroth & Atherton (2024) supply a reporting taxonomy for deviations; Nosek et al. (2019) frame preregistration as "a plan, not a prison". This is the incumbent solution the proposal displaces, and D.6 should say so explicitly — the argument is that structural incrementalism dominates post-hoc deviation reporting, and that argument only lands if the incumbent is named.

The **substitute family** achieves outcome-blindness without registration timing at all: blind analysis (MacCoun & Perlmutter 2015), analysis blinding tested head-to-head against preregistration (Sarafoglou et al. 2023), split-sample and holdout designs (Fafchamps & Labonne 2017), and the reusable holdout (Dwork et al. 2015), which offers a differential-privacy-based formal guarantee for *repeated* adaptive interrogation of the same data. Dwork et al. is the sharpest intellectual competitor: it solves "many sequential analyses on one dataset without invalidating inference" mechanically rather than procedurally. D.6 should acknowledge it and explain why a procedural commitment is preferred (auditability, no distributional assumptions, applicability to non-statistical design decisions).

The **restructure family** is where the proposal lives, and it is thin. Ioannidis (2022) floats per-step registration in a single sentence without developing it [characterisation unverified — see Verification advisory note 1]. Van den Akker et al. (2021) and Willroth et al. (2022) build analysis-level registration templates, but only because secondary and coordinated data analysis *forces* the unit down — the data already exist, so the analysis is the only registrable unit. Gerasimova (2024) proposes a validity-argument "living document" spanning multiple studies with preregistration components attached. Strobl et al. (2026) split a simulation study's lifecycle into a lean registered "Statement of Intent" plus later piloting documentation. And then Gould et al. (2026) put the whole thing together and name it.

The **clinical-trials tradition** sits outside this conversation and is largely uncited by it, which is a citation-network failure the paper can exploit. Trials have registered analysis plans separately from protocols for years, finalised after the trial is running but before unblinding — exactly the timing structure the proposal describes — with published content guidelines (Gamble et al. 2017, 458 citations on CrossRef; materially higher on other indices) and calls for prospective SAP reporting (Hemming et al. 2020). The psychology-and-open-science preregistration literature and the trials SAP literature barely talk to each other. Bridging them is a legitimate contribution independent of the novelty question.

Finally, the **computational-research corner** (van Miltenburg et al. 2021 for natural language processing; Vaccaro 2026 for AI-agent experiments; Thomas et al. 2026 for the elegant trick of preregistering against a not-yet-released model) is active but young, and it is uniformly about *what to register when studying models*. No one is using models to help register.

1. **LLM-assisted preregistration authoring is an empty cell.** Across four arXiv API queries, three OpenAlex/CrossRef keyword searches, and two WebSearch passes explicitly targeting AI-assisted registration writing, I found no paper proposing or evaluating LLM support for authoring preregistrations.
